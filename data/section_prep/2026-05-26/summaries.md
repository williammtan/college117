## Previous Lecture Summary (May 20 — Environmental Justice)

*(No lecture deck found; summary based on assigned readings and course theme.)*

The May 20 lecture closed out Week 8 on Environmental Justice by examining the ecological footprint of AI in healthcare. The session drew on two readings: a Cornell-led study introducing the SAHAI (Sustainably Advancing Health AI) framework, and a Health Economics Review article on sustainable AI in medicine. Together they argued that the dominant narrative of AI as a clean, cloud-based technology obscures significant material costs — carbon emissions from model training and inference, water consumed cooling data centers, and electronic waste from hardware cycles. The Cornell group modeled a real-world clinical AI tool (AI-assisted physician messaging at a large academic health system) and calculated that one year of operation produces roughly 48,000 kg of CO₂, equivalent to ~2,300 tree-years. Their SAHAI framework urged health systems to embed sustainability assessments before deployment, not after. The Bignami et al. article reinforced that AI's environmental burden falls disproportionately on communities near data centers and mining sites — connecting environmental injustice back to the distributive and participatory justice frameworks from earlier weeks. Both pieces shared the core position: without governance requiring transparency on energy use and emissions, the justice harms of healthcare AI will remain invisible until they are irreversible.

## Reading Summaries for May 27 Session

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57, 2614–2619 (2025).

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ASU News, UCSC Genomics Institute, EurekAlert.)*

This paper introduces the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States: the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D-I3 TDR), supported by a $9 million NIH RADx award. The central argument is that meaningful Indigenous health equity requires not merely including Indigenous populations in mainstream genomic databases but building separate, community-governed repositories where Tribal Nations hold sovereign control over data access, use, and benefit-sharing.

The paper's key move is institutional: rather than asking Indigenous communities to trust existing research infrastructure (NIH's dbGaP, biobank consortia), it proposes a parallel structure in which Tribal data is held and managed under Tribal Nation governance protocols. Researchers who wish to access the data must engage with tribal governance bodies that apply culturally specific criteria for permissible research questions, methods, and outputs. This operationalizes data sovereignty at a structural level — sovereignty is not a checkbox on a consent form but an ongoing institutional relationship.

The evidence marshaled includes the long record of research harms to Indigenous communities (from the Havasupai case to broader patterns of extractive genomics), the failure of standard IRB review to account for collective harms to communities, and the growing body of Indigenous data sovereignty scholarship (CARE Principles, OCAP®). The connection to COLLEGE 117's justice themes is direct: the paper enacts a model of participatory justice where the community defines terms of engagement, collective benefit is a design requirement rather than an afterthought, and the authority to refuse participation or revoke access is preserved.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *NEJM* 363(3), 204–207 (2010).

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: PubMed, SUNY Upstate Bioethics in Brief, Stanford Law Blog, PMC analyses.)*

This NEJM Perspective uses the 2010 settlement of Havasupai Indian Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents as a lens for rethinking consent norms in genetic research. In the early 1990s, Havasupai members donated blood samples to ASU researchers to study the genetic basis of the tribe's high Type II diabetes rates. Those samples were subsequently used — without additional consent — for studies on schizophrenia, population migration, and inbreeding. Tribal members discovered the secondary uses in 2003; litigation followed, and ASU eventually paid $700,000, returned stored samples (which were ceremonially buried), and agreed to provide medical and educational services.

Mello and Wolf's core argument is that standard individual informed consent, as practiced by IRBs, is structurally inadequate for research involving Indigenous communities because: (1) consent forms routinely use broad, vague language ("behavioral and medical disorders") that fails to communicate specific research plans; (2) individual tribal members cannot consent on behalf of the tribe's collective interests, including deeply held cultural beliefs about origins that genetic migration studies can directly threaten; and (3) stored sample repositories outlive their original consent context, enabling uses the participant never anticipated.

Their proposed lessons include requiring specific rather than blanket consent for secondary uses, engaging community governance bodies in protocol approval, and establishing community oversight mechanisms for sample repositories. In the justice frame of the course, the case illustrates how scientific extractivism operates: communities provide biological material and receive neither benefit nor control, while researchers gain publications and prestige. It also shows how seemingly neutral scientific findings (about migration, inbreeding) can carry real political risks for communities whose land claims depend on specific historical narratives.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(External link — PDF from GIDA; WebFetch returned 403. Summarized from the published Data Science Journal article, Wikipedia, ARDC documentation, and CODATA publication.)*

The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — were published in 2019 by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance's International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group as a complement and counterweight to the existing FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR optimizes data for open scientific exchange, CARE centers Indigenous peoples' rights, interests, and self-determination.

**Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems must be designed so Indigenous peoples derive benefit — economic, social, cultural — from data generated about or by them. This includes supporting Indigenous governance institutions, enabling community-driven data use, and ensuring that data does not reproduce systems of oppression.

**Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples have the right to govern collection, ownership, and application of data about their communities, territories, and ways of life. This extends to recognition of Indigenous Nations' authority over data through their own protocols and legal frameworks — not only through external regulatory bodies like IRBs.

**Responsibility (R):** Those working with Indigenous data — researchers, institutions, governments — have an affirmative duty to demonstrate that their use supports Indigenous peoples' self-determination and wellbeing. Passive non-harm is insufficient; active benefit is required.

**Ethics (E):** Data must be collected and used in ways consistent with Indigenous ethical frameworks, including the rights recognized in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP). Ethical review must include Indigenous voices rather than relying solely on Western bioethics standards.

The CARE Principles connect to earlier course frameworks by showing that procedural justice (who governs the process?) and distributive justice (who benefits?) are inseparable: without structural authority, promises of benefit remain extractive philanthropy rather than genuine equity. The principles also operationalize Ruha Benjamin's call for "real utopias" — redesigning institutions, not just reforming individual practices.
