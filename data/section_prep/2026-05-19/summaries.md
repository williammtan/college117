## Reading Summaries

### Previous Session (May 18): Kate Crawford, "Earth" — *Atlas of AI*, Chapter 1 (Yale University Press, 2021)

*(Summarized from full PDF text)*

Kate Crawford opens *The Atlas of AI* by insisting that AI is not immaterial. Her central argument is that AI systems demand a staggering planetary extraction of minerals, fossil fuels, water, and labor—and that the costs of this extraction are systematically concealed from consumers, policymakers, and most researchers. Crawford travels to Silver Peak, Nevada, where lithium brine pumped from an underground lake is left in iridescent evaporation ponds, supplying the rechargeable batteries inside smartphones and AI-powered devices. She traces the supply chain outward: Bolivia's Salar, Congo's Bayan Obo mines (which produce a 5.5-mile toxic black lake of rare earth processing waste), Indonesian tin islands, Malaysian gutta-percha forests stripped to extinction by Victorian telegraph cables—and forward to NSA data centers in Utah consuming 1.7 million gallons of water daily.

Key evidence is striking: China supplies 95% of the world's rare earth minerals, tolerating environmental devastation that other nations externalize; a single NLP model training run generates over 660,000 lbs of CO₂; the tech sector's carbon footprint has matched aviation at its peak and is growing faster; one container ship emits as much pollution as 50 million cars. Crawford frames this through the concept of the "planetary mine"—extraction is not localized but diffuse, structured by what Mumford called the "megamachine": an opaque, globally distributed system that conceals its human and environmental costs as a condition of its profitability.

For this course's justice themes, the chapter is foundational. The greatest benefits of AI extraction are captured by a few corporations and their investors, while environmental costs—toxic lakes, strip-mined communities, water scarcity—are borne by the many, disproportionately by Global South communities and low-income neighborhoods of color. The chapter insists we can only work toward justice by refusing the "strategic amnesia" that accompanies stories of technological progress.

### Previous Session (May 18): Keisha Ray & Jane Fallis Cooper, "The Bioethics of Environmental Injustice: Ethical, Legal, and Clinical Implications of Unhealthy Environments," *The American Journal of Bioethics* 24(3):9–17 (2023)

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: PhilPapers record, McGovern Center for Humanities and Ethics coverage, Tandfonline abstract, and related AJOB commentary)*

Ray and Cooper argue that bioethicists have a professional and ethical obligation to engage environmental injustice as a core concern of health justice, not a peripheral policy issue. Their central claim is that if bioethics takes justice and the protection of vulnerable populations seriously, it cannot ignore the environmental conditions that cause differential health burdens—particularly for Black, Latinx, and Indigenous communities. The paper advances three interlocking arguments grounded in established bioethical principles.

First, they document the factual landscape: facilities emitting hazardous toxins—toxic landfills, oil refineries, chemical plants—are disproportionately sited in predominantly Black, Latinx, and Indigenous neighborhoods, a pattern shaped not by geography but by structural racism and regulatory capture. Second, they develop a clinical argument: the medical consequences of environmental exposure (respiratory disease, cancer, developmental disorders, chronic illness) fall hardest on precisely those populations already identified as vulnerable by mainstream bioethics—children, the elderly, those with chronic conditions, and those living in poverty. Third, they make a legal and policy argument: bioethicists should actively support a legally enforceable right to a healthy environment, aligning bioethics with environmental law.

For this course, Ray and Cooper deepen the Week 3 social determinants framework by showing that environmental conditions are not neutral background factors but products of unjust decisions about where to locate harmful infrastructure. Their work bridges the course's ongoing concern—who bears the costs of technological and industrial development?—with the clinical and legal tools bioethicists can bring to that question. Alongside Crawford, this reading insists that environmental justice is inseparable from health justice.

### Current Session (May 20): "Balancing the Promise of Healthcare AI with Its Carbon Costs" — *Cornell Chronicle* / *NEJM Catalyst* (September 2025)

*(External link, paywalled/403 — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Cornell Chronicle article, Cornell Tech news page, SAHAI framework page, and web search summaries of the NEJM Catalyst paper by Ramachandran, Sarabu, Gupta, Ghose, and Lee)*

This paper, published in *NEJM Catalyst* (September 12, 2025) and covered by Cornell Chronicle, argues that healthcare systems deploying AI must integrate environmental sustainability into decision-making from the start, not treat carbon costs as afterthoughts. The authors—spanning Cornell Tech, Cornell Engineering, Stanford, Berkeley, and Harvard—introduce the SAHAI (Sustainably Advancing Health AI) framework: a decision tool for quantifying the greenhouse gas emissions, water consumption, and financial costs of specific AI implementations in healthcare.

Using AI-enabled patient messaging as a case study, the researchers calculate that one year of operating such a tool produces approximately 48,000 kilograms of CO₂—equivalent to roughly 2,300 "tree-years" of carbon sequestration. The framework models water consumption for server cooling and examines multiple scenarios affecting the emissions profile of a major health system, including energy source mix and data center location. The authors call for a health system coalition to adopt shared reporting standards.

For this course's justice themes, the SAHAI article makes a critical connection that is easy to miss: the environmental costs of AI-enabled healthcare are not borne by the hospitals or patients who benefit. Data centers are frequently located in water-stressed or coal-dependent regions—meaning the carbon and water burden of healthcare AI falls disproportionately on communities that may receive none of that AI's medical benefits. The paper thus connects environmental cost directly to health equity, mirroring earlier course discussions of who bears the risks of biotechnology development versus who captures its benefits. Its call for coalition reporting also echoes the course's participatory justice theme: communities deserve transparency about what AI deployments cost the environments they live in.

### Current Session (May 20): Bignami, Darhour & Bellini, "Sustainable AI in Medicine: Navigating Innovation, Challenges, and Environmental Impact," *Health Economics Review* (2025)

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Springer Nature abstract, web search summaries of Health Economics Review 2025 article, and related PMC literature)*

Bignami, Darhour, and Bellini offer a systematic review of the environmental footprint of AI in medicine, arguing that healthcare's AI adoption cannot be ethically or economically responsible without confronting its significant resource demands. The paper's core argument is that AI's dual role—simultaneously a consumer of environmental resources and a potential tool for optimizing healthcare sustainability—requires a "Green AI" paradigm grounded in lifecycle assessment, energy monitoring, and circular economy principles.

The authors document the costs: data centers are among the largest global consumers of electricity and water; training and deploying medical AI models contributes measurably to greenhouse gas emissions and electronic waste. They review governance frameworks, noting that the EU AI Act mandates transparency and accountability for high-risk AI (including healthcare AI) and encourages energy-efficient practices, while U.S. EPA proposals target reductions in data center energy and water use. On the solutions side, the paper reviews energy-efficient model design, renewable energy adoption, waste heat reuse, and equipment recycling—alongside AI's positive potential to reduce material waste in healthcare and optimize resource allocation.

For this course's justice themes, the paper's global perspective is significant: AI's environmental costs are not distributed evenly. Data centers concentrated in the Global South or in communities with weaker environmental regulations impose external costs on those least able to resist them—a pattern that structurally mirrors the conflict minerals supply chains Crawford describes. The paper's call for transparent reporting and governance aligns directly with the course's participatory justice framework from Week 2: communities should have a voice in whether and how AI systems with environmental consequences are deployed near them. Together with the SAHAI article, this reading pushes students to see environmental sustainability not as a technical optimization problem but as a justice problem.
