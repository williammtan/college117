### Reading 1: Anderson et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" *(Nat Genet, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2025 *Nature Genetics* paper by Matthew Z. Anderson, Krystal S. Tsosie, and over twenty co-authors describes the creation and design of the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository** — the first federally funded data repository for biomedical data from American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) research participants. The repository was funded through a $9 million NIH RADx (Rapid Acceleration of Diagnostics) award made in 2023, spanning six collaborative institutions including Arizona State University, Stanford University, and the Native BioData Consortium.

The paper's central argument is that a sovereignty-aligned repository model can allow researchers to work with AI/AN data while ensuring that **Tribal Nation governance** — not federal or academic institutional priorities — determines how data is collected, stored, shared, and used. This required months of legal review with tribal-nation attorneys to reconcile repository operations with tribal and institutional law, a process that itself models what genuine community engagement in research infrastructure looks like. The original impetus was COVID-19 health disparities data from AI/AN communities, but the repository is designed as a durable infrastructure for health research broadly.

The paper situates the D4I within a long history of biomedical data being extracted from Indigenous communities without reciprocal benefit, inadequate consent processes, or tribal oversight — exemplified by the Havasupai case and the 2002 Navajo Nation moratorium on genetic research. The authors argue that true data sovereignty requires not just community consent at the point of collection, but ongoing community authority over downstream uses, the ability to withdraw data, and benefit-sharing structures that prioritize Indigenous health outcomes. For this course's justice themes — particularly the Week 9 questions about what it means for a community to exercise sovereignty over its own data and what technological challenges arise when shifting data stewardship to communities — this paper is a direct case study in building biotechnology infrastructure that is **designed for justice** rather than retrofitted with it.

### Reading 2: Mello & Wolf — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This *New England Journal of Medicine* Perspective piece by bioethicists Michelle M. Mello (Stanford Law) and Leslie E. Wolf examines the landmark **Havasupai v. Arizona Board of Regents** case as a lens for understanding the ethical obligations researchers bear when storing biological samples for future, unspecified research uses.

The case facts: Beginning in the early 1990s, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from members of the Havasupai Tribe, a small Native American community in the Grand Canyon, ostensibly to study the genetic basis of **Type 2 diabetes**, which afflicts more than half the tribe. Participants signed a broad consent form authorizing use of samples to "study the causes of behavioral/medical disorders." Without further consent, researchers subsequently used the samples for studies on **schizophrenia, population migration, and inbreeding** — topics the community found deeply offensive both culturally and spiritually (many Havasupai hold religious beliefs that the body is sacred and should not be subjected to such uses). English is a second language for many community members, compounding questions about meaningful consent. When tribe members learned of these additional studies in 2003, they demanded the samples' return and filed suit. In 2010, ASU settled for **$700,000 paid to 41 tribal members**, along with the return of all DNA samples.

Mello and Wolf use this case to argue that the existing framework of **broad, one-time consent** is ethically inadequate for biospecimen research, because it cannot anticipate future studies and denies participants ongoing agency over how their biological material is used. They propose strategies including **tiered (layered) consent** that specifies permissible uses, re-consent procedures for substantially different studies, and enhanced community-level protections for group research. The paper connects to the course's core justice question — who is harmed and who benefits from biotechnology — by showing how research processes that appear procedurally compliant can still violate community dignity and trust when they treat participants' bodies as resources to be extracted from rather than as belonging to people with rights.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(GIDA, 2019)*

*(external link; publisher URL https://www.gida-global.org/s/CAREPrinciplesOnePagersFINALOct172019.pdf appears to be accessible, but the domain was unreachable from this session — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The **CARE Principles** were released in September 2019 by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)** following a year-long international consultation process involving Indigenous Peoples, scholars, nonprofits, and governments. They were published formally in *Data Science Journal* (2020) and are designed to complement the widely used **FAIR Principles** (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) for research data. While FAIR focuses on technical interoperability and data reusability for the research community, CARE addresses **governance authority and ethical commitments** — it asks *who has the right to control data* rather than merely how to make data technically shareable.

The four principles are:

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems and data use should be designed and function so that Indigenous Peoples derive benefit from data about themselves, their communities, and their territories. Benefits should align with Indigenous communities' own priorities and values, not only the interests of outside researchers or institutions.

- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognized and their authority to control such data empowered. This includes the right to determine how data is collected, managed, analyzed, and shared.

- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how they use the data, in ways that support Indigenous Peoples' self-determination and collective benefit. This encompasses active stewardship, not passive compliance.

- **Ethics (E):** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle — from collection and storage through analysis and application.

CARE matters for this course's justice framework because it operationalizes **participatory justice** (Week 2) at the data infrastructure level: it asks that communities be treated as governance authorities, not merely consulted subjects. When placed alongside the Havasupai case (what happens when these principles are absent) and the D4I repository (what happens when institutions actively implement them), CARE provides students the normative vocabulary to evaluate when a biotechnology is genuinely community-led versus merely community-adjacent.
