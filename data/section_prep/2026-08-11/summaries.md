## Previous Lecture Summary (2026-05-27 — Data Ownership)

> **Note:** No lecture slide deck was found in the repository for this session. The following is drawn from syllabus context and the guest lecturer's research profile.

The May 27th session featured guest lecturer **Krystal Tsosie, PhD, MPH, MA (Diné/Navajo Nation)**, focusing on data ownership and sovereignty through Indigenous Nations examples. The lecture addressed how historical extractive research practices—where outside scientists collected biological samples and data from Indigenous communities without meaningful community governance—have caused ongoing harms. Tsosie's work centers on rethinking the architecture of biomedical data systems to embed Tribal Nation governance at the design level rather than as an afterthought.

The session situated data ownership within the broader framework of Indigenous sovereignty: tribes are not merely "research subjects" but sovereign entities whose nations hold rights over data generated from their peoples and lands. Key questions included what it means for a community to exercise "data sovereignty," how federated and decentralized data architectures can embed community control rather than centralize access at a university or government repository, and how the Havasupai case (see readings) serves as a cautionary baseline. The session connected to prior course themes of participatory justice, research ethics, and structural inequities in who controls the knowledge generated from biotechnology.

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57 (2025): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2025 *Nature Genetics* paper introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository**—the first federally funded repository of its kind, backed by a $9 million NIH RADx award to a team at Stanford and the Native BioData Consortium (NativeBio), with nearly half of the leadership composed of Indigenous faculty scientists including lead authors Tsosie (Diné/Navajo) and Anderson (Eastern Band of Cherokee descent).

The article's central argument is that existing federal data infrastructure is structurally misaligned with Indigenous data sovereignty: data collected from Tribal nations typically flows to centralized federal repositories that give tribes no governance authority over downstream uses, re-use, or algorithmic applications. The D4I repository challenges this by placing Tribal Nation governance at the center of the access model. Rather than a single gatekeeper controlling all data, the architecture uses federated and decentralized access mechanisms that require community-level consent and tribal oversight before researchers can engage with data.

**Connection to justice themes:** This work operationalizes the course's concept of participatory justice at the infrastructure level—not just inviting communities to "participate" in research as subjects, but building data systems in which communities exercise actual authority. It also advances the Week 9 theme of data sovereignty by demonstrating that justice cannot be achieved through consent forms alone; it requires redesigning the systems that store, govern, and distribute data generated from Indigenous peoples.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective documents the 2010 settlement in which Arizona State University paid $700,000 to 41 Havasupai tribal members whose blood samples—originally collected in the early 1990s for diabetes research—were subsequently used without additional consent for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and population ancestry. Mello (Harvard) and Wolf (Georgia State) use the settlement to examine the core ethical and legal question: what constitutes adequate informed consent for biospecimens collected for one purpose but stored for potential future, unrelated studies?

The case reveals a fundamental tension between the scientific value of biospecimen banks and the individual and community rights of donors. For the Havasupai, the violation was compounded by cultural and spiritual dimensions: ancestry studies contradicted tribal oral history and beliefs about their origins, and mental health research carried stigma. The authors argue that the case illustrates the inadequacy of generic "blanket consent" forms that allow open-ended future research, and they call for more specific tiered-consent models as well as greater community engagement—especially with Indigenous populations whose collective interests and governance structures are not captured by individual consent alone.

**Connection to justice themes:** The Havasupai case is a foundational example of how the research infrastructure itself can perpetuate injustice. It demonstrates that participatory justice requires not only enrolling communities in research but honoring the limits they set, and that standard bioethics frameworks built on individual autonomy miss the communal, sovereign dimension of Indigenous data rights—directly motivating the CARE Principles and the D4I repository.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. (2019). Global Indigenous Data Alliance.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles were formalized in 2019 by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)** through its International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group within the Research Data Alliance, and published formally as a Data Science Journal article in 2020. They were designed to complement the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which govern open-data sharing norms but do not address Indigenous rights, power differentials, or historical context. Where FAIR is data-centric, CARE is **people- and purpose-centric**.

The four principles and their core sub-ideas:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous peoples derive benefit from their own data: through inclusive development, equitable outcomes, and improved community governance—not merely as subjects of others' benefit.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples hold rights and interests in data derived from their communities, lands, and knowledge. They must have authority over governance of that data, including decisions about collection, access, and downstream use.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data bear responsibility to nurture respectful relationships with the communities and to actively support Indigenous data sovereignty and collective benefit.
- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary concern at every stage of the data lifecycle. This includes minimizing harm, prioritizing dignity, and recognizing the legitimacy of Indigenous ethical frameworks alongside (or above) standard research ethics review processes.

**Connection to justice themes:** The CARE Principles provide the ethical vocabulary for evaluating whether a data practice respects Indigenous justice. They operationalize the course's justice frameworks at the level of data governance—connecting Week 4's research-ethics concerns, Week 7's surveillance and data-rights analysis, and Week 9's community-sovereignty discussions into a coherent standard that Tribal nations can assert when negotiating with researchers and institutions.
