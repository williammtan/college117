## Reading Summaries — Previous Session (2026-05-27)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57, 2614–2619 (2025).

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2025 Nature Genetics article, co-authored by genomics researcher and bioethicist Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) and a large multidisciplinary team of Indigenous and non-Indigenous scholars, presents the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States: the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (DI3) Tribal Data Repository. The repository is a landmark intervention in the field of Indigenous genomic research, designed to invert the traditional extractivist model in which outside researchers collect genetic and health data from tribal communities and retain control over its use.

The repository builds governance infrastructure that places Tribal Nations as the sovereign authority over data collection, access, and reuse. Researchers who wish to engage with the data must operate under rigorous Tribal Nation governance frameworks that prioritize community data-sharing interests over academic publication or biomedical discovery alone. This directly embodies the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics), translating an ethical framework into operational infrastructure.

The article's key argument is that structural data sovereignty — not just informed consent — is necessary to protect Indigenous communities from ongoing exploitation. Historical violations, including unauthorized repurposing of Havasupai blood samples and large-scale genomic surveillance of minorities, demonstrate that procedural protections (consent forms) are insufficient without governance authority. The DI3 repository represents a justice-centered model of research partnership, connecting directly to the course's theme of community-led justice and the question of who benefits from biotechnology.

### 2. Mello, M.M. & Wolf, L.E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3), 204–207 (2010).

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective piece by bioethicist and legal scholar Michelle Mello and Leslie Wolf examines a landmark case in research ethics: the Havasupai Tribe of the Grand Canyon's legal dispute with Arizona State University (ASU). In the early 1990s, Havasupai members donated blood samples to ASU researchers — they understood the samples would be used exclusively for diabetes research, a disease disproportionately affecting the tribe. Without re-consent, ASU researchers subsequently used the stored samples for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding — topics the tribe found culturally offensive and spiritually violating. When tribe members discovered this in 2003, they sued. In April 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 distributed to 41 tribe members.

Mello and Wolf use the case to examine two fundamental questions: What constitutes legally adequate informed consent for stored biospecimens? And do donors retain any property interests in donated samples? They argue that case law generally holds that donors lose property rights once samples are lawfully collected under valid consent — but that "valid consent" is itself unsettled. Broad consent language ("health research") that fails to specify the range of possible future uses is ethically inadequate, particularly for Indigenous communities whose relationships to genetic material, ancestry, and identity are culturally distinct.

The key lesson for justice in biotechnology: procedural consent cannot substitute for genuine respect for a community's values, and research institutions must invest in understanding the cultural and moral stakes of genomic research for the populations they study. This directly connects to the course's justice frameworks — particularly participatory justice and the question of who bears the burdens while others gain the benefits.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Released by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance in September 2019, the CARE Principles articulate a people- and purpose-oriented framework for Indigenous data governance intended to complement (and challenge) the data-centric FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR focuses on the technical properties of data, CARE foregrounds the social, political, and relational dimensions of data sovereignty.

**C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems and research outcomes must be designed so that Indigenous Peoples can derive tangible benefits — not just knowledge produced for academic or commercial interests. Data should enable Indigenous self-determination and community well-being.

**A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples hold inherent rights over their data. Research must recognize and actively empower their authority to govern data collection, access, storage, and reuse — authority that flows from sovereignty, not from consent forms.

**R — Responsibility:** Researchers, institutions, and governments working with Indigenous data bear ongoing responsibility for how that data is used. This includes transparent reporting on data use, sharing results back to communities, and supporting Indigenous data infrastructure.

**E — Ethics:** Indigenous rights and well-being must be the primary concern across the entire data life cycle. This extends to historical context and power asymmetries that shape contemporary research relationships.

The CARE Principles connect powerfully to the course's justice frameworks: they operationalize the idea that justice is not only about who bears the costs of biotechnology, but about who governs the knowledge systems that biotechnology produces. Together with the Havasupai case and the DI3 repository article, CARE offers a normative and institutional roadmap for community-led justice in data and genomics.
