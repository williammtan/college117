## Previous Lecture Summary

**Session: Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples) — May 27, 2026**  
*(No lecture deck PDF was committed to the repository; summary derived from syllabus session description and course context.)*

This lecture, delivered by guest Krystal Tsosie — a Diné/Navajo genomics scholar and co-founder of the Native BioData Consortium — examined what it means for Indigenous communities to exercise genuine stewardship and sovereignty over their own health data. Tsosie's work sits at the intersection of genomics, Indigenous rights, and bioethics, and she brought both personal and scholarly authority to the session.

The lecture explored how Indigenous Nations have historically been subjected to extractive research practices in which biological samples and health data were collected by outside researchers and then used — sometimes for decades — without meaningful community consent, governance oversight, or shared benefit. Case studies such as the Havasupai tribe's experience with ASU demonstrated how this dynamic produces concrete harms: betrayal of trust, violation of cultural and spiritual beliefs, and reinforcement of colonial knowledge hierarchies.

Tsosie argued that genuine data sovereignty requires more than passive consent; it demands that Indigenous Nations have authority over who holds their data, how it is governed, what studies it may be used for, and who benefits. She introduced frameworks such as the CARE Principles and tribal data repositories as practical governance tools that instantiate sovereignty rather than merely acknowledging it. The lecture also posed the course's central justice question: can existing research infrastructure be reformed, or must Indigenous communities build entirely parallel systems to secure their rights?

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository, described as the first federally funded data repository built explicitly under Tribal Nation governance. The central argument is that existing genomic research infrastructure is structurally incompatible with Indigenous data sovereignty because it situates control with researchers and institutions rather than with communities. The D4I repository is designed as a "safe harbor" — a governed space where Indigenous communities set the terms for data access, use, and benefit rather than ceding those decisions to outside researchers or IRBs.

The authors extend the ethical principles of community-based participatory research (CBPR) into the genomic domain, meaning that tribal laws, policies, and community knowledge-keepers have legal and procedural authority over what research can be conducted and how findings are disseminated. This is not merely a data-management innovation but a governance claim: that Indigenous Nations possess inherent sovereign rights over biological and health data generated from their members.

The paper connects directly to the course's justice themes by demonstrating what procedural and distributive justice looks like in practice. Rather than asking how researchers can do better by Indigenous communities, it asks how communities can govern their own data on their own terms. The D4I repository operationalizes the CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — in a federally recognized institutional form, offering a replicable model for other tribal nations seeking to advance health equity without surrendering sovereignty.

**Key justice connection:** Data governance as an expression of Indigenous sovereignty; community-controlled infrastructure as a prerequisite for equitable research.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207 (2010).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This perspective piece uses a landmark legal case as a vehicle for interrogating the ethics of biospecimen banking. In the 1990s, researchers at Arizona State University collected blood samples from members of the Havasupai Indian tribe, ostensibly to study the high rates of type 2 diabetes in the community. Without additional consent, the samples were subsequently used to investigate schizophrenia, inbreeding patterns, and population migration histories — research that conflicted with the tribe's cultural beliefs and raised profound concerns about genetic determinism and stigmatization. In 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 in direct compensation to 41 tribal members, funds for a tribal clinic and school, and the return of the physical samples.

Mello and Wolf use the case to surface the central bioethical question: what constitutes adequate informed consent when biospecimens collected for one purpose are stored and later used for entirely different studies? They argue that the standard model of individual informed consent is insufficient when research involves communities with collective identities, cultural stakes in genetic data, and histories of exploitation. The case illuminates a gap in IRB oversight — standard protections were designed with individual autonomy in mind and are poorly equipped to handle community-level harms.

The authors call for enhanced consent processes that explicitly enumerate potential secondary uses, greater IRB attention to community-level interests, and recognition that biospecimen banking requires ongoing consent relationships rather than one-time authorization. The paper resonates throughout the course as a concrete illustration of how procedural failures compound historical injustice, and why trust between research institutions and Indigenous communities is so fragile and so consequential.

**Key justice connection:** Informed consent inadequacy; community vs. individual harm; legacy of extractive research on Indigenous populations.

### 3. Carroll, Stephanie Russo, et al. "CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance." (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019; published *Data Science Journal* 2020.)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles were developed in 2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group, a working group of the Research Data Alliance, in response to the limitations of the widely adopted FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR governs the technical openness of data, it is agnostic to who controls data and who benefits from its use — a gap with severe consequences for Indigenous communities whose data has historically been extracted and weaponized against them. CARE addresses this gap by centering people and purpose rather than processes and pipelines.

The four principles are:

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems shall be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous peoples to derive benefit from the data. This includes inclusive development, improved governance, equitable outcomes, and reinvestment in Indigenous communities.
- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognized and their authority to control such data be empowered. This means governance structures, laws, and protocols set by Indigenous Nations — not external IRBs — govern data access and use.
- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how those data are used to support Indigenous peoples' self-determination and collective benefit. Researchers are accountable to the communities whose data they use.
- **Ethics (E):** Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle and across the data ecosystem. Minimum ethical standards should not be treated as ceilings; researchers are obligated to actively promote Indigenous flourishing.

CARE is explicitly people- and purpose-oriented, making it a natural complement to FAIR's technical orientation. In the course's justice framework, CARE represents a shift from procedural compliance (did we get consent?) to substantive justice (do Indigenous communities retain power over their data?). It also operationalizes the concept of data sovereignty introduced in the Anderson et al. paper and contextualizes why cases like Havasupai keep happening in the absence of binding governance structures.

**Key justice connection:** Indigenous data sovereignty as a justice framework; complementing technical data standards with ethical and political accountability.
