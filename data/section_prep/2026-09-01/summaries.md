### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This paper announces the creation of the first federally funded Tribal data repository — the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — developed by Krystal Tsosie and collaborators at Arizona State University in partnership with multiple Tribal Nations. The repository represents a structural response to decades of extractive genomic research: rather than Indigenous peoples' data flowing to outside institutions for outside benefit, the D4I creates a "safe harbor" governed under Tribal Nation authority. Researchers who wish to access Indigenous health data must engage within a framework that centers Tribal governance, community data-sharing priorities, and explicit benefit-sharing agreements.

The paper's key argument is that data sovereignty for Indigenous communities cannot be achieved through policy statements alone — it requires dedicated, community-controlled technical infrastructure. By embedding Tribal governance into the data access architecture itself, the D4I operationalizes principles such as the CARE framework (see Reading 3) at an institutional level. The authors situate the repository within the broader context of Indigenous data sovereignty movements, connecting health data governance to self-determination. For this course's justice themes, the paper illustrates how equitable biotechnology development demands that marginalized communities have active control — not merely inclusion — in the systems that use their data. The paper also signals that federal funding can support rather than undermine Tribal sovereignty when governance is structured appropriately, offering a replicable model for other Indigenous nations globally.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207 (2010)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective examines the landmark settlement in which Arizona State University paid $700,000 to 41 members of the Havasupai tribe after researchers used blood samples well beyond the scope of the tribe's original consent. Beginning in the early 1990s, Havasupai members donated blood samples to study the causes of their unusually high rates of Type II diabetes, signing broadly worded consent forms allowing "study of the causes of behavioral/medical disorders." Without their knowledge, ASU researchers subsequently used those same samples to publish 23 papers on unrelated and culturally sensitive topics: schizophrenia, alcoholism, inbreeding, and population migration origins — the last directly contradicting the Havasupai's own oral traditions about their origins in the Grand Canyon.

Mello and Wolf use the case to interrogate the ethics of broad consent for biospecimen banking. Their central argument is that broad consent, while administratively efficient for researchers, fails to adequately protect research participants — especially when the study population holds distinct cultural values that researchers did not take time to understand. The Havasupai case demonstrates how the gap between what participants understood they were consenting to and what researchers did with their samples can cause profound harm to community identity and trust. The authors argue that genuine informed consent requires not only clear disclosure of possible future uses but ongoing engagement and, where research purposes change substantially, a return to the community for renewed consent. For the course's justice themes, this case is a foundational example of how biomedical research infrastructures can perpetuate injustice against already-marginalized communities when cultural context and community sovereignty are ignored.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles — **C**ollective Benefit, **A**uthority to Control, **R**esponsibility, and **E**thics — were developed at a 2018 workshop on Indigenous data sovereignty in Gaborone, Botswana, and formalized by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA). They are designed to complement the technical FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) by adding the human and political dimensions that FAIR lacks: FAIR tells us *how* to manage data; CARE tells us *for whom* and *under whose authority*.

**Collective Benefit** asserts that Indigenous data use must produce tangible advantages for Indigenous communities — not merely for external researchers or institutions. **Authority to Control** holds that Indigenous peoples have the right to govern how data about their cultures, lands, resources, and peoples is represented, accessed, and used. **Responsibility** requires those working with Indigenous data to demonstrate that their practices benefit rather than harm the communities involved, with transparency about how data are deployed. **Ethics** demands that Indigenous rights and wellbeing be centered across the data lifecycle, from collection through archiving.

The CARE Principles are explicitly people- and purpose-oriented, repositioning Indigenous peoples from passive data subjects to active data governors. For this course's justice framework, CARE is crucial because it shifts the ethical question from "how do we do open science well?" to "who has authority over knowledge derived from Indigenous communities?" The principles connect directly to the Havasupai case (where all four were violated) and to the D4I repository (which operationalizes them institutionally), making all three readings a coherent unit on what community-led justice looks like in practice for genomic and health data.
