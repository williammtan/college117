## Reading Summaries

### Reading 1 — Anderson MZ et al., "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty," *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources including UCSC Genomics Institute news release, ASU News, and andersonlab.genetics.wisc.edu)*

This article presents the rationale, design, and early implementation of the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository**, the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. The project was awarded $9 million by NIH through the RADx (Rapid Acceleration of Diagnostics) initiative and is led by Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation, co-founder of the Native BioData Consortium) and Matthew Z. Anderson (University of Wisconsin–Madison), with broad participation from tribal communities, medical directors, and Indigenous knowledge-keepers.

The D4I repository takes a fundamentally different approach from conventional research biobanks. Rather than treating Indigenous genomic and health data as a resource to be mined by external researchers, it builds a **safe harbor** where Tribes retain sovereign control: participating Nations set the terms under which their data are accessed, can limit use to tribe-specific purposes or opt into collective data-sharing when it serves them, and may revoke access. The Native BioData Consortium — housed on the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation in Eagle Butte, South Dakota — physically locates the infrastructure within a sovereign Indian nation, extending tribal law directly over the data.

The authors argue that this model extends the ethical precepts of **community-based participatory research (CBPR) to genomics**, addressing a long history of extractive science in which Indigenous samples were collected under narrow consent but used for unrelated studies (a pattern exemplified by the Havasupai case, also assigned this week). The repository operationalizes the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics), treating data governance as an expression of political sovereignty rather than a courtesy granted by outside institutions.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This reading is a direct counterpoint to the extractive biomedical research paradigm the course has critiqued throughout. It instantiates participatory justice (Week 2) and community-led justice (Week 9) simultaneously: communities do not merely participate in research designed by outsiders — they govern the data infrastructure itself, deciding who benefits and on whose terms, answering the course's central question ("Who benefits and who is left behind?") by structurally ensuring the answer is "the communities themselves."

### Reading 2 — Mello MM & Wolf LE, "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples," *NEJM* 363(3): 204–207 (2010)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources including AMA Journal of Ethics, embryo.asu.edu, and FindLaw)*

This *New England Journal of Medicine* Perspective piece examines the landmark **Havasupai Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents** case and draws practical and ethical lessons for biomedical researchers working with stored biological specimens from Indigenous communities.

Between 1990 and 1994, ASU researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 members of the Havasupai Tribe of the Grand Canyon, ostensibly for a study on the high rates of **type 2 diabetes** afflicting the community. Tribal members consented to that narrow purpose. In 2003, Carletta Tilousi, a tribal council member, discovered that the samples had been repurposed — without additional consent — for unrelated studies on schizophrenia, inbreeding patterns, and population migration (including research challenging the tribe's oral history of having originated in the Grand Canyon). The tribe sued in 2004, and in April 2010 ASU settled for **$700,000** (paid to 41 individual plaintiffs), returned all remaining samples to the tribe, provided scholarships for tribal members, and agreed to help the impoverished community obtain federal funding for a health clinic.

Mello and Wolf use the case to illuminate the contested terrain of **informed consent for biospecimen storage and future use**. They argue that consent forms permitting broad future use are scientifically useful but ethically problematic when study populations — particularly Indigenous communities whose cultural frameworks treat genetic material as inalienable from identity and community belonging — have not meaningfully understood or endorsed those future uses. The article discusses three models for biospecimen consent: (1) **broad consent** (permitting any future research); (2) **tiered consent** (permitting specified categories of future research); and (3) **dynamic consent** (allowing ongoing revision as new studies are proposed). No model is perfect; the key contribution is insisting that researchers must understand and respect community-specific values about what genetic material represents.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The Havasupai case is a paradigmatic example of biomedical research justice failure — specifically the failure to treat study participants as rightful arbiters of their own biological material. It connects to genetic essentialism (Week 3, Dar-Nimrod & Heine) — the tribe's concern was partly that DNA evidence would be weaponized to undermine their political and cultural self-understanding. It also illustrates the limits of standard IRB frameworks when applied to communities whose relationship to biological samples differs fundamentally from the liberal-individualist assumptions those frameworks embed.

### Reading 3 — Carroll SR et al., "CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance," *Data Science Journal* 19(1): 43 (2020) [distributed via GIDA one-pager, Oct 2019]

*(external link, blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources including GIDA website, Research Data Alliance, ARDC, and CASRAI)*

The **CARE Principles** were developed through the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group within the Research Data Alliance (RDA-IIIDSIG), drawing on prior frameworks from the Te Mana Raraunga Māori Data Sovereignty Network (New Zealand), the US Indigenous Data Sovereignty Network, and the Maiam nayri Wingara Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Data Sovereignty Collective. They were formally published by Stephanie Russo Carroll, Maui Hudson, and co-authors in 2020, and their one-pager dates to a 2018 workshop at International Data Week in Gaborone, Botswana.

The four principles are **people- and purpose-oriented**, intended as a complement to the data-and-function-oriented **FAIR Principles** (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Together, "Be FAIR and CARE" provides a dual framework for responsible data stewardship.

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous peoples derive meaningful benefit from data — including through inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and citizen engagement, and equitable outcomes. Data flows must benefit communities, not just external researchers.

- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in data must be recognized, and their authority to control such data must be actively empowered. Sub-components include recognizing existing rights and interests; Indigenous communities using data for their own governance purposes (data for governance); and Indigenous communities setting the rules under which their data are used (governance of data). Indigenous governing bodies determine how their peoples, lands, resources, and knowledges are represented in data systems.

- **R — Responsibility:** Those working with Indigenous data are responsible for demonstrating how their use of data supports Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit. This includes providing access to resources for generating Indigenous data, building an Indigenous data workforce, and ensuring that data creation, interpretation, and use are conducted respectfully and in ways approved by Indigenous communities.

- **E — Ethics:** Ethical data practices must prioritize Indigenous peoples' wellbeing and rights across all stages of the data life cycle. Indigenous ethical frameworks — not only Western bioethics — should inform data use. The goal is to minimize current and future harm, maximize benefits, promote justice, and allow for future Indigenous-determined uses.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The CARE Principles function as a meta-framework that synthesizes justice themes from across the course. Collective Benefit resonates with Week 2 (participatory justice) and Week 5 (access justice in precision medicine). Authority to Control is a direct operationalization of data sovereignty as an extension of political sovereignty — the governance solution the D4I repository builds upon. Responsibility connects to the research ethics failures examined throughout the course (Havasupai, Uyghur genomic surveillance, Roma data misuse). Ethics grounds Indigenous data governance in value pluralism rather than Western bioethical universalism, challenging the course's recurring thread about whose ethical frameworks count globally.
