## Reading Summaries — Week 9: Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples)

### Reading 1: Anderson et al., "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" (Nature Genetics, 2025)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [ASU News / NIH press release](https://news.asu.edu/20231206-nih-awards-9-million-indigenousled-tribal-data-repository-improve-community-health-response), [EurekAlert NIH announcement](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1010226), [UCSC Genomics Institute](https://genomics.ucsc.edu/news/2023/12/indigenous-scientists-awarded-9m-to-establish-the-first-radx-tribal-data-repository-that-upholds-indigenous-data-sovereignty/), [Science News profile of Tsosie](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/genetics-krystal-tsosie-indigenous-data-sovereignty))*

This article, published in *Nature Genetics* (volume 57, pp. 2614–2619, November 2025) by a large Indigenous and allied research team led by Matthew Z. Anderson and Krystal S. Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), describes the design and rationale of the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded, Indigenous-led biomedical data repository in the United States.

The paper's central argument is that existing research data infrastructure fails Indigenous communities because it was designed without their governance or meaningful input, reproducing colonial patterns of data extraction. The D4I repository is specifically built to invert this: it places Tribal Nation governance at the center of data access, use, and stewardship. Rather than a single administrative gatekeeping key, the system uses **federated and decentralized access models**, meaning different tribal nations can each control access to their own data under their own cultural and legal protocols. The repository is designed to be a "protected home" for federally funded biomedical data originating with U.S. tribal nations and Indigenous peoples — a space where researchers must engage with Indigenous data under rigorous tribal governance rather than extracting data for off-site analysis.

Funded by a $9 million NIH RADx grant, the project involves a six-institution collaborative: Stanford University (prime awardee), Arizona State University, the Native BioData Consortium, Ohio State University, UC Santa Cruz, University of Washington, and University of Wisconsin–Madison. Tsosie leads a $1.2 million sub-award focused on the ethical frameworks for data sharing and analysis. The system is designed to incorporate modern tools including machine learning and AI, with the justice rationale that Indigenous communities should benefit from — not merely be subject to — advanced biotechnologies.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This reading directly enacts the course's core question of who benefits from biotechnological advances. Rather than arguing for abstract principles, the team has built working infrastructure. It operationalizes concepts from both the CARE Principles (Authority to Control, Collective Benefit) and the broader participatory justice framework: communities are not passive subjects but active co-designers and governors of their data. The article also illustrates the tension between "open science" (making data widely accessible) and data sovereignty (restricting access to protect community interests).

### Reading 2: Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E., "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" (NEJM, 2010)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [SSRN preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1626200), [Wikipedia case entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havasupai_Tribe_v._the_Arizona_Board_of_Regents), [AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/genetic-research-among-havasupai-cautionary-tale/2011-02), [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5310710/))*

This *NEJM* Perspective piece by Harvard bioethicist Michelle Mello and Leslie Wolf examines a landmark case in Indigenous research ethics and draws lessons for biospecimen governance more broadly.

**What happened:** Between 1990 and 1994, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 members of the Havasupai tribe, a small Indigenous nation living in the Grand Canyon. The samples were collected for a diabetes study, under a consent form that broadly stated the research would "study the causes of behavioral/medical disorders." Most signers had not completed high school, and English was a second language for many. Critically, tribal members understood they were consenting to diabetes research only. In 2003, a tribal member discovered that the samples had been used, without additional consent, for unrelated studies including research on schizophrenia, population migration, and inbreeding — topics the Havasupai found deeply offensive and contrary to their worldview and religious beliefs about their origins. The tribe demanded the return of the samples and filed suit against the Arizona Board of Regents in 2004. In April 2010 — the same month this NEJM Perspective was published — ASU settled for $700,000 and returned the samples to the tribe.

**Core ethical argument:** Mello and Wolf use the case to illuminate the "bedeviling question" of what constitutes adequate informed consent for biospecimens that may be stored and reused in future, unrelated studies. The conventional framework of individual, written consent was inadequate here for two intersecting reasons: (1) the consent language was ambiguous enough to permit broad reuse that no community member actually envisioned; and (2) it failed to account for group-level harms — the research findings stigmatized the community and violated culturally specific beliefs in ways that no individual consent negotiation could address. The authors call for reconsideration of how biospecimen consent is structured, including community-level consultation and consent for uses that touch on sensitive group characteristics.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The Havasupai case is a touchstone example of how standard bioethical frameworks (designed around individual autonomy) fail Indigenous communities that hold collective identities and interests. It anticipates the CARE Principles' emphasis on collective benefit and authority to control: the tribe was not a collection of individual consenting subjects but a governed community with its own authority over representations of their origins and identities. The case also illustrates the interaction between research justice and structural inequities — lower literacy, language barriers, and the power asymmetry between a university and a small tribal nation all enabled the violation.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [GIDA overview](https://www.gida-global.org/care), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CARE_Principles_for_Indigenous_Data_Governance), [Data Science Journal original paper](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043), [Ada Lovelace Institute blog](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/care-principles-operationalising-indigenous-data-governance/))*

The CARE Principles — **C**ollective Benefit, **A**uthority to Control, **R**esponsibility, **E**thics — are a governance framework for Indigenous data developed in 2018–2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (II-DSIG), a working group within the Research Data Alliance. They were formally published in the *Data Science Journal* in 2020 (Carroll et al., 2020). The principles were developed at International Data Week in Gaborone, Botswana in 2018, through consultation among Indigenous peoples, scholars, nonprofits, and governments.

**The four principles and their key sub-principles:**

**C — Collective Benefit:** Data use must produce tangible benefits for the Indigenous collective, not only for external researchers or funders. This includes: (C1) inclusive development and innovation — data ecosystems should be designed to support Indigenous nations' own use and reuse; (C2) improved governance and citizen engagement — data should inform Indigenous peoples' own policy decisions and self-governance; and (C3) equitable outcomes — benefits must flow equitably to the communities whose data is used.

**A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples must have the right to govern and control data about themselves, their lands, resources, knowledges, and identities. This includes: (A1) rights and interests — recognizing Indigenous data rights even when data is held by external entities; (A2) data for governance — communities should have access to data for their own governance needs; and (A3) governance of data — Indigenous peoples must be active leaders in stewardship and access decisions.

**R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data — researchers, institutions, governments — bear active responsibility to share how data is used, ensure data use supports self-determination, and foster positive relationships with Indigenous communities. Responsibility is ongoing, not a one-time informed consent transaction.

**E — Ethics:** Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary concern at every stage of the data life cycle (collection, storage, analysis, sharing, publication). This requires culturally respectful practices and recognition that Western research ethics frameworks have historically failed to protect Indigenous communities.

**Relationship to FAIR principles:** CARE was explicitly developed as a complement to the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which are widely adopted in open-science policy. FAIR focuses on technical interoperability and broad data sharing — goals that can directly conflict with Indigenous data sovereignty. CARE provides the people-and-purpose counterweight: it insists that data is never merely a technical object but is embedded in social and political relationships, and that "accessible" and "reusable" must be assessed in light of who controls access and who benefits from reuse.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The CARE Principles represent one of the clearest examples in the course of a justice framework designed and led by affected communities rather than imposed by external institutions. They directly address the colonial logic embedded in standard research data practices and provide actionable criteria for evaluating whether a data system is just. Together with the Anderson et al. repository and the Havasupai case, they frame a coherent argument: that data sovereignty is not separable from health sovereignty, and that justice in biotechnology requires structural change in who governs research infrastructure — not merely better individual consent forms.
