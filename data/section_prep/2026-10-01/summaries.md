## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson et al. (2025). A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty. *Nature Genetics*, 57(11), 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article presents the first federally funded tribal data repository — the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — designed to center Indigenous governance over health and genomic data. Led by Matthew Z. Anderson, Krystal S. Tsosie, and a broad coalition of tribal nation partners, the paper describes a federated, decentralized data infrastructure that replaces single-institution gatekeeping with rigorous Tribal Nation governance structures.

The key argument is that existing "open science" and biomedical data-sharing paradigms systematically disadvantage Indigenous communities by treating their data as a resource to be extracted and centralized by external institutions. The D4I repository reframes data sharing around sovereignty: communities decide who can access their data, for what purposes, and under what conditions — operationalizing the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) in concrete technical and legal architecture.

The evidence includes the repository's governance design, its use of modern machine learning and AI tooling adapted to tribal data needs, and its grounding in federal recognition of tribal data rights. The repository enables Indigenous communities to build their own datasets, control their own data narratives, and compete for research funding on their own terms rather than being perpetual donors to others' research agendas.

In connection to the course's justice themes, this work exemplifies a structural justice approach: rather than asking whether a study was ethical after the fact, it redesigns the infrastructure of research participation so that power is redistributed at the institutional level. It moves from "do no harm" ethics to active capacity-building for Indigenous self-determination — a model for how data systems can be designed with justice as a first principle rather than an afterthought.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples. *New England Journal of Medicine*, 363(3), 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This *NEJM* perspective analyzes the landmark legal settlement between the Havasupai Indian Tribe and Arizona State University — one of the most consequential cases in the history of research ethics and Indigenous rights. The case began in the early 1990s when Havasupai members donated blood samples for what they understood to be diabetes research. They later discovered that their samples had been used without their knowledge or consent for studies on schizophrenia genetics, population migration, and inbreeding — research that ran contrary to tribal cultural beliefs and that was, according to the case record, deliberately concealed by the research team during the consent process.

Mello and Wolf's central argument is that the case exposes fundamental inadequacies in how informed consent is conceptualized for biospecimen research. Consent forms permitting use for "behavioral/medical disorders" were deliberately vague, and participants were never informed of the full range of intended research. Critically, the authors show that existing IRB frameworks were designed to protect individual autonomy — but the Havasupai case demonstrated that research on genetic material can cause profound *group-level* and *dignitary* harms that individual consent cannot address. The case also revealed that at least one researcher had instructed team members to actively withhold information from tribe members during consent.

The settlement — $700,000 to 41 tribal members, ceremonial return and burial of the samples, and university-provided medical and educational services — set a precedent for community-level remediation.

For the course's justice themes, this reading is a direct indictment of procedural justice's limits: the formal apparatus of consent was weaponized to obscure harm. It raises the question of whether just research requires not just individual consent, but community-level negotiation, tribal oversight, and enforceable rights over downstream data use — precisely the gap that the CARE Principles and the D4I repository attempt to fill.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles offer a policy and ethical framework specifically designed to center Indigenous peoples as the rightful governors of their own data. Published in 2019 by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) — led by Stephanie Russo Carroll, Maui Hudson, Tahu Kukutai, and colleagues — CARE was developed in response to the limitations of the dominant FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which prioritize technical openness without addressing who holds power over data or who benefits from its use.

The four principles are: **Collective Benefit** — data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous peoples derive direct benefit, including for governance, innovation, and equitable outcomes, not just as contributors to others' research; **Authority to Control** — Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized and empowered, including the right to govern data, not merely access it; **Responsibility** — those working with Indigenous data have an active obligation to share how data are used, nurture respectful relationships with communities, expand Indigenous capacity to steward their own data, and support the languages and worldviews encoded in that data; and **Ethics** — data processes must minimize harm, uphold Indigenous rights, and align with Indigenous ethical frameworks rather than imposing external standards.

The principles reposition Indigenous peoples from *subjects* of data that perpetuate unequal power distributions to *self-determining users* and *governors* of data for development and wellbeing. Developed through Indigenous-led workshops at International Data Week (Gaborone, 2018), they are explicitly people- and purpose-oriented.

In the course's justice framework, CARE represents a shift from *procedural* justice (following rules for individual consent) to *substantive* and *restorative* justice — actively repairing historical harms and redistributing structural power. It frames data governance as inseparable from political sovereignty: who controls data controls whose lives are studied, whose suffering is legible, and whose futures are shaped.
