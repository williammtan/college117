### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty"
*Nature Genetics, Nov 2025, 57(11): 2614–2619*  
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1010226), [UC Santa Cruz News](https://news.ucsc.edu/2023/12/radx-grant/), [D4I website](https://d4itdr.org/about/), and Web Search coverage)*

This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D4I TDR)—the first federally funded tribal data repository in the United States. Funded through a $9 million NIH RADx grant to Stanford University in collaboration with the Native BioData Consortium (NativeBio), the project was designed in direct response to the COVID-19 pandemic's disproportionate impact on Indigenous communities and to longstanding structural exclusion of Indigenous nations from the research infrastructure that studies them.

The article's core argument is that data sovereignty is not simply a policy preference but a precondition for equitable health research. By embedding rigorous Tribal Nation governance at the repository's center—rather than treating tribal input as advisory—D4I enables communities to set the terms of data collection, access, and use. Researchers engage with data only within governance frameworks established by the nations themselves, reversing the extractive model that characterized prior decades of Indigenous genomic research.

Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation, co-first author) led a $1.2 million sub-award designing the ethical approaches to data sharing and analysis, drawing on the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. The repository is accompanied by community-accessible explainer videos that translate technical governance concepts for tribal members, addressing the asymmetric information that has historically made meaningful consent difficult.

The connection to the course's justice themes is direct: D4I exemplifies participatory justice (communities shape research design), distributive justice (benefits flow to the community whose data are used), and corrective justice (responding to decades of violation). It also demonstrates how technological infrastructure is never neutral—the repository's architecture encodes authority structures that either reinforce or disrupt colonial power relations in science.

### 2. Mello, M.M. & Wolf, L.E. — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples"
*New England Journal of Medicine, 2010, 363(3): 204–207*  
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havasupai_Tribe_v._the_Arizona_Board_of_Regents), [AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/genetic-research-among-havasupai-cautionary-tale/2011-02), and Web Search coverage)*

This NEJM Perspective analyzes the legal settlement and ethical implications of the Havasupai v. Arizona Board of Regents case—one of the most consequential informed consent controversies in the history of genomic research. Beginning in 1989, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 Havasupai tribal members for a diabetes genetic study. When no diabetes gene link was found, researchers used the stored samples for additional studies on schizophrenia, population migration patterns, and inbreeding—all without the tribe's knowledge or consent, and all on topics the Havasupai considered culturally taboo and spiritually harmful. In 2003 a tribal member attending an ASU lecture discovered the unauthorized secondary uses; the tribe sued in 2004 and settled in 2010 for $700,000, with the blood samples returned and destroyed.

Mello and Wolf use the case to interrogate the central unresolved question in biospecimen research: what constitutes adequate informed consent when samples collected for one purpose are stored and reused for future, unrelated studies? They show that the case sits at the intersection of law and ethics—existing legal frameworks were insufficient to prevent harm or provide a clear remedy—and argue that the study population's own cultural and spiritual values must be incorporated into consent design, not merely acknowledged as an afterthought.

For this course, the case illustrates how power asymmetries in research relationships can reproduce colonial dynamics even within ostensibly beneficial health research. It also shows how abstract questions of consent translate into concrete violations of dignity, autonomy, and community self-determination—connecting directly to the week's themes of data ownership, community-led justice, and the limits of standard bioethical frameworks when applied across cultural contexts.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance
*Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019*  
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [ARDC summary](https://ardc.edu.au/resource/the-care-principles/), [Data Science Journal article](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043), and Web Search coverage)*

The CARE Principles were developed in 2018 at an international workshop on Indigenous data sovereignty in Gaborone, Botswana, organized by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance. They provide a people- and purpose-centered counterpart to the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which govern most open scientific data practices. Where FAIR optimizes for broad data sharing, CARE insists that the interests, rights, and self-determination of Indigenous data subjects must take precedence.

The four principles are:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems must be designed so that Indigenous peoples can derive benefit from data about them. This includes inclusive development and innovation, the right to equitable outcomes, and the expectation that data institutions build Indigenous capacity rather than substitute for it.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their authority to control those data must be substantively empowered—not merely formally acknowledged. This extends to data collected at individual, community, and national levels.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data bear ongoing responsibility to share how those data are used, to support Indigenous peoples' self-determination and governance, and to nurture respectful relationships with data-generating communities.
- **E — Ethics:** The creation, interpretation, and use of Indigenous data must uphold the dignity of Indigenous nations and communities. Researchers must navigate Indigenous rights and wellbeing as the primary ethical frame.

The CARE Principles have become a foundational governance document for Indigenous data sovereignty globally. They complement the Anderson et al. D4I paper—which explicitly builds on CARE—and provide the theoretical scaffolding for understanding why governance architecture, not just data access, is the site of justice in biotechnology research.
