## Readings Due — May 27 Section (Week 9: Community-Led Justice)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57 (2025): 2614–2619.

(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)

**Key Argument:** This paper introduces the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded tribal data repository in the United States — as a structural solution to the historical exclusion of tribal nations from control over their own biomedical data. The central claim is that Indigenous data sovereignty cannot be achieved through policy gestures or consent reform alone; it requires dedicated, tribally governed infrastructure that technically and ethically centers communities' authority over data from the ground up.

**Evidence & Mechanism:** Led by Krystal Tsosie (ASU), the D4I repository is described as a "safe harbor" for federally funded biomedical data originating from U.S. tribal nations. The governance model requires researchers to obtain approval from tribal governance bodies before accessing the repository's data — inverting the conventional model in which institutions hold data and occasionally consult communities. The repository integrates CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) alongside FAIR data principles, recognizing that technical interoperability is insufficient without accountability to Indigenous communities.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The article directly engages the course's central question — *who benefits from biotechnology, and who is left behind* — by diagnosing the structural asymmetry: genomic and health data about Indigenous peoples has historically benefited institutions, publishers, and broader populations while the originating communities received little. The D4I repository models what community-led justice *looks like in practice*: sovereignty over data, not just over land. By requiring tribal governance as a technical prerequisite (not an afterthought), it operationalizes participatory justice (Week 2) and responds directly to the research ethics failures documented in the Mello & Wolf reading.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363, no. 3 (2010): 204–207.

(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)

**Key Argument:** This NEJM perspective uses the landmark Havasupai v. Arizona Board of Regents case as a prism for a fundamental research ethics problem: broad consent forms designed for biological sample collection do not constitute adequate authorization for unlimited secondary use of those samples in wholly unrelated studies. The authors argue that the research community must establish clearer norms about what scope of future use requires fresh consent, and that Indigenous communities in particular have interests that existing informed-consent frameworks systematically fail to protect.

**Evidence:** The Havasupai, a small nation (approximately 650 members) living in the Grand Canyon, donated blood samples in 1989–1990 explicitly for research on the genetic basis of their community's disproportionate burden of type 2 diabetes. ASU researchers subsequently used the samples in at least 23 additional published studies — examining schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding — without the tribe's knowledge or consent. Some of these studies (particularly migration studies suggesting routes inconsistent with Havasupai oral histories) were experienced by the tribe as attacks on their cultural identity and origin narratives, not just as a procedural oversight. In April 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 to 41 tribal members, agreed to return the samples, and established support programs for the tribe. Mello and Wolf frame the central unanswered legal and ethical question: What constitutes adequate informed consent when samples collected for one purpose are stored and later repurposed?

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The case crystallizes the justice concerns running through the entire course. The Havasupai experienced a form of epistemic injustice: their consent was used to authorize research that affirmatively undermined their community's self-understanding. The authors' recommendations — specificity in consent forms, ongoing governance over biobanks, meaningful community consultation — anticipate the CARE Principles and the D4I repository. The case also illustrates the global dimension: Indigenous communities everywhere face analogous dynamics when research infrastructures controlled by majority institutions treat biological samples as resources to be extracted rather than as materials held in trust.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (2019).

(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources; CARE Principles document is publicly available at gida-global.org but PDF returned 403 in this session)

**Key Argument:** The CARE Principles — **C**ollective Benefit, **A**uthority to Control, **R**esponsibility, **E**thics — provide a governance framework for data *about* or *generated by* Indigenous peoples, designed to complement the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR governs technical data quality, CARE governs *power*: who controls data, who benefits, and what obligations data stewards owe to originating communities. The document's core claim is that data governance is not merely a technical challenge but a justice challenge, and that existing frameworks — even well-intentioned FAIR standards — are insufficient without structural accountability to Indigenous sovereignty.

**Evidence & Structure:** Each principle addresses a distinct axis of power:

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems must be designed so that Indigenous peoples can derive benefit from their data — including through inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and civic engagement, and equitable outcomes. Data collection from a community must flow back to that community, not only to researchers or institutions.
- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized and their authority to control such data must be empowered. This includes governance over data practices, Indigenous data governance institutions, and the right to determine who may access data and under what conditions.
- **Responsibility (R):** Those working with Indigenous data bear an affirmative responsibility to support Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit — including by expanding local capacity, supporting Indigenous researchers, and ensuring research outcomes serve community goals.
- **Ethics (E):** The Principles reframe ethics from regulatory compliance (IRB/ethics board approval) to value-based relationships embedded in Indigenous cultures and knowledge systems. The aim is to minimize harm, balance power, and position research within Indigenous ethical frameworks — not impose external ones.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The CARE Principles directly operationalize what the course calls participatory justice (Week 2) and data justice (Week 7) at the level of infrastructure. They were developed through an Indigenous-led process (Gaborone, Botswana, 2018), modeling the very community-led approach they advocate. They provide a vocabulary for evaluating whether a particular research practice — including the Havasupai case's failures and the D4I repository's promises — lives up to Indigenous communities' claims on their own data.
