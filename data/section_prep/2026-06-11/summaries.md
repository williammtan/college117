### Data Ownership: Indigenous Nations Examples (2026-05-27)

*(No lecture deck was found for this session; summary is reconstructed from syllabus context and course materials.)*

The May 27 lecture introduced **data ownership and sovereignty** as frameworks for understanding how communities—particularly Indigenous nations—can assert control over data generated about and by their members. Delivered by guest lecturer **Krystal Tsosie** (Diné/Navajo Nation, co-founder of the Native BioData Consortium), the session situated Indigenous data governance within a long history of extractive research: outside scientists collecting biological and cultural data from communities, then using it without consent or benefit-sharing.

Key themes included: (1) the distinction between *data ownership* (legal title) and *data sovereignty* (the broader right of peoples to govern data within their own cultural and political frameworks); (2) how colonial research practices—exemplified by the Havasupai case—eroded trust between Indigenous communities and biomedical institutions; (3) the emergence of the CARE Principles as a counter-framework to the FAIR data principles, centering people and purpose rather than mere technical openness; and (4) the D4I Tribal Data Repository as a structural, federally funded model for enacting sovereignty in practice. The session connected to prior course themes of participatory justice (Week 2) and research justice (Week 4), asking how communities can move from consultative roles to positions of genuine stewardship and control over biotechnology knowledge systems.

### Reading 1: Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025)

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [ASU News](https://news.asu.edu/20231206-nih-awards-9-million-indigenousled-tribal-data-repository-improve-community-health-response), [Science News profile of Tsosie](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/genetics-krystal-tsosie-indigenous-data-sovereignty), and web search summaries)*

**Key argument:** Anderson, Tsosie, and 20 co-authors present the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D4I TDR)**—the first federally funded, Indigenous-led data repository—as a structural solution to longstanding failures of data sovereignty in health research involving Native communities.

**Evidence and context:** The D4I TDR was funded by NIH ($9 million, via the RADx-UP initiative) and is led by Krystal Tsosie (ASU), a Diné/Navajo Nation member and co-founder of the Native BioData Consortium. The repository was developed in direct response to documented harms from prior research (including COVID-19 surveillance data collection) in which Indigenous health data was aggregated without Tribal Nation governance or benefit-sharing. The repository builds "rigorous Tribal Nation governance" protocols so that communities—not outside researchers—determine who accesses data, for what purposes, and under what conditions.

**Architecture of sovereignty:** Rather than making data openly available under FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable), D4I TDR gates access through community-controlled governance structures. Tribal nations can share data with researchers while retaining oversight of how it is used, interpreted, and disseminated. The system creates a "safe harbor" for Indigenous data by centering community data-sharing interests as primary.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This reading operationalizes the course's persistent question—*who benefits?*—by designing an institution in which Indigenous peoples are the answer. It moves the conversation from normative principles to institutional engineering, showing how sovereign data control translates into sovereignty over research agendas. It also connects to Week 2 (participatory justice) by demonstrating that genuine participation requires structural power, not just consultation.

### Reading 2: Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *NEJM* 363(3): 204–207 (2010)

*(External link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [Seeds of Native Health / NEJM PDF context](https://seedsofnativehealth.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Linda-Frizzell-HavasupaiAZStateData.pdf), [PMC Genomic Justice article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5310710/), [Havasupai Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havasupai_Tribe_v._the_Arizona_Board_of_Regents), and web search summaries)*

**Key argument:** Mello and Wolf use the Havasupai settlement (2010) to argue that existing informed consent frameworks are structurally inadequate for biospecimen research involving vulnerable populations—particularly when samples may be stored and repurposed for future, unrelated studies. The case is not merely a procedural failure but a signal that the entire consent architecture for biobanking needs redesign.

**The case:** Between 1990 and 1994, ASU researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 Havasupai tribal members, telling participants the samples would be used to study the genetic basis of type 2 diabetes—a pressing health problem for the community. Consent forms were written in English (a second language for many members) and contained broad language permitting "behavioral and medical" genetic studies. Unbeknownst to participants, samples were subsequently used for studies on schizophrenia, population migration, and inbreeding—topics culturally taboo and spiritually harmful to the Havasupai. In 2004 the tribe sued; in 2010 ASU settled for $700,000 and returned the blood samples.

**Ethical violations identified:** (1) *Scope mismatch*: consent was obtained for diabetes research but applied to unrelated investigations; (2) *language and literacy barriers*: broad English-language forms did not produce genuine understanding; (3) *cultural harm*: research on migration origins contradicted tribal cosmology; (4) *lack of reciprocity*: the community bore all the risks and received no health benefits.

**Recommendations:** Mello and Wolf argue for tiered consent systems, regular re-consent for new uses, and community-level engagement before any secondary use of stored samples—especially for Indigenous and other vulnerable communities.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This is the foundational "cautionary tale" for the week. It demonstrates how research justice failures are not random accidents but arise from structural asymmetries in power, literacy, language, and institutional interest. It also foreshadows the CARE Principles and D4I TDR as corrective responses—the case makes clear why governance frameworks centered on Indigenous authority and collective benefit are necessary, not merely aspirational.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)

*(External link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [GIDA/RDA one-pager PDF context](https://www.gida-global.org/care), [Data Science Journal full paper](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/dsj-2020-043), [ARDC explainer](https://ardc.edu.au/resource/the-care-principles/), and web search summaries including Carroll, Garba, et al. 2020)*

**Key argument:** The CARE Principles—**Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics**—are a people- and purpose-oriented governance framework for Indigenous data, developed by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (GIDA) in 2019. They argue that the dominant open-data paradigm (codified in the FAIR principles) is insufficient and potentially harmful for Indigenous communities because it ignores power differentials, colonial histories, and collective rights.

**The four principles explained:**

- **Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems must be designed so that Indigenous peoples can derive benefit from data about them. This means data should support community well-being, Indigenous-led governance, and equitable outcomes—not only researcher interests.

- **Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples have the right to determine how data representing their cultures, knowledges, lands, and peoples is governed, accessed, and used. This authority is grounded in Indigenous legal traditions and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), and it demands that Indigenous peoples be *leaders*, not merely consultants, in data stewardship.

- **Responsibility:** Non-Indigenous researchers and institutions working with Indigenous data bear responsibility for ensuring that data use supports self-determination and collective benefit. This includes building Indigenous data capacity, supporting an Indigenous data workforce, and ensuring respectful interpretation.

- **Ethics:** Indigenous rights and well-being must be centered at every stage of the data life cycle. Ethics is not a box-checking IRB exercise but an ongoing, relational commitment embedded in the full arc of research—from design through dissemination.

**Relationship to FAIR:** CARE does not replace FAIR but *frames* it. FAIR addresses technical data quality; CARE addresses justice in data governance. Both are necessary, but FAIR alone—in the absence of CARE—reproduces colonial extraction in a digital form.

**Connection to course justice themes:** CARE directly operationalizes several frameworks the course has examined: it applies capabilities-based thinking (who can actually exercise control?), participatory justice (genuine authority, not token engagement), and restorative justice (addressing the legacy of extractive research). In the context of Week 9, CARE functions as the normative backbone linking the Havasupai case (illustrating what happens without it) to D4I TDR (illustrating what structural implementation looks like).
