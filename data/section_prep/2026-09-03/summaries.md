## Reading Summaries — Due for May 27 Session (Week 9: Community-Led Justice)

### 1. Anderson MZ et al. (2025). "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics*, 57(11): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** This 2025 Nature Genetics correspondence introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D4I TDR)** — the first federally funded Tribal data repository. It argues that meaningful Indigenous data sovereignty requires dedicated institutional infrastructure, not simply policy commitments. By building a repository governed explicitly by Tribal Nation protocols, the work demonstrates that data sovereignty can be operationalized in practice, not just in principle.

**Evidence:** The repository was developed by a large author team that includes both academic researchers and community representatives from multiple Tribal Nations. It was funded federally and designed so that researchers can engage with Indigenous health data only under rigorous Tribal governance and with community-defined data-sharing agreements. The work builds on years of community-based participatory research frameworks and critiques of extractive genomic research.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The D4I TDR directly addresses the tension between open science norms (which favor broad data sharing) and Indigenous data sovereignty (which asserts community rights over self-generated data). It responds to a history in which Indigenous genomic data was collected under broad consent, then used in ways communities did not sanction — a pattern that eroded trust. By centering Tribal governance rather than individual researcher discretion, the repository instantiates a structural, rather than procedural, remedy for research injustice. Co-author Krystal Tsosie's involvement connects this paper directly to the session's guest lecture.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine*, 363(3): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** This NEJM perspective piece analyzes the landmark 2010 settlement in which Arizona State University paid $700,000 to 41 Havasupai tribal members after researchers used stored blood samples — collected in the 1990s for diabetes research — for additional genetic and anthropological studies without specific consent. Mello and Wolf argue that the case exposes a fundamental flaw in informed consent practices for biospecimen research: generic blanket consent for "future research" is ethically insufficient when a research community's values, beliefs, and vulnerabilities may make certain secondary uses profoundly harmful.

**Evidence:** Havasupai members consented to blood collection for diabetes research, but samples were subsequently used for studies on schizophrenia, inbreeding, and ancient migration patterns — research that contradicted tribal origin beliefs and stigmatized the community. The tribe sued the Arizona Board of Regents, eventually settling in 2010. Mello and Wolf examine the consent forms, the IRB oversight failures, and the gap between what researchers considered permissible secondary use and what the tribe understood they were consenting to.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The case illustrates how "standard" research ethics procedures (IRB approval, signed consent forms) can still produce injustice when they fail to incorporate the specific worldview and interests of the research community. For Indigenous populations, whose epistemologies may treat genetic data as tied to spiritual identity or communal belonging, standard individualistic consent frameworks are inadequate. The case became a foundational argument for community-level consent, data return policies, and ultimately for frameworks like CARE. It directly motivates the structural solution proposed in the Anderson et al. (2025) repository.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** The CARE Principles — **Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics** — were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) to address the limitations of the existing FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) for Indigenous contexts. Where FAIR focuses on making data open and technically accessible, CARE centers the rights and interests of data-generating communities. The core argument is that Indigenous peoples have the right to govern their own data as an expression of self-determination.

**Evidence and Sub-Principles:**

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems must enable Indigenous communities to benefit from data about them. This means inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and citizen engagement, and equitable outcomes — not just datasets made available to outside researchers.
- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples and nations have rights to data about their communities, territories, and resources. They must have the power to access, control, and govern these data according to their own protocols. This rejects the assumption that data, once collected, becomes the intellectual property of researchers or institutions.
- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to ensure that use of those data supports Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit. This shifts responsibility from communities to researchers and institutions.
- **Ethics (E):** Rights and wellbeing of Indigenous peoples must be the primary concern throughout the data lifecycle. Minimizing harm is insufficient; active benefit and respect for Indigenous rights and interests is required.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The CARE Principles represent a move from procedural justice (did we follow the rules?) to substantive justice (did communities actually benefit and retain control?). They emerged directly from failures like the Havasupai case and offer a practical governance vocabulary for evaluating whether data partnerships with Indigenous communities are genuinely equitable. The principles have since been adopted by national research infrastructure bodies (e.g., the Australian Research Data Commons) and are foundational to the D4I TDR's design.
