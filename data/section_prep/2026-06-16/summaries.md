### 1. Anderson, Tsosie, et al., "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty," *Nature Genetics* 57(11):2614–2619 (2025)

Anderson, Tsosie, Peters, Trinidad, and co-authors describe the first federally funded Tribal Data Repository — "Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations" (D4I), funded through the NIH's RADx initiative — created in response to a long history of genomic and health data being extracted from Indigenous communities without tribal consent or control. The repository is anchored institutionally within the legal jurisdiction of the Native BioData Consortium on the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation, the first Indigenous-led biodata repository housed inside a Tribal Nation's own authority — a structural choice co-author Krystal Tsosie describes as a "safe harbor" operating under tribal law and tribally defined data-sharing policies, rather than solely under federal human-subjects regulations. Instead of a single centralized gatekeeper, D4I uses a federated, decentralized access model, letting individual tribal nations or contributing communities set and enforce their own terms for who may use which data and for what purposes, while still supporting large-scale genomic and health research. The argument: Indigenous data sovereignty and rigorous scientific infrastructure are not in tension — tribal governance can be built into a repository's technical and legal architecture from the ground up rather than bolted on afterward. This directly extends the course's justice themes: it is a constructive, infrastructural answer to the harms documented in the Havasupai case and the surveillance/data-extraction patterns examined earlier in the quarter, operationalizing the CARE Principles' demand for Indigenous "Authority to Control" in an actual functioning institution.

### 2. Mello, M.M., & Wolf, L.E., "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples," *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3):204–207 (2010)

Mello and Wolf use the 2010 settlement of *Havasupai Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents* to examine what counts as adequate informed consent when biological samples collected for one purpose are stored and reused in unrelated future research. Between 1990 and 1994, ASU researchers collected blood samples from roughly 400 Havasupai tribal members who understood they were participating in a study of the genetic causes of the tribe's high rate of type 2 diabetes. In 2003, tribal member Carletta Tilousi discovered the same samples had also been used, without specific consent, in studies of schizophrenia, inbreeding, and population migration — including theories about Havasupai origins that contradicted the tribe's own traditional narrative, compounding the scientific harm with cultural and dignitary injury. The tribe sued in 2004; the case settled in April 2010, with ASU paying $700,000 to 41 plaintiffs, returning the physical samples, and acknowledging wrongdoing. Mello and Wolf use the case to illuminate a genuine values conflict in biobank ethics: broad, one-time consent for future secondary use is what makes biobanks scientifically valuable, but it can strip participants — especially members of small, identifiable, historically marginalized communities — of meaningful control over how their genetic and cultural information is used. The case is a touchstone for the course's data-ownership and community-led-justice themes: it makes concrete why procedural consent forms alone are insufficient, and why later frameworks like the CARE Principles and institutions like the D4I repository emerged as direct responses to exactly this kind of failure.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Embryo Project Encyclopedia/ASU, PMC review articles, NPR)*

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)

The CARE Principles, published in 2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (a Research Data Alliance group anchored by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance, GIDA), set out four people- and purpose-oriented principles meant to complement the data-oriented FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) that dominate open-science and open-data policy. **Collective Benefit** holds that data ecosystems should be designed so Indigenous peoples and communities — not only outside researchers or institutions — derive value, supporting Indigenous innovation, self-determination, and equitable outcomes. **Authority to Control** asserts that Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in data about themselves, their lands, and their knowledge must be recognized, grounded in individual and collective Free, Prior and Informed Consent, with Indigenous governing bodies empowered to determine what happens to "their" data. **Responsibility** places accountability on researchers and data users to build positive, reciprocal relationships with Indigenous communities, to be transparent about how data are used, and to build communities' own capacity and infrastructure for managing data rather than only extracting it. **Ethics** requires that data practices minimize harm, maximize benefit as defined by Indigenous frameworks (not outside metrics), promote justice, and anticipate and guard against future misuse. Together, the principles argue that "open" and "ethical" data are not synonyms — unrestricted access can itself be a form of injustice when it bypasses the people the data describes. CARE directly frames Week 9's discussion questions about stewardship, sovereignty, and the misuse of "community engagement," supplying the normative vocabulary used to evaluate cases like Havasupai and infrastructure like the D4I repository.
