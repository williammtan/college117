## Readings Due

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics*, 2025, 57(11):2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: PubMed abstract, ASU news coverage, EurekAlert)*

This comment piece in *Nature Genetics* introduces the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository**, described as the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. Authored by a consortium that includes Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) alongside two dozen co-authors from diverse Tribal Nations and research institutions, the article argues that existing genomic data systems have systematically excluded Indigenous communities from governance while harvesting their biological data.

The core argument is structural: current "broad consent" frameworks allow data collected for one purpose (e.g., diabetes research) to be reused indefinitely for unrelated studies (e.g., schizophrenia, migration) — often far from the communities that provided samples. The D4I repository proposes a federated, decentralized access model that routes data governance through Tribal Nation laws and policies rather than institutional review boards alone. This "safe harbor" design is intended to enable modern analytical tools including AI/ML, while ensuring that findings flow back to participating communities according to their own priorities.

**Connection to justice themes:** The article is a practical operationalization of the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics). It frames data sovereignty as inseparable from political sovereignty and health equity. The NIH funding (~$9 million spread across related awards) represents a federal acknowledgment that community-controlled data infrastructure is a public health imperative — but the article also implicitly asks whether institutions can cede enough control to make such partnerships genuinely self-determined.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2010, 363(3):204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: SSRN abstract, Semantic Scholar, Stanford Law School listing, PMC secondary analyses)*

This NEJM Perspective by Michelle Mello and Leslie Wolf (both then at Stanford) uses the landmark Havasupai settlement to examine the ethics and law of informed consent for stored biological specimens. In 2010, Arizona State University agreed to pay $700,000 to 41 Havasupai tribe members and return their DNA samples — the culmination of a case in which blood collected for type 2 diabetes research was subsequently used, without renewed consent, for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding. Crucially, the settlement set no legal precedent.

The article's central question is: **what constitutes adequate informed consent when biospecimens collected today may be used in unrelated studies years later?** Mello and Wolf do not provide a single answer; instead, they map the genuine tension between two legitimate values — scientific efficiency (broad consent enables large longitudinal datasets) and individual/community autonomy (specific consent respects the right to refuse participation in studies one might find offensive or harmful). They argue the Havasupai case reveals that generic consent language masks the reality that stored samples become a transferable resource, effectively separating people from their biological material in perpetuity.

**Connection to justice themes:** For Indigenous communities specifically, the stakes go beyond individual autonomy. Genomic data can be used to make population-level claims about ancestry, migration, or biological difference that carry cultural, political, and spiritual implications tribal members never authorized. The case illustrates how IRB oversight can fail to protect communities when individualist consent models are applied to inherently collective contexts — a justice failure at the level of research infrastructure design, not just researcher intent.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled/PDF — summarized from publisher landing page at gida-global.org (unreachable) + secondary sources: Carroll et al. 2020 Data Science Journal publication, Wikipedia, Ada Lovelace Institute, CASRAI)*

The **CARE Principles** (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in 2018–2019 as a people-centered counterpart to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR is oriented toward making data technically accessible to the broadest possible audience, CARE is oriented toward ensuring data governance serves the communities whose lives are represented in those data.

The four principles:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous Peoples to benefit from the data in ways aligned with their own collective goals and priorities — not primarily those of researchers or institutions.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples have rights and interests in Indigenous data, and their authority to control those data should be recognized and supported across the entire data lifecycle — from collection through governance, analysis, and future use.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how they are using those data and to help build data-related capacity in Indigenous communities and governance institutions.
- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data lifecycle, and research should support Indigenous self-determination, cultural health, and intellectual property rights.

**Connection to justice themes:** CARE provides the normative framework underlying both the Havasupai case analysis and the D4I repository design. A dataset can be fully FAIR (technically open and interoperable) while completely failing CARE — for example, if it is accessible to global researchers but was collected without community consent, strips cultural context, or enables uses (e.g., ancestry-based discrimination) the source community never authorized. CARE reframes "open data" as a justice question: open for whom, to do what, with whose permission?
