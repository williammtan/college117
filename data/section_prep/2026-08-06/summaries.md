## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty"
*Nature Genetics, Vol. 57, pp. 2614–2619 (2025)*
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article presents the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D4I), the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. Funded by a $9 million NIH RADx grant and led by Indigenous scientists at Stanford and the Native BioData Consortium, the D4I repository was designed to address a longstanding injustice: Indigenous communities have historically had their biological samples and health data collected by outside researchers with little say over how those data are used, who benefits, or who governs access.

The central argument is that conventional research data infrastructure—built around institutional norms that prioritize open science and broad data sharing—is incompatible with Indigenous data sovereignty. The D4I repository instead embeds Tribal Nation governance at its core: participating tribes retain authority over their data, determine what research questions can be asked, and must consent to any secondary use. The guiding motto, "For us, by us," encodes a commitment to centering Indigenous research priorities rather than external academic agendas.

The article situates this technical innovation within a history of colonial extractivism in genomic research—most notoriously the Havasupai case—and argues that structural, not merely procedural, change is required. By placing data stewardship in community hands, the D4I model offers a replicable template for achieving community-led justice in biotechnology. Its connection to the course's broader justice themes is direct: it operationalizes participatory justice (Week 2) by giving affected communities genuine decision-making authority, and addresses access and data justice (Weeks 5 and 7) by ensuring benefits flow to the source communities rather than being extracted from them.

### 2. Mello, Michelle M. & Wolf, Leslie E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples"
*New England Journal of Medicine, 363(3): 204–207 (2010)*
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective piece uses the Havasupai tribe lawsuit against Arizona State University as a lens for examining the law and ethics of informed consent when biological samples are collected for one purpose and later used for another. The case facts are stark: between 1990 and 1994, approximately 400 Havasupai members donated blood samples for a diabetes genetics study. Researchers subsequently used the same samples—without additional consent—for studies of schizophrenia, migration history, and population inbreeding, topics culturally taboo or deeply threatening to Havasupai identity. When tribe members discovered this in the early 2000s, they filed suit; the case settled in 2010 for $700,000 and the return of the samples.

Mello and Wolf identify two core failures. First, the original consent form used vague language ("behavioral/medical disorders") that could arguably authorize broad secondary research, but that participants—many of whom were not native English speakers and had not finished high school—reasonably understood as limited to diabetes. Second, existing federal regulations (the Common Rule) were poorly suited to govern secondary uses of stored biospecimens, creating a gap between legal compliance and genuine ethical practice.

Their key prescriptive argument is that researchers and IRBs must move beyond pro forma consent documents toward honest communication: explaining explicitly that stored samples may be used for future, unspecified studies, and building in mechanisms—such as community advisory boards—for ongoing tribal input. The case is a paradigmatic example for the course's justice themes: it demonstrates how research institutions, operating under the guise of scientific progress, can perpetuate colonial patterns of exploitation, and why community engagement and meaningful consent are preconditions for just biotechnology.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance
*Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019*
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles—Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics—are a governance framework developed by Indigenous data advocates through the Global Indigenous Data Alliance to complement the technically-focused FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR optimizes data for reuse by the research community at large, CARE insists that data stewardship must be evaluated through the lens of Indigenous rights and self-determination.

Each principle carries substantive content. **Collective Benefit** requires that data ecosystems be designed so that Indigenous communities—not just researchers or institutions—derive tangible benefits: improved health outcomes, stronger governance, equitable economic returns. **Authority to Control** recognizes Indigenous peoples' inherent rights over data pertaining to their lands, lives, and knowledge, and demands that this authority be actively empowered, not merely acknowledged. **Responsibility** places affirmative duties on researchers and institutions: those who work with Indigenous data must share how it is used and actively support the capacity of communities to govern their own data. **Ethics** requires that Indigenous rights and well-being be the primary consideration at every stage of the data lifecycle.

The principles are not merely aspirational—they have been operationalized in data sharing agreements, IRB protocols, and repositories like D4I. For the course, CARE functions as a concrete justice framework that bridges abstract ethical principles (Week 1's Rawlsian and capability approaches) with the practical governance tools needed to correct historical wrongs (Week 7's data justice themes). CARE makes explicit that data is never neutral: how it is collected, stored, analyzed, and shared always reflects and reproduces—or can challenge—existing power structures.
