## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al., "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57 (2025): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2025 Nature Genetics article by Matthew Anderson, Krystal Tsosie, Charee Peters, and colleagues introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository** — described as the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. The project grew from COVID-era research: the NIH's RADx-UP program generated Indigenous Common Data Elements that required a governance structure rooted in Tribal sovereignty rather than standard institutional data-sharing agreements.

The core argument is that existing data repositories were designed around mainstream research norms that do not accommodate Indigenous data sovereignty. The D4I TDR operationalizes the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) alongside FAIR data standards, ensuring Tribal Nations retain governance authority over their own data throughout the research lifecycle. Participating Tribes set their own data-sharing priorities; researchers work within those constraints rather than the reverse.

The repository is housed within the Native BioData Consortium, the first Indigenous-led nonprofit biodata organization located on a Tribal Nation (the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation). NIH awarded $9 million through Stanford University for its development. Tsosie, a member of the Diné/Navajo Nation, leads the ethical frameworks component.

The paper's justice argument is that data infrastructure is itself a site of colonial power: who designs, governs, and benefits from health data systems determines who has agency in medical research. By centering Indigenous governance structures from the ground up — technically and ethically — the D4I TDR models an alternative to extractive research paradigms. This connects directly to the course's recurring question of who benefits from biotechnology: here, the community explicitly owns the instrument of potential benefit.

### 2. Mello MM, Wolf LE. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *NEJM* 363 (2010): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2010 NEJM Perspective analyzes the landmark Havasupai Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents case as a lens for understanding the unresolved ethics of biospecimen research. In the 1990s, members of the Havasupai Tribe provided blood samples to ASU researchers for a diabetes study. The samples were subsequently used — without specific re-consent — in studies on schizophrenia, inbreeding patterns, and population migration. Researchers reportedly withheld information about the schizophrenia study during the consent process because tribal members were known to find the topic taboo. In 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 and returned the DNA samples to the tribe.

Mello and Wolf's key argument is that the case exposes a fundamental ambiguity in research ethics: **what constitutes adequate informed consent for samples that may be stored and used in future, unanticipated studies?** The consent form in the Havasupai case authorized "behavioral/medical" research broadly — language the tribe did not understand to cover studies unrelated to diabetes, partly because English was a second language for many members.

The article emphasizes three intersecting failures: (1) *scope failure* — consent did not specify or limit the range of permissible uses; (2) *cultural failure* — the consent process did not account for the tribe's collective decision-making norms or its spiritual beliefs about bodily materials (the tribe considered DNA sacred and expected its return); (3) *trust failure* — the breach permanently damaged relationships between the tribe and outside researchers, with some tribal nations still refusing to collaborate with ASU. The authors argue that biospecimen research requires specific consent for each new study, culturally appropriate consent processes, and community-level agreements where group interests are at stake. The case foregrounds the course's justice theme: when research subjects are marginalized communities, procedural harms (consent lapses) can compound into cultural and dignitary injuries that individual-focused frameworks cannot capture.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles were developed at a workshop during International Data Week in Gaborone, Botswana (2018) and formally released by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance in September 2019. They were created in response to the inadequacy of the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) for Indigenous contexts: FAIR focuses on data characteristics and maximizing open access, but says nothing about *who* governs data, *why* it is collected, or *whose interests* it serves. The CARE Principles reorient data governance around people and purpose.

**C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous Peoples can derive tangible value from data generated about or by them. This includes using data for community-defined priorities, not only for external research agendas.

**A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their power to govern that data must be actively strengthened — not merely acknowledged. This means creating governance structures where communities can say yes or no to specific data uses.

**R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data bear a positive obligation to be transparent about how data are used and to actively support Indigenous data governance capacity.

**E — Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should guide every stage of the data lifecycle. This includes cultural protocols, consent practices, and the decommissioning of data, not just initial collection.

The CARE Principles connect to the course's justice framework in a direct way: they model justice not as the absence of harm but as the presence of Indigenous agency. They are explicitly designed for "contemporary data ecosystems" — including genomic databases, health AI training sets, and biobanks — where data about Indigenous communities continues to be generated and used in ways that benefit outside parties. When placed alongside the Havasupai case (which illustrates what goes wrong without such governance) and the D4I Tribal Data Repository (which builds CARE into its infrastructure), the Principles supply the normative vocabulary that links individual case failures to systemic solutions.
