## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al. (2025). "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This Nature Genetics paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — described as the first federally funded genomic/health data repository designed to operate under rigorous Tribal Nation governance. The paper was co-authored by a large multi-stakeholder team including Tribal Nation representatives, Indigenous scholars (including Krystal S. Tsosie), bioethicists, and clinical researchers, reflecting the community-centered authorship model it advocates.

**Key argument:** Existing biomedical data repositories were not built with Indigenous data sovereignty in mind. Standard "open science" frameworks — which presuppose that broad data sharing is an unqualified good — inadvertently reproduce colonial extraction by treating Indigenous communities as data sources rather than data stewards. The D4I repository inverts this by requiring researchers to engage with Indigenous data only under explicit governance agreements negotiated with Tribal Nations, embedding tribal laws and community protocols directly into data access infrastructure.

**Evidence and design:** The repository functions as a "safe harbor" — a governed data commons that allows researchers to contribute and access data while remaining subject to Tribal Nation oversight. Governance protocols can vary by nation, reflecting the diversity of Indigenous communities and their distinct legal sovereignties. The paper draws on years of advocacy from the Indigenous data sovereignty movement and parallel frameworks like the CARE Principles, situating the D4I repository as a concrete technical implementation of long-standing normative demands.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This paper directly addresses structural justice in data infrastructure: who controls biomedical data about Indigenous communities, under what terms, and for whose benefit. It challenges the assumption that scientific openness is neutral, demonstrating instead that default data-sharing norms encode the interests of academic institutions and funders rather than source communities. The D4I repository is also an example of community-led innovation — rather than waiting for regulatory reform, Indigenous scholars and Tribal Nations built new infrastructure on their own terms. This connects to the course's broader examination of how communities can secure justice in biotechnology not merely by critiquing existing systems but by constructing alternatives.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspectives piece analyzes one of the most consequential cases in Indigenous research ethics: the 2010 settlement in which Arizona State University agreed to pay $700,000 to 41 members of the Havasupai Indian Tribe and return stored blood samples, following a lawsuit alleging that researchers used samples collected for diabetes research in unauthorized genetic studies. The unauthorized studies examined schizophrenia predisposition, inbreeding, and — most incendiary to the Havasupai — population migration history, which contradicted the tribe's own origin narratives and spiritual beliefs.

**Key argument:** The case exposes fundamental inadequacies in how informed consent is conceived for stored biospecimen research. ASU researchers believed their broad consent forms legally permitted secondary uses; the Havasupai argued they were misled, lacked meaningful understanding of what they had consented to, and that the unauthorized research violated tribal cultural and religious values. Mello and Wolf argue that neither side was simply wrong — the case reveals irresolvable tensions between three competing values: respect for individual persons (requiring informed consent), respect for communities (recognizing collective cultural and sovereignty interests), and scientific progress (which benefits from broad, open access to biological samples).

**Evidence:** The authors trace the consent language, the range of studies conducted without re-consent, and the legal and ethical debates that ensued. They find existing frameworks — based on individual informed consent and federal Common Rule protections — inadequate for contexts where harm is collective, where cultural injury cannot easily be priced, and where communities lack formal legal standing to object to research.

**Proposed reforms:** Mello and Wolf recommend enhanced community-level consent processes, ongoing engagement between researchers and source communities, and governance mechanisms that allow communities to restrict or withdraw future uses of stored samples. They also endorse "tiered consent" — allowing individuals and communities to specify acceptable uses rather than signing broad blanket consents.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The Havasupai case is the paradigmatic example of how biomedical research has exploited Indigenous communities under the cover of facially valid legal consent. It illustrates that formal procedural compliance (IRB approval, signed consent forms) does not guarantee substantive justice when communities lack the power to define the terms of research about themselves. The case is also a bridge to the CARE Principles and the D4I repository: both can be read as direct responses to the gaps the Havasupai case exposed.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources; the GIDA PDF is publicly accessible but the domain was blocked in this session)*

The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance's International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group as a people- and purpose-centered complement to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR focuses on making data infrastructure technically open and interoperable, CARE insists that openness without governance is not justice — data about Indigenous peoples can be FAIR while still violating Indigenous sovereignty and causing harm.

**The four principles:**

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous peoples — not only researchers or institutions — derive meaningful benefit. This includes being included in data-generating processes, receiving equitable outcomes from research, and retaining the right to self-determined development goals. The principle rejects the common framing in which Indigenous communities are research subjects whose "benefit" is an afterthought.

- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples possess rights and interests in data relating to their communities, lands, and cultures, and must have recognized authority to govern those data. This encompasses the right to establish governance protocols, set consent standards, determine who may access data and under what conditions, and withdraw data from use. Authority to Control operationalizes the political concept of Indigenous data sovereignty as a practical governance requirement.

- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data — researchers, institutions, funders — bear active responsibility for how those data are used and for nurturing relationships with source communities. This includes expanding Indigenous data literacy, supporting community capacity to govern their own data, and being transparent about data uses in ways communities can actually understand and act on.

- **Ethics (E):** Indigenous data practices must be grounded in relationships built on respect, reciprocity, trust, and mutual understanding — defined by the communities themselves rather than by external IRBs or funder ethics requirements. This principle recognizes that Western bioethics frameworks were not designed with Indigenous epistemologies or collective rights in mind and may actively misapply when imposed without modification.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The CARE Principles function as a normative architecture for thinking about data justice in Indigenous contexts. They provide language for articulating what justice requires beyond legal compliance — moving from "did we get consent?" to "does this data relationship reflect genuine respect for Indigenous self-determination?" In the context of the course's broader inquiry into who benefits from biotechnology and who is left behind, the CARE Principles help explain why technical openness, on its own, is insufficient: equitable access to data is not the same as equitable power over data.
