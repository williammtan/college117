## Readings Due — May 27, 2026 (Week 9: Community-Led Justice)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" *(Nature Genetics, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This Nature Genetics paper introduces the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository** — the first federally funded repository of its kind — designed to advance Indigenous health through community-governed genomic and health data infrastructure. Led by Arizona State University's Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), the repository departs fundamentally from extractive research models by placing Tribal Nation governance at the center of all data access decisions. Rather than depositing Indigenous community data into open or federally managed repositories where the originating communities retain little control, D4I builds a technical and ethical architecture in which community data-sharing preferences are authoritative. Researchers wishing to use D4I data must engage with governance structures defined by the participating Tribal Nations themselves.

The paper connects directly to course justice themes by operationalizing the concept of **Indigenous data sovereignty** — the right of Indigenous peoples to govern data about their communities, lands, and cultures. It responds to a long history of research exploitation (illustrated vividly by the Havasupai case covered in Reading 2) by creating institutional infrastructure rather than relying solely on consent forms. It also engages the CARE Principles (Reading 3): the repository embodies Collective Benefit by directing research toward community-defined health priorities, Authority to Control by ensuring Tribes govern access, Responsibility through ongoing researcher accountability, and Ethics by centering Indigenous values. The D4I model represents a shift from "participatory" add-ons to a governance architecture where sovereignty is built into the system itself.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective piece, published in July 2010, analyzes the legal settlement and ethical implications of Arizona State University's misuse of blood samples from members of the Havasupai Indian Tribe. In the early 1990s, ASU researchers collected DNA from Havasupai members ostensibly to investigate the genetic basis of type 2 diabetes, a serious health burden for the community. Without additional consent, the samples were subsequently used in a range of unrelated studies — including research on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and population genetics — in ways that contradicted the tribe's cultural beliefs and undermined their self-determination. In April 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 paid to 41 tribal members, along with several non-monetary remedies.

Mello and Wolf use the case to expose a structural problem at the heart of biomedical research: **the inadequacy of standard informed consent frameworks for stored biological specimens**. One-time consent, signed at the point of collection, cannot anticipate the full range of future uses that genomic data enables. The authors argue for stronger prospective consent mechanisms, community consultation before re-use, and robust governance frameworks for biobanks. For this course, the case is foundational: it shows how research infrastructure — even when nominally focused on community health — can reproduce colonial patterns of extraction, violation of cultural sovereignty, and disregard for community-defined research priorities. It illustrates why the CARE Principles and structures like D4I are not merely aspirational but necessary correctives to documented harms.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance

*(external link, paywalled/hosted externally — summarized from citation + secondary sources including GIDA and RDA documentation)*

The CARE Principles were developed in 2019 by the **International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group**, a working group under the Research Data Alliance, and published formally in the *Data Science Journal* (2020). They provide a people- and purpose-oriented complement to the data-centric FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which — while valuable for open science — do not address power imbalances, historical injustices, or Indigenous rights.

**CARE** stands for:
- **Collective Benefit**: Data ecosystems should enable Indigenous peoples to derive benefit from data; data governance should support self-determined community development and well-being.
- **Authority to Control**: Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data — including data about their lands, territories, and ways of life — must be recognized and their authority to govern such data affirmed.
- **Responsibility**: Those working with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how the data will be used, to support Indigenous data capabilities, and to nurture respectful relationships.
- **Ethics**: Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle; research must be aligned with Indigenous ethical frameworks, not just institutional review standards.

The CARE Principles are aligned with the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) and operationalize Indigenous data sovereignty as actionable governance criteria. In the course justice framework, they represent a transition from procedural consultation (asking communities for permission) to substantive self-determination (communities defining the terms of engagement). They also show how "justice" in data governance requires centering marginalized communities as governance actors, not merely beneficiaries or research subjects.
