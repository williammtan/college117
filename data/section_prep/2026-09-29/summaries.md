## Reading Summaries (for previous session, May 27, 2026)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. (2025). "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics*, 57(11), 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States, supported by a $9 million NIH RADx grant. Lead authors Matthew Z. Anderson (Eastern Band Cherokee) and Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo), together with a large consortium of tribal and academic partners, describe an infrastructure that inverts the conventional research data model: instead of routing Indigenous health and genomic data into academic or federal institutions, the D4I TDR places storage, access, and management authority with participating Tribal Nations under their own governance frameworks.

The article's central argument is that data infrastructure is itself a justice issue. Conventional repositories, however neutral they appear technically, systematically disadvantage Indigenous communities by situating control outside the community, making it impossible for tribes to determine how data about their members are analyzed, shared, or monetized. The D4I TDR treats data sovereignty as the baseline condition rather than an aspirational add-on: participating tribes retain access to and management of their data, the system is built to adhere to tribal laws and policies, and community-level datasets can be used to demonstrate health needs for grant applications — converting data sovereignty directly into material self-determination. The governance architecture is explicitly grounded in the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics), operationalizing rather than merely citing them.

Connection to course justice themes: The paper connects the course's analysis of who benefits from biotechnology to the more foundational question of who controls knowledge production. An Indigenous-led repository challenges the extractive model under which research communities have historically taken data from marginalized populations while returning few benefits — illustrating that designing just biotechnology requires not only equitable access to outputs but community ownership over the entire data lifecycle.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine*, 363(3), 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective examines the legal and ethical fallout from the Havasupai tribe's experience with Arizona State University researchers. Between 1990 and 1994, approximately 400 members of the Havasupai — a small Indigenous nation in the Grand Canyon — donated blood samples for an ASU diabetes study, believing their contribution was targeted at understanding the tribe's extremely high rates of type 2 diabetes. The consent form described study purposes broadly as investigating "behavioral and medical disorders." By 2003 the tribe discovered their DNA had been repurposed — without specific consent — for studies on schizophrenia, population migration, and patterns of inbreeding. These uses were not only outside the original agreement: the schizophrenia study in particular targeted a condition carrying heavy cultural stigma, and the migration findings contradicted Havasupai oral history about their ancestral origins. The case settled in April 2010 with ASU paying $700,000 to 41 tribal members and returning the remaining biological samples.

Mello and Wolf use the case to probe the unresolved core question in genetic research ethics: what constitutes adequate informed consent when biospecimens collected for one purpose are stored and potentially used in future, unrelated research? They argue that standard broad consent forms are structurally insufficient for populations where most signatories had not completed high school, many spoke English as a second language, and the collective implications of genetic findings (which attach to the tribe as a whole, not just individual donors) were neither explained nor considered. The case illustrates how bureaucratically "neutral" consent processes can mask coercive relationships, particularly when researchers hold power over communities that have historically had limited recourse against academic institutions.

Connection to course justice themes: The Havasupai case is a textbook illustration of epistemic injustice operating through research institutions. The tribe's communal knowledge, values, and interests were overridden by an individualist consent framework calibrated for different populations and purposes. It also reveals the racial and power asymmetries embedded in genetic research: harms fell on an already-marginalized Indigenous nation while researchers gained academic credentials from data taken without meaningful negotiation. The case directly motivates the governance frameworks — CARE Principles, tribal data repositories — discussed in the other readings.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019).

*(external link — document hosted on gida-global.org, access blocked by egress proxy — summarized from citation + secondary sources including GIDA documentation and Data Science Journal publication)*

The CARE Principles were developed and released by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in September 2019, principally through the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group within the Research Data Alliance. They were designed as a deliberate complement to the existing FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR is data-oriented — focused on the technical properties that make datasets maximally usable by researchers — CARE is people- and purpose-oriented, asking not just whether data are available but whether the communities they originate from benefit from, and exercise authority over, their use.

The four principles and their core sub-components:

**C — Collective Benefit.** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous Peoples can derive tangible, not merely symbolic, benefits from data about themselves. This means inclusive development and innovation, governance that reflects community priorities, and equitable outcomes — challenging extractive research models where outsiders profit from Indigenous data while communities receive little.

**A — Authority to Control.** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their authority to control those data must be actively empowered. This extends to data about Indigenous lands, territories, resources, knowledges, and geographic indicators, as well as data about people. It entails governance enabling communities to determine how they are represented and identified in data systems.

**R — Responsibility.** Those who work with Indigenous data are accountable for demonstrating — through meaningful, openly available evidence — how those data are used to support Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit. Responsibility is not passive; it requires active disclosure and benefit-sharing practices.

**E — Ethics.** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary concern at every stage of the data life cycle: collection, storage, analysis, and dissemination. This requires respecting Indigenous values, norms, and collective rights rather than subordinating them to general research ethics frameworks calibrated for Western individualist contexts.

Connection to course justice themes: The CARE Principles provide a structural vocabulary for the justice issues the course has tracked across genomics, AI, and neurotechnology. They reframe data governance as a site of sovereignty — a claim about who gets to define, collect, analyze, and benefit from knowledge. The framework directly responds to cases like the Havasupai situation and provides practical architecture for the kind of community-led justice the course's Week 9 module foregrounds.
