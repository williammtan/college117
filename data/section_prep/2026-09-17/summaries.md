### Reading 1: Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57 (2025): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — described as the first federally funded biomedical data repository built from the ground up under rigorous Tribal Nation governance. The authors argue that existing data infrastructure fails Indigenous communities because it centralizes control outside those communities, violating principles of Indigenous data sovereignty. The D4I repository is designed as a protected "safe harbor" for federally funded biomedical data originating with U.S. tribal nations and Indigenous peoples, where tribes themselves govern access, use, and sharing decisions.

**Evidence and mechanism:** The repository employs federated and decentralized access models rather than a single gatekeeping key, technically distributing control in alignment with the political sovereignty of individual Tribal Nations. Modern tools — including machine learning and AI — are incorporated, but always subordinated to tribal governance protocols and Tribal laws and policies. The design addresses long-standing critiques that even well-intentioned research institutions reproduce extractive data relationships with Indigenous communities.

**Connection to justice themes:** This paper exemplifies the course's Week 9 theme of community-led justice: it rejects charity-based or institution-centric frameworks in favor of structural redesign that places governance power where the community interest lies. Led by Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), the project embodies the principle that genuine health equity requires not just equitable access to research benefits but Indigenous authority over the research enterprise itself. The D4I repository operationalizes Indigenous data sovereignty — the right of Indigenous peoples to govern the collection, ownership, and application of data about their communities — as both a political and technical reality.

### Reading 2: Mello, Michelle M. and Leslie E. Wolf. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363, no. 3 (2010): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This perspective piece uses the landmark Havasupai case as a lens to interrogate foundational weaknesses in informed consent frameworks for biospecimen research. The authors argue that research ethics norms — as then practiced — were inadequate to protect Indigenous research participants when samples collected for one purpose are repurposed for unrelated studies, particularly when those studies implicate sensitive community-level questions about identity, ancestry, and stigmatized conditions.

**Evidence (the case):** In the early 1990s, Havasupai tribe members in Arizona donated blood samples to Arizona State University (ASU) researchers they understood were studying the high rates of Type II diabetes in their community. They later discovered (in 2003) that their DNA had been used — without their knowledge or consent — for additional studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding. The unauthorized use was not merely procedurally offensive; it cut against Havasupai cultural beliefs, particularly beliefs about migration origins. ASU ultimately paid $700,000 in settlement to 41 affected tribal members. Mello and Wolf analyze the case to ask: what would adequate informed consent have looked like, and why did existing IRB-approved consent frameworks fail?

**Connection to justice themes:** The Havasupai case crystallizes the tension between research utility (broad consent for sample reuse accelerates science) and community rights (communities have interests in how knowledge about them is produced and deployed). For COLLEGE 117, this reading is foundational: it illustrates how standard bioethical protections — IRB review, written consent — can still produce injustice when they are designed without attention to community-level harms, Indigenous worldviews, and the power asymmetries between research institutions and marginalized communities. The case directly motivates the sovereignty-centered frameworks in the other Week 9 readings.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — constitute a normative framework for governing research data involving Indigenous peoples, communities, lands, and knowledge. Developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance through an Indigenous-led workshop at International Data Week (Gaborone, Botswana, 2018), CARE is explicitly people- and purpose-oriented, in contrast to the data-centric orientation of the widely adopted FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). The CARE authors argue that FAIR principles are necessary but insufficient: without CARE, open and accessible data can become a tool for extracting value from Indigenous communities rather than serving them.

**The four principles:**

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous peoples can derive tangible benefit from data about themselves, their lands, and their knowledge. This includes enabling Indigenous innovation and self-determination, not just satisfying external research agendas.
- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognized and their authority to control such data must be empowered — including the right to govern access, use, and interpretation, consistent with Indigenous laws, customs, and protocols.
- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data have active responsibilities: to share how data are used, to support Indigenous data capabilities, and to nurture respectful relationships with communities. Responsibility is ongoing, not discharged at the moment of consent.
- **Ethics (E):** Rights and well-being of Indigenous peoples must be the primary concern at all stages of the data lifecycle, and research should align with Indigenous ethical frameworks rather than imposing external ethical standards.

**Connection to justice themes:** The CARE Principles directly address the structural dimension of justice: they reframe data governance as a domain of Indigenous political rights rather than research compliance. The principles respond to histories of extractive research — epitomized by the Havasupai case — by asserting that equitable biotechnology requires institutions to cede control, not merely refine consent forms. In the context of COLLEGE 117's core question ("Who benefits and who is left behind?"), CARE provides a concrete normative toolkit for redesigning data relationships so that communities historically excluded from research benefits become the primary beneficiaries and governors.
