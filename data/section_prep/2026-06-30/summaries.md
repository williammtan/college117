## Reading Summaries (Due: May 27, 2026)

### 1. Anderson MZ et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57, no. 11 (2025): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. Supported by a $9 million NIH award and led by Krystal Tsosie at Arizona State University in collaboration with Stanford University, the Native BioData Consortium, and multiple Tribal Nations, D4I grew out of COVID-19-era efforts to improve community health infrastructure for Indigenous peoples.

The article's central argument is that Indigenous health data must be governed by the communities from which it originates, not by outside academic or government institutions. D4I operationalizes this through governance structures in which data and engagement committees — each including representatives from every participating Tribal Nation — hold decision-making authority over how data is stored, accessed, and used. Researchers engage with data under rigorous Tribal Nation law and policy, inverting the historical model in which community data flowed outward to institutions with minimal community control or benefit.

The framework is explicitly grounded in the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance and the broader Indigenous Data Sovereignty movement. The repository functions as what Tsosie calls "a safe harbor" — a protected space where Indigenous data is held under Indigenous rules. This connects directly to the course's justice themes: equitable access to health biotechnologies requires equitable data infrastructure. When genomic and health data is extracted from marginalized communities without governance rights or benefit-sharing, the resulting research reinforces rather than remedies health disparities. D4I proposes data sovereignty as a structural solution, not merely an ethical aspiration.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363, no. 3 (2010): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM perspective examines one of the most consequential cases in the history of biomedical research ethics involving Indigenous communities. Between 1990 and 1994, researchers at Arizona State University collected blood samples from approximately 400 members of the Havasupai Indian tribe of Arizona to study the genetic basis of type 2 diabetes, which the tribe experiences at high rates. Participants signed a broadly worded consent form permitting study of "behavioral/medical disorders." In 2003, tribe member Carletta Tilousi discovered that the samples had also been used — without disclosure or consent — for studies on schizophrenia, inbreeding, and population migration. The schizophrenia research was particularly harmful given the cultural stigma attached to mental illness within the community.

In April 2010, Arizona State University settled for $700,000 and returned the DNA samples to the tribe. Because the case never went to trial, no binding legal precedent was established, leaving IRBs and research institutions without formal federal guidance on secondary uses of stored biospecimens.

Mello and Wolf's core argument is that the case reveals a structural mismatch between researchers' and participants' understandings of consent: institutions treat broad consent as authorization for flexible future uses; Indigenous communities typically understand consent as specific and purpose-limited. The authors call for tighter federal regulation of secondary research, culturally informed IRB practices, and consent procedures co-designed with community input. In the course's justice framework, the Havasupai case is an exemplar of epistemic injustice — a community's knowledge of itself appropriated without their meaningful participation — and a direct precursor to the governance models proposed in the CARE Principles and the D4I repository.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles — **C**ollective Benefit, **A**uthority to Control, **R**esponsibility, and **E**thics — were developed in 2019 by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance, an international coalition hosted within the Research Data Alliance. They were elaborated in the *Data Science Journal* (2020) and explicitly designed to complement the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which govern technical data management in research. Where FAIR focuses on openness and interoperability, CARE insists that technical accessibility without political accountability is insufficient — and potentially harmful — for communities whose data has historically been extracted without consent or benefit.

**Collective Benefit** holds that data systems must be designed so that Indigenous Peoples derive tangible, equitable outcomes — in governance capacity, innovation, and community wellbeing — not merely that data about them is discoverable by outsiders. **Authority to Control** recognizes Indigenous Peoples' inherent rights to govern their own data through free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC), distinguishing between "data for governance" (access to data that supports Indigenous self-determination) and "governance of data" (Indigenous Peoples actively controlling how data about them is used and shared). **Responsibility** obliges researchers, institutions, and governments using Indigenous data to actively support Indigenous self-determination and to provide transparent, verifiable evidence of community benefit — accountability must be demonstrated, not asserted. **Ethics** requires that Indigenous rights and wellbeing be the primary concern at every stage of the data life cycle, from collection through storage, analysis, dissemination, and archiving.

In the course's justice framework, CARE reframes the dominant open-data assumption that wider access is inherently progressive. For communities with deep histories of data extraction and exploitation — as the Havasupai case and the colonial history of genomic research make concrete — the right to restrict, govern, and benefit from data is itself a justice claim. CARE provides both the ethical rationale and the practical governance vocabulary for repositories like D4I.
