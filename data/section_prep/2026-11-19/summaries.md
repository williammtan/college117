## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" *(Nat Genet, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This Nature Genetics perspective introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository**, described by its authors as the first federally funded data repository of its kind, led by Arizona State University's Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation). The core argument is that Indigenous health data must be governed by Tribal Nations themselves — not extracted into general-purpose biomedical repositories controlled by outside institutions — in order to advance both health equity and Indigenous sovereignty.

The paper situates D4I within a growing framework of Indigenous data sovereignty: the principle that Indigenous Peoples have the right to own, control, access, and possess data derived from their communities. D4I is designed so that data-sharing agreements and access decisions adhere to each Tribal Nation's own laws and policies, rather than to federal or institutional defaults. A key technical innovation is the repository's support for **federated machine learning**, which allows health models to be trained across distributed tribal datasets without centralizing sensitive genomic or clinical data — preserving both privacy and governance.

The authors connect D4I directly to the course's justice themes: existing biomedical databases (e.g., the NIH's All of Us) are dominated by European-ancestry data, leaving Indigenous populations both underrepresented and dependent on research institutions for health insights about their own communities. By giving tribes a "safe harbor" for their own data, the repository enables tribes to generate local statistics for practical purposes — such as evidence to support grant applications for ambulances or health services — while also contributing to broader scientific knowledge on their own terms.

**Connection to justice themes:** The paper exemplifies **community-led justice** — not asking "how do we include Indigenous people in our systems" but "how do we build systems that Indigenous communities control." It bridges data justice, health equity, and self-determination.

### 2. Mello MM & Wolf LE — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This New England Journal of Medicine Perspective by bioethicists Michelle Mello and Leslie Wolf uses the Havasupai Indian Tribe case as a lens to examine fundamental failures of informed consent in research involving stored biological samples. The case began in 1990 when members of the Havasupai Tribe in Arizona agreed to provide blood samples to Arizona State University researchers to study the tribe's high rates of type 2 diabetes. Without the tribe's knowledge or additional consent, the samples were later used for unrelated studies on population migration patterns, schizophrenia, and inbreeding — research touching on deeply sensitive cultural and spiritual beliefs about Havasupai origins. When tribal member Carletta Tilousi discovered the unauthorized uses in 2003, the tribe sued; the case settled in 2010 for $700,000 and the return of all samples.

Mello and Wolf argue the case exposed structural gaps in the consent framework for biobank research: standard consent forms at the time did not adequately distinguish between the original research purpose and potential future uses of samples. The authors call for broader, more transparent consent protocols that explicitly enumerate the range of permissible uses — including sensitive research on ancestry, mental health, and behavioral genetics — and that build in ongoing community engagement mechanisms.

**Connection to justice themes:** The case illustrates how the existing research ethics apparatus failed to protect Indigenous research participants, treating their biological material as a scientific resource rather than recognizing their rights to self-determination. It is a canonical example of how procedural compliance (IRB approval) can coexist with deep substantive injustice. For the course's framework, it grounds abstract principles in a concrete legal and ethical failure, and shows why community consent — not just individual consent — matters in Indigenous research contexts.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(GIDA, 2019)*

*(external link — publisher PDF blocked; summarized from open secondary sources including CODATA Data Science Journal publication and RDA documentation)*

The CARE Principles were published in 2019 by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)**, developed by an Indigenous-led working group within the Research Data Alliance's International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group. They are explicitly framed as a **people-and-purpose complement** to the data-centric FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which address how data should be managed but not by or for whom. The acronym stands for:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should enable Indigenous Peoples to benefit collectively from data about their communities, including through inclusive development, improved governance, and equitable outcomes. Data use that benefits only the researcher or institution, while imposing risks on the community, is insufficient.

- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples have the right to govern their data. This includes rights to develop cultural governance protocols, determine who may access data, and have data returned to community control. It is the strongest statement of data sovereignty in the framework.

- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how that data is used, and to anticipate potential future harms. Responsibility extends beyond the immediate research relationship to future uses of archived data.

- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing must be prioritized across the entire data lifecycle, from collection to archival. This requires researchers and institutions to continuously evaluate whether their data practices minimize harm and maximize community benefit, centering Indigenous values and epistemologies.

**Connection to justice themes:** CARE provides a governance framework that makes the abstract principle of Indigenous sovereignty operational. It is directly applied in the design of repositories like D4I: each of the four principles maps onto a design requirement (e.g., federated learning for Collective Benefit, tribal law adherence for Authority to Control). The principles also critique the FAIR framework as an incomplete standard for data justice, since FAIR optimizes for scientific accessibility while CARE centers community rights.
