The May 27 lecture examined data ownership and Indigenous data sovereignty as dimensions of justice in biotechnology. The session asked: what does it mean for a community to exercise sovereignty over its own data, and what technological and ethical challenges arise when data stewardship shifts from outside researchers to the communities themselves? Drawing on the Week 9 Community-Led Justice framework, the lecture analyzed how historical research abuses — particularly involving Indigenous biologic samples — shaped contemporary demands for community control. The Havasupai case served as a key case study in how broadened consent, data repurposing, and researcher indifference to community values can cause serious harm. The CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) were introduced as a practical governance framework developed *by* Indigenous communities to correct the extractive orientation of conventional open-data norms (e.g., FAIR principles). The lecture also explored how newer institutional forms — such as the Tribal data repository — offer concrete infrastructure for communities to govern their own genomic and health data in ways that align with cultural values and self-determination. Discussion questions probed how community engagement can be misused to perpetuate injustice and what genuine justice in biotechnology research governance looks like.

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" *(Nat. Genet. 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** Anderson, Tsosie, and colleagues introduce the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D4I TDR) — the first federally funded Tribal data repository — as a model for reconciling the demands of large-scale health research with Indigenous data sovereignty. The article argues that conventional research data infrastructure systematically undermines tribal self-determination because it is designed for open sharing under norms that treat data as a public good, ignoring Indigenous peoples' collective rights over information about their communities.

**Evidence and structure:** The D4I TDR was established with $9 million in NIH (RADx) funding across six collaborative awards anchored by Stanford University and including ASU, the Native BioData Consortium, UC-Santa Cruz, UW-Seattle, and UW-Madison. Nearly half of the research teams are led by Indigenous faculty scientists. The repository's architecture places Tribal Nations — not universities — in a governance position: tribes control access, set sharing conditions, and retain the right to withdraw data. This operationalizes CARE Principles at the infrastructure level.

**Connection to justice themes:** The paper directly addresses distributive and procedural justice in research: who benefits from genomic data, who controls it, and who bears the risks of misuse. By centering tribal governance, it challenges the extractive model in which Indigenous communities contribute biological samples and bear research risks while scientific and economic benefits flow outward. The co-authorship of 22 researchers (many Indigenous) itself models the community-engaged alternative. This reading pairs naturally with the Havasupai case as "problem / institutional solution."

### 2. Mello & Wolf — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** Mello and Wolf use the Havasupai case as a lens to examine a critical unresolved problem in biomedical ethics: what constitutes adequate informed consent when biospecimens collected for one purpose are stored and subsequently used in secondary, unrelated research? Their core claim is that standard broad-consent practices are ethically insufficient for research involving Indigenous communities, and that regulatory frameworks (primarily the Common Rule) failed to protect tribal members' rights and interests.

**Facts of the case:** Beginning in 1989–1990, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 Havasupai tribal members for diabetes research. Tribal members signed a consent document authorizing study of "behavioral/medical disorders" — language many did not understand clearly, as English was a second language for many participants. Without seeking additional consent, other ASU researchers later used the stored samples to publish studies on schizophrenia, population inbreeding, and the tribe's Asian origins — topics directly contrary to Havasupai cultural beliefs and historical memory. The tribe discovered the unauthorized use in 2003. In April 2010, ASU paid $700,000 to 41 tribal members, returned all stored samples, and agreed to fund scholarships and health clinic support.

**Evidence and policy recommendations:** Mello and Wolf analyze how regulatory loopholes (broad consent forms, lax IRB oversight of secondary use, no requirement for community-level consultation) enabled the harm. They propose that IRBs require more specific consent language, that community advisory boards be convened before any biospecimen research with tribal populations, and that researchers treat cultural and spiritual values — not only physical harm — as legitimate ethical concerns.

**Connection to justice themes:** The case exemplifies epistemic injustice (the tribe's knowledge of its own origins was dismissed), procedural injustice (no meaningful community input), and distributional injustice (scientific knowledge extracted, community stigmatized, no benefit returned). It anchors Week 9's broader question about how biotechnology research can harm rather than help marginalized communities, and sets up the CARE Principles and the D4I TDR as corrective frameworks.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(GIDA, 2019)*

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** Developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) and first presented at International Data Week 2018 in Botswana, the CARE Principles articulate a people- and purpose-oriented governance framework for Indigenous data. The central claim is that the dominant open-data framework — FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) — while valuable for scientific reuse, is insufficient and potentially harmful for Indigenous data because it prioritizes data availability over the rights and interests of the communities the data describes. CARE principles do not oppose FAIR; rather, they insist that openness must be conditioned on Indigenous authority, benefit, responsibility, and ethics.

**The four principles:**
- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems shall be designed so that Indigenous peoples can derive benefit — economic, social, cultural — from data about their communities. Research should create reciprocal value, not extraction.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their authority to govern access and use must be operationalized in practice, not merely acknowledged in principle.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those working with Indigenous data have an active responsibility to share how data are used to support Indigenous self-determination, to build Indigenous data capacity, and to account for how their work either advances or undermines tribal governance.
- **E — Ethics:** Research involving Indigenous data must prioritize ethical practices defined by Indigenous communities themselves, taking seriously Indigenous values, cultural norms, and rights — not just IRB compliance under Western bioethical frameworks.

**Connection to justice themes:** CARE is simultaneously a diagnostic framework (exposing how FAIR and conventional open-access norms encode settler-colonial assumptions) and a constructive one (providing actionable criteria for redesigning research infrastructure). It resonates with the course's Week 1 ethical frameworks by making explicit which conception of justice — liberal individualism, communitarian, capabilities-based — is operating in data governance. By grounding rights in collective identity rather than individual consent, CARE challenges individualist informed-consent models central to the Mello/Wolf analysis. This tension — individual vs. collective rights in genomic research — is a key discussion thread for the May 27 section.
