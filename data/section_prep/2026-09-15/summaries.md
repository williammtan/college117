### 1. Anderson MZ et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" *(Nature Genetics 57:2614–2619, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article introduces the **RADx Tribal Data Repository (TDR)**, the first federally funded data repository for American Indian and Alaska Native health data that is governed by Indigenous communities themselves. Funded through the NIH's Rapid Acceleration of Diagnostics (RADx) program with approximately $9 million, the project is led by the **Native BioData Consortium** (the first Indigenous-led nonprofit biodata repository, located on the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation) in collaboration with Stanford University and a broad coalition of tribal scientists and advisors.

The repository's central innovation is not technical but governance-based: Tribal Nations have sovereign rights to determine which data is shared, with whom, under what conditions, and for what purposes—without requiring federal or university oversight to intermediate those decisions. The infrastructure is built on Indigenous ethical frameworks emphasizing respect, trust, transparency, and sustainability, and it incorporates the CARE Principles for Indigenous data governance.

The paper argues that standard research data repositories have historically extracted data from Indigenous communities without meaningful return or control, contributing to health inequities rather than addressing them. The TDR attempts to reverse this by ensuring that communities derive direct benefit from their own data, that data are not repurposed without consent, and that the repository is accountable to Indian Country rather than to academic or federal funders.

In the course's justice framework, this article represents a structural intervention: not merely advocating for inclusion in existing research systems, but building sovereign infrastructure that embeds participatory justice at the institutional level.

### 2. Mello MM & Wolf LE — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM 363(3):204–207, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources; PDF available at seedsofnativehealth.org and Semantic Scholar)*

In 1989, ASU researchers initiated a "Diabetes Project" with the Havasupai Tribe—a small Indigenous community living in a remote area of the Grand Canyon with unusually high rates of Type 2 diabetes. Tribal members donated blood samples and signed consent forms to "study the causes of behavioral/medical disorders." However, the samples were subsequently used for at least a dozen other studies, including research on schizophrenia, migration patterns (which conflicted with the tribe's own origin beliefs), and population inbreeding—topics the Havasupai considered deeply taboo and harmful to their cultural identity.

In 2003, a tribal member attending an ASU lecture discovered these additional studies. After a decade-long legal battle, the Havasupai and ASU settled in 2010 for **$700,000**, and the blood samples were returned to the tribe.

Mello and Wolf use the case to examine a core tension in biomedical research ethics: **what constitutes adequate informed consent when biological samples may be stored and repurposed in future, unanticipated studies?** Their key recommendations include: requiring study-specific rather than blanket consent for sample reuse; returning to communities for re-consent before launching materially different analyses; and recognizing that Indigenous communities experience data harms collectively, not just individually—so conventional individual-consent frameworks are insufficient.

For this course, the Havasupai case is a canonical illustration of how extractive research practices inflict dignitary, cultural, and political harm on communities that trusted researchers, and why participatory justice requires structural safeguards, not just good intentions.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)*

*(external link — publisher PDF at gida-global.org is blocked remotely; summarized from GIDA website, ARDC, and published open-access commentary)*

The **CARE Principles** (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) and formally released in September 2019 following a workshop at the International Data Week/RDA Plenary in Gaborone, Botswana. They are explicitly designed as a **people- and purpose-oriented complement** to the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which focus on technical data management without addressing power relations or who benefits.

- **Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems must be designed to enable Indigenous peoples to derive direct benefit from data generated about or by their communities—in economic, governance, cultural, and health terms.
- **Authority to Control (OCAP-adjacent):** Indigenous peoples have the right to govern their own data—including what is collected, how it is stored, who can access it, and what questions it may be used to answer—in accordance with their own values and collective interests.
- **Responsibility:** Those working with Indigenous data have ongoing obligations to share how data are being used, to support data literacy and capacity within communities, and to ensure that data activities strengthen, rather than undermine, Indigenous self-determination.
- **Ethics:** The creation, interpretation, and use of Indigenous data must uphold the dignity of Indigenous nations and peoples; ethical review must engage community values and lived experience, not just Western bioethical frameworks.

The CARE Principles have been adopted by major data governance bodies and research institutions worldwide. In the course's justice framework, they represent a codified counterpoint to extractive data practices: governance standards that center Indigenous sovereignty rather than treating communities merely as data sources to be managed responsibly.
