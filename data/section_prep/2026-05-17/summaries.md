## Previous Lecture Summary: Genomic Surveillance — May 13, 2026

The May 13 lecture, "Data Justice & Genomic Surveillance," examined the public health promise and justice failures of genomic surveillance. Opening with two guiding questions—who benefits and who is harmed, and what trade-offs arise—the lecture defined genomic surveillance as the continuous sequencing and analysis of pathogen and human genomes to track disease, endorsed globally by the WHO during COVID-19. Genuine benefits were outlined: early outbreak detection, vaccine and diagnostic development, tracking antimicrobial resistance, and guiding public health policy, especially in low-resource settings disproportionately burdened by infectious disease.

The lecture then foregrounded justice tensions. Genomic infrastructure is expensive ($100,000–$700,000 to establish; $20–$200 per sequence; up to 10× more costly for poor countries), meaning poorer nations are least able to benefit from a tool marketed as a global public good. The second half turned to misuse through U.S. examples: the FBI's CODIS database (Combined DNA Index System); the 2013 Supreme Court ruling *Maryland v. King* allowing DNA collection from unconvicted arrestees in 30 states; and California's database of over 900,000 unconvicted arrestees. The lecture's central justice point: racial minorities are disproportionately represented in law enforcement DNA databases, creating risk of targeting via familial matches. A concluding slide declared "broader data collection ≠ justice," connecting genomic surveillance to the course's broader inquiry into who bears the costs of biotechnology.

## Readings: Previous Session (May 13 — Week 7: Data & Justice)

### 1. Moreau, Y. (2019). Crack down on genomic surveillance. *Nature*, 576(7785), 36–38. https://doi.org/10.1038/d41586-019-03687-x

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Yves Moreau, a computational biologist at KU Leuven, published this short but pointed comment in *Nature* to indict the international scientific community for enabling China's genomic surveillance state—specifically its targeting of the Uyghur Muslim minority in Xinjiang. His central argument is that academic journals, universities, and laboratory equipment manufacturers that publish, partner with, and supply China's police-linked genomics research bear direct moral responsibility for the harms that research enables, and must act to end their complicity.

Moreau's evidence is striking: Uyghurs are studied in China's genetic literature approximately 30 times more intensively than the Han majority population. Across more than 500 publications characterizing China's ethnic minority populations genetically over the prior eight years, roughly half included a co-author from the Chinese police, military, judiciary, or government institution. The research is conducted without meaningful informed consent—DNA is collected from Uyghurs as part of mandatory state processes, including military conscription and ordinary police interactions.

Moreau calls for four forms of action: academic journals should retract papers relying on non-consensually collected data; laboratory equipment manufacturers such as Thermo Fisher Scientific should cease selling forensic DNA tools to Chinese state police; universities should bar faculty from collaborating with Chinese police-affiliated institutions; and international scientific bodies must develop binding standards for research conducted under authoritarian regimes. The piece anticipates the wave of retractions that would follow years later and foreshadows both the Hawkins and Imbler readings.

For COLLEGE 117's justice framework, Moreau illustrates how biotechnology supply chains—including the global scientific publishing apparatus—can become instruments of ethnic persecution, and how the language of legitimate science can mask surveillance-state violence against marginalized communities.

### 2. Hawkins, A. (2023). Academic paper based on Uyghur genetic data retracted over ethical concerns. *The Guardian*. https://www.theguardian.com/world/2023/dec/29/academic-paper-uyghur-genetic-data-retracted-ethical-concerns

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Amy Hawkins's Guardian article reports on the December 2023 retraction of a paper published in *Forensic Science International: Genetics*, four years after Moreau's Nature call to action made it notorious. The 2019 paper, "Analysis of Uyghur and Kazakh populations using the Precision ID Ancestry Panel," was produced by forensic geneticists at the University of Copenhagen; it involved 203 blood and saliva samples from Uyghurs and Kazakhs in Xinjiang. Elsevier's post-publication investigation found that the researchers had not obtained required ethical approval for sample collection.

The retraction was not an isolated event. Investigators, including Moreau himself, identified over 80 papers in international journals using DNA from Uyghur or Tibetan populations, with serious questions as to whether collection involved any real consent—human rights organizations contend that Xinjiang DNA collection is a function of state surveillance, not voluntary research participation. A parallel retraction wave, driven by the same researchers and activists, saw 19 papers retracted from a single genetics journal in early 2024.

Hawkins highlights the systemic failure of peer review: for years, editorial systems designed to screen for ethical red flags allowed police-adjacent research to pass through. The article raises uncomfortable questions about how journals headquartered in democratic countries apply—or fail to apply—ethical standards to research produced under authoritarian conditions, and whether the speed of retraction was sufficient.

For COLLEGE 117, the piece shows how research institutions in liberal democracies have served as unwitting—or complicit—conduits for surveillance-state science, and raises the question of who bears responsibility when marginalized ethnic groups become research subjects without meaningful consent or benefit.

### 3. Imbler, Sabrina. "The DNA of Roma People Has Long Been Misused, Scientists Reveal." *The New York Times*, November 17, 2021. https://www.nytimes.com/2021/11/17/science/genetics-ethics-roma.html

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Sabrina Imbler's New York Times science article reports on a landmark study published in *Nature* in which researchers in Germany and the UK audited more than 450 genetic papers that used Roma DNA, documenting a decades-long pattern of profound ethical violations. The Roma—Europe's largest ethnic minority, historically subject to persecution including genocide during the Nazi period—have been among the most studied and least protected populations in genomics research.

The violations documented span decades and methods: a 1981 Hungarian study sampled blood from Roma people incarcerated in Hungarian prisons, classifying prisoners as Romani based solely on appearance before sampling—a practice that is both scientifically baseless and ethically indefensible. A 2015 study on Roma genetic origins, after finding evidence of South Asian ancestry, uploaded its full dataset to public databases used by law enforcement globally for forensic identification—without participants' awareness that their DNA could be used to build criminal databases.

The researchers call for a global oversight board to audit Roma and other marginalized-community DNA currently in public databases, enhanced ethics training for genomicists studying historically persecuted groups, and engagement with Roma communities before, during, and after research. They argue that modern genetics has replicated the logic of historical race science: populations are studied for the benefit of the scientific enterprise, not the studied community.

For COLLEGE 117, this piece connects the course's threads on genomic surveillance and research ethics: it shows that the same patterns of non-consensual collection and surveillance-adjacent data use that characterize China's treatment of Uyghurs are embedded in European genomics history as well. The misuse of genetic data is not a singular geopolitical aberration—it is a structural feature of how science has historically related to marginalized populations.

## Readings: Current Session (May 18 — Week 8: Environmental Justice)

### 4. Crawford, Kate. "Earth." Chapter 1 in *The Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence*. Yale University Press, 2021.

*(summarized from matched PDF)*

Kate Crawford opens *The Atlas of AI* by descending over Silicon Valley in a jet and ends in the Nevada desert at a lithium brine mine, making a literal material argument: AI is not a cloud of pure code but a planetary extraction enterprise, and its costs are borne primarily by disadvantaged communities and the natural world while benefits concentrate with corporate elites.

Crawford traces AI's supply chain through interlocking layers. The *mineralogical layer* runs from Silver Peak, Nevada (the only U.S. lithium mine, acquired by Albemarle Corporation for $6.2 billion in 2014) to cobalt from the war-torn Congo, to rare earth elements processed in Baotou, Inner Mongolia, where a toxic black lake five-and-a-half miles in diameter holds over 180 million tons of ore-processing waste, to tin from the Indonesian islands of Bangka and Belitung where unregulated gray-market miners scrape the seabed using their lungs as suction tubes. The *energy layer* demolishes the "myth of clean tech": a single NLP model training run produces over 660,000 pounds of CO₂—equivalent to five car lifetimes—while China's data centers drew 73% of their power from coal in 2018. The *logistical layer* adds that a single container ship emits the pollution equivalent of 50 million cars. Crucially, these costs are systematically kept out of sight: "ignorance of the supply chain is baked into capitalism."

Crawford frames AI as a contemporary "megamachine" (Lewis Mumford's concept)—a vast system whose inner workings are concealed even from those who operate it. She closes at Blair, a Nevada ghost town born and abandoned within twelve years of a gold rush, warning that Silver Peak will follow. The chapter's justice argument is direct: environmental damage is an externalized cost, systematically displaced onto the world's poorest people and ecosystems. Understanding AI's planetary footprint is prerequisite to working toward justice.

### 5. Ray, Keisha, and Jane Fallis Cooper. (2023). "The Bioethics of Environmental Injustice: Ethical, Legal, and Clinical Implications of Unhealthy Environments." *The American Journal of Bioethics*, 24(3), 9–17. https://doi.org/10.1080/15265161.2023.2201192

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Keisha Ray (University of Texas McGovern Medical School) and Jane Fallis Cooper (University of Toronto) argue that bioethicists have neglected environmental injustice as a core domain—and that this neglect directly undermines any serious commitment to health justice. Their central claim: if bioethics is to pursue health equity, it must engage with environmental injustice, because differential exposure to toxic environments is one of the most powerful and systematically racialized drivers of health disparities in the United States and globally.

The article advances three interlocking arguments. First, classical bioethical principles—autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice—already imply a duty to address environmental harms: exposure to pollution compromises autonomy by degrading cognitive and bodily capacity; it causes harms clinicians are obligated to prevent; and it tracks race and socioeconomic status so systematically as to constitute a direct violation of the justice principle. Second, environmental law offers underutilized tools: the authors advocate for a legally enforceable right to a healthy environment as a necessary complement to bioethical discourse, arguing that bioethicists should engage with regulatory and legislative frameworks as well as philosophical ones. Third, clinicians themselves carry novel ethical obligations when treating patients whose conditions are caused or worsened by environmental toxins—disclosure of environmental risk factors, appropriate referral, and community advocacy become ethical requirements of clinical practice.

Ray and Cooper's deeper critique is that bioethics has remained fixated on individual patient-level interactions while ignoring the structural conditions—industrial pollution, chemical contamination, heat islands, flooding—that determine who gets sick in the first place. The paper generated substantial commentary, signaling its status as a programmatic provocation for the field.

For COLLEGE 117, this piece bridges the course's bioethics foundations and its environmental justice week: it argues that standard ethical frameworks are not merely applicable to environmental harms but obligated to address them, particularly given their disproportionate impact on communities of color.
