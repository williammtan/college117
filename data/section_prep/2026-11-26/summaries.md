## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" — *Nature Genetics*, 2025

*(external link — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2025 comment in *Nature Genetics* introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository, described as the first federally funded repository specifically designed to hold biomedical data from U.S. tribal nations and Indigenous peoples under Indigenous governance. The project is led by ASU bioethicist Krystal Tsosie and is anchored by the Native BioData Consortium (NativeBio), with $9 million in NIH funding through the RADx initiative. External advisory committees composed of tribal subject-matter experts include voting panels that review and approve data-access requests, centering community decision-making rather than delegating control to academic institutions.

The article's key argument is that data sovereignty is not merely a legal concept but a structural condition that must be built into research infrastructure from the outset. The D4I repository operationalizes this by implementing the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics), enabling federated machine-learning models that respect community-defined principles of dynamic consent, and creating mechanisms for Tribal Nations to set the terms of access rather than simply being consulted. This directly addresses the historical pattern — exemplified by the Havasupai case — in which Indigenous biological data was collected under narrow consent and then reused in ways communities found harmful or culturally violating.

The connection to the course's justice themes is sharp: the D4I model challenges the assumption that scientific progress is inherently beneficial and demands that "benefit" be defined by the affected community. It also engages participatory justice (Week 2) by moving beyond symbolic consultation toward genuine co-governance of research infrastructure.

### 2. Mello, M.M. & Wolf, L.E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" — *NEJM*, 2010

*(external link — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2010 NEJM Perspective uses the Havasupai v. Arizona Board of Regents case as a lens for examining what constitutes adequate informed consent when biological samples are collected for one research purpose and later used for others. Between 1990 and 1994, approximately 400 members of the Havasupai Tribe provided blood samples to ASU researchers hoping to find genetic clues to the tribe's high diabetes rate. The tribe later discovered — reportedly around 2003 — that the samples had been used in studies on schizophrenia, human migration patterns, and inbreeding, some of which directly conflicted with the tribe's traditional origin stories. The tribe sued in 2004; the case settled in 2010 with Arizona State University paying $700,000 to 41 tribal members and returning all blood samples.

Mello and Wolf use this case to surface a then-unresolved controversy in research ethics: the tension between (a) broad, flexible consent that facilitates future scientific uses of biospecimens and (b) specific consent that respects participants' genuine understanding of how their biological materials will be used. The article proposes strategies for improving consent practices for stored biospecimen research, arguing that broad forms — even when technically legal — do not achieve the meaningful, autonomous authorization that ethical research requires.

The justice connection is foundational: the case illustrates how research infrastructures built without community authority can convert an act of community trust into a vehicle for harm, and how existing consent frameworks often lack the cultural sensitivity to protect Indigenous communities whose interests are collective rather than purely individual.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance — Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019

*(external link — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) following a 2018 workshop in Gaborone, Botswana, as a people- and purpose-oriented complement to the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles that have dominated open-science discourse. Where FAIR focuses on the technical properties of data, CARE centers the rights, interests, and self-determination of the people from whom that data originates — particularly Indigenous peoples whose data has historically been extracted, archived, and reused without ongoing community control.

The four principles are:

- **Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should enable Indigenous peoples to derive benefit from data about their communities, territories, and ways of knowing. Research should support community well-being rather than external metrics of "open science."
- **Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights to govern data about themselves and their communities should be recognized and respected at every stage of the data lifecycle — collection, storage, analysis, dissemination, and reuse.
- **Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data bear an affirmative responsibility to support Indigenous data governance, build local capacity, and disclose how data is being used.
- **Ethics:** Indigenous rights and wellbeing must guide all data practices, with attention to collective harms, power asymmetries, and the long-term consequences of data decisions for communities.

CARE directly challenges data practices that treat information concerning Indigenous peoples as an open scientific commons. The framework has been adopted by an expanding range of research funding bodies and aligns closely with the sovereignty-oriented model of the D4I repository. For the course's justice arc, CARE represents one of the most concrete and institutionally actionable proposals for operationalizing community-led justice in biotechnology.
