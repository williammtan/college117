### Reading 1: Anderson MZ et al., "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" — *Nature Genetics*, 2025

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2025 Nature Genetics article describes the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository**, the first federally funded biomedical data repository designed and governed by Indigenous scientists and tribal nations. The project, funded by a $9 million NIH RADx grant awarded to Arizona State University (led by Krystal Tsosie), Stanford University, the Native BioData Consortium, and UC Santa Cruz, responds directly to the long history of extractive genomic research on Indigenous communities.

The D4I repository is built around the principle of **tribal data sovereignty**: participating Tribal Nations retain authority over how data collected from their communities is stored, accessed, and used. The architecture is deliberately federated and decentralized, meaning data does not reside in a single centralized cloud controlled by federal agencies or universities; instead, it is governed by participating tribes according to their own laws and protocols. The system incorporates modern machine learning and AI tooling while keeping tribal governance structures in command.

The article argues that conventional biomedical data infrastructure was never designed with Indigenous communities in mind — frameworks like the NIH data-sharing mandate, which requires broad access to federally funded research data, can inadvertently reproduce colonial dynamics if applied without tribal consent. D4I offers a technical and governance architecture that respects Indigenous data rights while still enabling population-level health research that benefits those communities. Tsosie describes the repository as "a safe harbor" where communities can build datasets, control their own narratives, and compete for research resources on their own terms. The authors connect this to broader justice themes: data sovereignty is not merely legal protection but a mechanism for Indigenous self-determination in biomedical knowledge production.

### Reading 2: Mello MM & Wolf LE, "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" — *NEJM*, 2010

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This 2010 *New England Journal of Medicine* Perspective piece by legal scholars Michelle Mello and Leslie Wolf uses a landmark legal case to dissect a foundational problem in biomedical research ethics: the inadequacy of one-time informed consent for biospecimens stored for future, unrelated studies.

The **Havasupai case** arose from blood samples collected in the 1990s from members of the Havasupai tribe of the Grand Canyon by Arizona State University researchers, ostensibly to study the high rates of Type 2 diabetes in the community. Without additional consent, the samples were subsequently used for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding — topics the tribe found culturally harmful, spiritually threatening, and at odds with their cosmological beliefs about their origins. In 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 to 41 tribal members and returned the biological samples, but the legal outcome left unanswered the key question: **what constitutes adequate informed consent when biospecimens are stored for future use?**

Mello and Wolf argue the case exposes critical gaps in research ethics infrastructure. First, broad consent forms that permit "future research" are insufficient when communities have specific cultural, spiritual, or political objections to particular uses. Second, institutional review boards (IRBs) failed to catch the secondary uses because they were not required to review each new application of already-collected samples. Third, the case illustrates that formal consent processes do not substitute for genuine community engagement and partnership. The authors connect this to justice themes by showing how research institutions — structurally positioned as knowledge gatekeepers — can exploit communities' trust and biological materials in ways that cause concrete cultural and psychosocial harm. The lessons they draw: research institutions must implement community-level engagement, specific re-consent mechanisms for stored samples, and grievance processes that are accessible to research participants and communities.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The **CARE Principles** — developed in 2018 at International Data Week in Gaborone, Botswana, by the Research Data Alliance's International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group and formally published in 2020 — provide a values-based complement to the technical FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR focuses on making data shareable and machine-readable, CARE insists that data governance must also center **people and purpose**, particularly for Indigenous communities whose data has historically been extracted without meaningful consent or reciprocal benefit.

The four CARE principles are:

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data use must generate tangible benefits for Indigenous collectives — through inclusive innovation, improved governance, and equitable outcomes — not only for external researchers or institutions. Data practices should not enrich outsiders at a community's expense.

- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples have the right to determine how their cultures, lands, resources, knowledges, and peoples are represented in data. This includes the right to develop cultural governance protocols and to be active leaders in stewardship of and access to their data. It directly challenges the default assumption that data, once collected, becomes a public or institutional good.

- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data bear obligations: to document how data use supports Indigenous self-determination, to provide resources for Indigenous data capacity, and to ensure that creation, interpretation, and use of data is respectful and does not perpetuate deficit framing.

- **Ethics (E):** Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing are the primary concern at every stage of the data life cycle. Data practices should minimize harm — present and future — and must not stigmatize or misrepresent Indigenous cultures and knowledges.

The CARE Principles connect directly to the course's justice themes by reframing data governance as a site of both injustice and remedy. They challenge biomedical research norms in which data-sharing mandates operate as if Indigenous data were simply raw material, and instead insist on relational, accountability-based frameworks. Alongside the D4I repository and the Havasupai case, CARE illustrates how communities are building institutional and normative infrastructure to convert the abstract principle of sovereignty into concrete protections.
