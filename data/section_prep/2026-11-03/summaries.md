Week 9's lecture on Data Ownership foregrounded Indigenous Nations as a case study in community-led data justice. The session was guest-led by Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), a bioethicist and geneticist whose work sits at the intersection of Indigenous data sovereignty and biomedical research. The core tension of the lecture was between the institutional logic of data sharing — wherein aggregating health data is framed as an unambiguous public good — and the lived reality for Indigenous communities, for whom that same data sharing has historically produced harm through unauthorized secondary uses, misrepresentation of ancestry and origin, and extraction without benefit-return to source communities.

The session situated these harms within the broader Week 9 theme of "Community-Led Justice": what does it look like when communities themselves, rather than outside researchers or institutions, hold governance authority over data? The discussion questions pushed students to examine both the technological challenges of redistributing data storage toward communities (infrastructure, interoperability, cost) and the conceptual risks of "community engagement" as a rhetorical move that can reproduce extractive relationships under a veneer of participation. The three required readings — the D4I Tribal Data Repository paper, the Havasupai case lessons piece, and the CARE Principles — provided a past–present–framework arc for the session.

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11):2614–2619, 2025.

*(external link — summarized from citation + secondary sources: web search results referencing ASU news coverage and PubMed record. Publisher page www.nature.com is outside this session's allowed network domains.)*

This Comment piece in *Nature Genetics* introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository**, described as the first federally funded repository of its kind. The paper's central argument is that biomedical data originating with U.S. tribal nations and Indigenous peoples should be stored and governed under Tribal Nation authority rather than in conventional institutional repositories controlled by universities or federal agencies. The D4I model enables researchers to access Indigenous health data, but only under governance structures set by the contributing tribal communities — a significant inversion of the standard extractive research model.

The piece situates the repository as an institutional operationalization of Indigenous data sovereignty: the principle that Indigenous peoples have the right to govern collection, ownership, and application of data about their communities. The authors draw a direct line from historical harms — such as unauthorized use of genetic samples — to the structural inadequacy of existing consent frameworks, arguing that consent alone is insufficient when data can be repurposed across studies and generations. The D4I model adds governance layers: tribal nations set the terms for data access, and community priorities (health improvement, cultural integrity, economic benefit) are built into the data-sharing agreement architecture rather than left to researchers' discretion.

Connection to course justice themes: the paper advances a structural, not merely procedural, account of justice in research. It moves beyond IRB-level "do no harm" ethics toward affirmative capacity-building and self-determination — aligning with the course's running distinction between corrective and transformative justice.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3):204–207.

*(external link — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Semantic Scholar, SSRN record, and secondary coverage. Publisher page doi.org/NEJM is outside this session's allowed network domains.)*

This Perspective piece in the *New England Journal of Medicine* uses the Havasupai case as a case study in the legal and ethical failures of stored biological sample research. The background: members of the Havasupai tribe donated blood samples to Arizona State University researchers in the 1990s, consenting to diabetes research. They later discovered their samples had been used — without re-consent — for studies of schizophrenia, migration patterns, and theories of tribal origins that directly contradicted tribal oral histories and sovereignty claims. ASU ultimately settled in 2010 for $700,000 to 41 tribal members and returned the samples.

Mello and Wolf's key argument is that existing regulatory frameworks — particularly federal Common Rule consent provisions — created conditions for this harm by treating initial consent as a durable authorization for a broad category of future research. The authors identify the core failures: consent forms were vague about scope; there was no mechanism for tribal-level (as opposed to individual) consent; and researchers had no obligation to return to the community when research purposes evolved. The piece argues for tighter specificity in consent language, community-level governance mechanisms for tribal group data, and clearer limits on secondary use.

Connection to course justice themes: the Havasupai case is a paradigmatic example of participatory injustice — the tribe was treated as a resource pool rather than a partner. The case illustrates how formal procedural compliance (an IRB signed off) can coexist with substantive injustice, a tension central to the course's interrogation of ethics frameworks. It also shows how research harms can be cultural and relational, not only physical — undermining stories of origin has political and identity stakes that standard risk frameworks fail to capture.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Wikipedia, CASRAI, Ada Lovelace Institute, and CODATA Data Science Journal PDF. Publisher page gida-global.org is outside this session's allowed network domains.)*

The CARE Principles were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance and the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (part of the Research Data Alliance), following a 2018 workshop at International Data Week in Gaborone, Botswana. Published in final form in October 2019, CARE is explicitly designed as a complement to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which focus on maximizing data openness. Where FAIR is data-centric and oriented toward accessibility, CARE is **people- and purpose-oriented**, foregrounding who benefits, who governs, and who bears responsibility.

The four principles:

- **Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous Peoples, not just external researchers or institutions, derive value from data about them. This includes economic, health, social, and cultural benefits aligned with community priorities.
- **Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples have the right to govern their own data — including what is collected, how it is stored, who accesses it, and how it is used and interpreted. This is a governance right, not merely a privacy right.
- **Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data have affirmative obligations: to explain how data is used, to support Indigenous data governance capacity, and to nurture respectful relationships with source communities.
- **Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing must guide the entire data lifecycle, from design through archiving. The focus is on minimizing harm and maximizing benefit in ways that align with Indigenous values and frameworks — not solely external bioethics standards.

Connection to course justice themes: CARE operationalizes the structural justice argument at the infrastructure level. It represents a challenge to the technocratic assumption that "open data = good data," insisting instead that openness without governance is just a more efficient form of extraction. The framework maps directly onto the course's communitarian and capabilities-based justice frameworks, asking not just "is this fair?" but "does this enable Indigenous communities to flourish on their own terms?"
