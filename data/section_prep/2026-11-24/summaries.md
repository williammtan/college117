**Session:** May 27, 2026 — Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples)

No lecture slide deck was found for this session. Summary is based on syllabus context and reading themes.

This session, led by guest lecturer Krystal Tsosie of the Native BioData Consortium, centered on data ownership and sovereignty as justice frameworks in the context of Indigenous nations. Drawing on cases from genomics, health research, and tribal governance, the session examined what it means for communities to exercise genuine control over data generated from their members. Key tensions explored include the gap between individual consent models (which allow broad, open-ended data reuse) and collective sovereignty frameworks that require ongoing tribal oversight of how biological and health data are stored, accessed, and analyzed. The session highlighted how historical violations of research trust—such as the Havasupai case—continue to shape Indigenous distrust of academic institutions, and how emerging frameworks like the CARE Principles and tribally governed data repositories offer structural responses. Justice-oriented data governance is framed not merely as procedural protection but as an affirmative claim: Indigenous peoples have an inherent right to benefit from, and exercise authority over, the data their communities generate.

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty"
*Nat Genet. 2025 Nov;57(11):2614–2619*  
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** This paper introduces the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions and Innovation) Tribal Data Repository, a tribally governed infrastructure for storing and managing Indigenous health data with sovereignty as a first principle. The authors argue that existing research data repositories are designed around models of broad consent and open sharing that undermine tribal self-determination. By centering tribal oversight from the technical architecture up—including access control systems that reflect tribal governance structures—the repository offers a model for conducting health research with, not on, Indigenous communities.

**Evidence:** The project was funded through a $9 million NIH RADx award, with almost half of the university teams led by Indigenous faculty scientists. Lead authors Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) and Matt Anderson (Eastern Band of Cherokee descent) bring together genomics, ethics, and informatics expertise. The repository addresses concrete gaps: standard broad consent allows data to be repurposed for studies—including ancestry inference or population genetics—never anticipated by participants. A sovereignty-aligned repository empowers communities to retain veto power and benefit-sharing rights.

**Connection to Course Themes:** This paper directly extends the course's examination of participatory and community-led justice (Weeks 2 and 9). It illustrates how structural interventions in research infrastructure—not merely individual consent forms—are required to realize justice for historically exploited communities. The tribal data repository represents a form of technological sovereignty, countering the colonialist logics embedded in conventional genomic data-sharing models.

### 2. Mello & Wolf — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples"
*New England Journal of Medicine 363, no. 3 (2010): 204–207*  
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** Mello and Wolf use the Havasupai case as a lens for examining what constitutes adequate informed consent when biological samples collected for one purpose are later used for unrelated studies. They propose that the case reveals a fundamental structural failure in consent practices: open-ended biospecimen storage, combined with minimal re-consent requirements, creates conditions for exploitation even when researchers believe they are acting in good faith.

**Evidence:** The Havasupai Tribe originally donated blood samples in the 1990s for diabetes research at Arizona State University. Without tribal knowledge or additional consent, ASU researchers subsequently used the samples for studies on schizophrenia, population migration, and inbreeding—topics the tribe found offensive and that contradicted their cultural and spiritual beliefs about identity and origins. In 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 to 41 tribal members, the return of DNA samples, and tangible community benefits (a tribal clinic and school funding). Because the case settled, no legal precedent was established regarding consent or biospecimen ownership.

**Connection to Course Themes:** This piece is a paradigmatic case study in research justice (Week 4) and community-led justice (Week 9). The harms were not merely individual but collective—stigmatization of the group and violation of a cultural worldview. The case exemplifies how the absence of ongoing, community-level oversight creates vulnerability to research that perpetuates colonial extraction. It also motivates the structural solutions proposed in Anderson et al. (2025) and encoded in the CARE Principles.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** The CARE Principles—Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics—establish a people- and purpose-centered framework for Indigenous data governance that complements the existing FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR focuses on maximizing data sharing and technical interoperability, CARE insists that data practices must also be evaluated for whether they serve Indigenous communities' self-determination and wellbeing. The principles were developed in 2019 by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) and the Research Data Alliance's International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group.

**Evidence:** Each principle addresses a specific dimension of justice:
- **Collective Benefit:** Any value created from Indigenous data should flow back equitably to Indigenous communities and support their aspirations, not primarily enrich outside researchers or institutions.
- **Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their capacity to exercise control over it actively strengthened—not merely acknowledged in principle.
- **Responsibility:** Those working with Indigenous data are accountable for how that data is used; transparency and reciprocity are required throughout the data lifecycle.
- **Ethics:** Indigenous rights and wellbeing must be the primary ethical concern at every stage, from collection to archiving to publication.

**Connection to Course Themes:** The CARE Principles synthesize multiple justice frameworks covered across the course—Rawlsian fairness, participatory justice (Week 2), and community-led sovereignty (Week 9). They represent a concrete governance tool that operationalizes abstract ethical commitments, showing how justice principles can be embedded in data infrastructure policies. In dialogue with the Havasupai case and the Anderson et al. repository paper, the CARE Principles form the normative backbone of a sovereignty-centered alternative to extractive research models.
