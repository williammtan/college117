## Reading Summaries (Previous Session — May 27, 2026)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619, 2025.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** This paper introduces the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded, Indigenous-led data repository designed to operationalize Tribal data sovereignty in practice. The authors argue that including Indigenous communities in mainstream genomic data infrastructure is insufficient; genuine justice requires that Tribal Nations hold binding governance authority over data derived from their members.

**Evidence:** Funded through a $9 million NIH RADx award (with a $1.2 million sub-award to Tsosie for ethical design), D4I employs a federated architecture: individual Tribal Nations may operate their own repositories that interoperate with the D4I system while retaining full control. All data access is subject to approval by advisory committees composed of tribal subject-matter experts with voting power, and operations must conform to each Tribal Nation's own laws and policies. The Native BioData Consortium — a tribal health research nonprofit co-founded by Tsosie — serves as community steward and is described as "a safe harbor" for Indigenous data.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** D4I operationalizes the CARE Principles and directly challenges the extractive research paradigm in which Indigenous health data flows outward to non-Indigenous institutions without community benefit. By embedding sovereignty in technical infrastructure rather than policy aspirations alone, the paper models how community-led justice can be achieved at scale. It responds structurally to harms like the Havasupai case: data access is governed by the communities themselves, not by external review boards that may not represent or understand Indigenous cultural values and priorities.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207, 2010.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** Using the 2004–2010 Havasupai lawsuit against Arizona State University as a case study, this NEJM perspective interrogates what constitutes adequate informed consent for stored biological samples, arguing that standard Western consent paradigms systematically fail Indigenous communities whose cultural values, sovereign political status, and collective interests cannot be captured at the level of individual consent.

**Evidence:** In the early 1990s, Havasupai tribe members donated blood samples for a study on the genetic basis of type 2 diabetes — a disease with devastating prevalence in their community. ASU researchers subsequently used those samples, without re-consent, for studies on schizophrenia, tribal migration patterns, and population inbreeding. All three unauthorized uses touched subjects that are culturally sensitive or stigmatizing in Havasupai tradition: mental illness carries communal shame, and migration data contradicted origin narratives the tribe holds sacred. In 2004 the tribe sued; in April 2010 ASU settled for $700,000, returned the blood samples, provided tribal scholarships, and assisted in securing a federal health clinic. The authors note that the settlement left unresolved the normative question of how broad "future use" consent can justifiably be.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** The Havasupai case is a paradigmatic illustration of how biomedical research infrastructure — including IRB approval systems designed to protect individual subjects — can perpetuate colonial harm at the community level. Biospecimens carry cultural and collective significance that outstrips individual consent: a tribe member's DNA encodes group history, migration, and vulnerability in ways that affect the whole community. The case has since catalyzed calls for community-level consent, tribal data sovereignty frameworks, and institutional accountability mechanisms — precisely the structures the CARE Principles and D4I repository attempt to provide.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key Argument:** The CARE Principles provide a people- and purpose-centered framework for Indigenous data governance, designed to complement the technical FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) with an ethical and political layer that centers Indigenous self-determination. Where FAIR asks how data can be shared efficiently, CARE asks on whose terms, for whose benefit, and under whose authority.

**Evidence:** Developed at a 2018 International Data Week workshop in Gaborone, Botswana by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (a working group of the Research Data Alliance), CARE formalizes four principles:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data use must produce tangible, equitable outcomes for Indigenous communities, not only for external researchers or institutions. This includes inclusion in innovation, improved governance capacity, and equitable economic participation.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Consistent with the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), Indigenous Peoples hold rights to govern data about their communities, territories, and resources. Data governance must actively support Indigenous nations' right to make their own decisions.
- **R — Responsibility:** Non-Indigenous researchers and institutions working with Indigenous data have an affirmative obligation to share how data are used, to support Indigenous self-determination, and to be accountable for harm.
- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous rights and wellbeing must be the primary consideration at every stage of the data life cycle — collection, storage, analysis, archiving, and reuse — rather than afterthoughts addressed by compliance checklists.

**Connection to Justice Themes:** CARE reframes data governance from a technical challenge into a justice problem: the question is not only "how do we share data safely?" but "who has authority over data, for what purposes, and with what accountability?" In the course's framework, CARE represents a structural answer to participatory injustice — it does not ask Indigenous communities to "participate" in externally designed systems, but demands that those systems be rebuilt under Indigenous governance. The framework has been adopted by national data infrastructure bodies and shaped genomic governance policy, demonstrating that justice frameworks can scale into institutional practice and structural change.
