## Readings Due (Previous Session: 2026-05-27)

### 1. Anderson et al. (2025) — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty"
*Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-025-02382-0

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Nature Genetics abstract, EurekaAlert NIH release, ASU news coverage, d4itdr.org, Wikipedia entry for Krystal Tsosie)*

This paper introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository** — described as the first federally funded Tribal data repository — as a structural response to longstanding failures of data governance in health research involving Indigenous peoples. Funded through NIH's Other Transaction Authority with an initial $9 million award, the D4I TDR is built on de-identified data collected under the RADx-UP COVID-19 initiative and is explicitly designed to remain under **Tribal Nation governance** rather than institutional or federal control.

The paper's central argument is that existing data infrastructure for health research systematically excludes Indigenous communities from control over their own health data, concentrating benefits at academic and federal institutions while communities bear the risks and burdens. The D4I model counters this by embedding Indigenous sovereignty at every layer of repository design: a federated architecture allows individual Tribal nations with their own repositories to interoperate while retaining full local governance authority; external advisory committees composed of tribal subject-matter experts — including voting panels — review and approve all data-access requests; and the governance framework was co-developed with tribal-nation attorneys to align repository operations with tribal law.

The connection to COLLEGE 117's justice themes is direct: this is a real-world infrastructure project that translates abstract data-sovereignty principles (CARE) into enforceable institutional mechanisms. It asks who controls the knowledge generated from a community's bodies and lives, and insists the answer must be the community itself. The paper also models what responsible collaboration between academic institutions (ASU, Duke Clinical Research Institute) and Indigenous governance bodies looks like when sovereignty — not mere consultation — is the starting point.

### 2. Mello & Wolf (2010) — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples"
*New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1005203

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: NEJM abstract, Semantic Scholar, GSU Law reading room, Georgetown repository, Wikipedia: Havasupai Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents, NPR, secondary academic commentary)*

This NEJM Perspective piece uses the 2010 settlement of *Havasupai Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents* as a lens for examining the inadequacy of existing informed-consent frameworks when biospecimens collected for one purpose are later repurposed for unrelated research. The Havasupai case began in 1989–1994, when tribe members donated blood samples specifically to help researchers investigate the unusually high rates of type 2 diabetes in their community. When no genetic link to diabetes was found, ASU researchers used the stored samples — without re-consent and without the tribe's knowledge — in studies of schizophrenia, inbreeding, and population migration patterns. A tribal member discovered this at an academic lecture in 2003. The lawsuit, filed in 2004, resulted in a $700,000 settlement to 41 tribe members and the physical return of their DNA samples to the tribe, but established no legal precedent.

Mello and Wolf's key argument is that the case exposed a structural gap: existing consent forms and IRB protocols were designed for a narrow research transaction, not for the long-term stewardship of living tissue that could be repurposed across decades. They propose that researchers must move toward consent frameworks that are **ongoing, layered, and community-informed** — distinguishing between consent for initial collection and consent for future use, and recognizing that for Indigenous communities, the relevant unit of consent may be the community or nation, not only the individual. The piece also addresses strategies for storing samples in ways that keep future researchers accountable to original consent terms.

For COLLEGE 117, the Havasupai case is paradigmatic: it shows how research infrastructure that appears race-neutral (anyone can donate blood for health research) can enact profound injustice by ignoring the specific historical, cultural, and political contexts of Indigenous communities. Migration-pattern research using Havasupai DNA implicitly challenged tribal oral histories of their origins — an epistemic harm independent of any physical risk.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance
*Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.*  
https://www.gida-global.org/s/CAREPrinciples_OnePagersFINAL_Oct_17_2019.pdf

*(external link, paywalled/inaccessible — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Carroll et al. 2020, Data Science Journal DOI 10.5334/dsj-2020-043; Imperial College London summary; University of Auckland summary; Harvard tagTeam; d4itdr.org governance documentation)*

The CARE Principles — **Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics** — were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance to address a fundamental limitation of the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR optimizes data sharing across institutions, it treats data as a technical object largely severed from the people and communities it describes; in doing so, FAIR can actively reproduce colonial power dynamics when applied to Indigenous data. The CARE Principles are people- and purpose-oriented, asking not only *how* data is shared but *for whom* and *under whose authority*.

**Collective Benefit** holds that any value derived from Indigenous data must flow back to Indigenous communities equitably and in alignment with their own wellbeing goals — not primarily to academic careers, institutional rankings, or state surveillance. **Authority to Control** asserts that Indigenous peoples and governing bodies must have the power to determine how their peoples, lands, resources, and knowledge systems are represented in data — effectively recognizing data sovereignty as an extension of political sovereignty. **Responsibility** requires those who work with Indigenous data to demonstrate, through openly available evidence, how their work advances Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit. **Ethics** directs that throughout the data lifecycle, governance must be guided by ethical frameworks rooted in the values of the relevant Indigenous community rather than by external or universal standards — with explicit attention to potential future harms.

The CARE principles connect directly to the course's justice themes by arguing that procedural justice (following IRB rules, obtaining individual consent) is insufficient when the underlying data infrastructure is itself structured to extract rather than empower. They provide the theoretical foundation for the D4I repository and for broader calls, like those in the Anderson et al. paper, to restructure research institutions around Indigenous authority rather than Indigenous access.
