### 1. Anderson MZ et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article introduces the first federally funded Tribal data repository — the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (D4I TDR)** — built by a coalition of Indigenous academics and participating Tribal Nations. The central argument is that existing genomic and health research databases fail Indigenous communities because they were designed without Tribal governance, consent protocols, or benefit-return mechanisms. The D4I TDR reorients data management by embedding rigorous **Tribal Nation governance**: data are held under each nation's own protocols rather than aggregated under federal or university ownership, and community data-sharing interests are explicitly prioritized over researcher convenience.

The authors position this infrastructure as an enactment of **Indigenous data sovereignty** — the right of Indigenous Peoples to own, control, access, and possess data about their communities. Grounded in the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics), the D4I TDR shifts the framing from extraction-model research (in which Indigenous communities contribute samples but derive little benefit) toward **community-driven innovation**: participating Nations help shape the research questions, determine who can access data, and receive results in forms usable for their own health planning. The article connects directly to the course's core justice theme by demonstrating that data infrastructure itself is a site of justice struggle — controlling the repository is inseparable from controlling the narrative of Indigenous health. The work of co-authors including Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) emphasizes that meaningful participation requires structural, not merely consultative, power.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This *NEJM Perspective* examines the landmark settlement in which Arizona State University paid $700,000 to 41 members of the Havasupai Tribe after researchers used blood samples — originally collected in the 1990s with consent limited to type 2 diabetes research — for additional genetic studies (including schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding) without re-consent. The case came to light when tribal member Carletta Tilousi discovered the broader uses.

Mello and Wolf's core argument is that the case exposes a structural gap in research ethics: **informed consent frameworks designed for discrete studies fail when biospecimens are stored and repurposed over time**, especially for Indigenous communities whose group identity and cultural integrity can be implicated by population-level genetic findings. Three tensions animate the article: (1) individual versus group consent (DNA can reveal tribal history, not just individual genetics); (2) the limits of broad/blanket consent versus the impracticality of re-contacting donors for every new study; and (3) the asymmetry of benefit — universities and researchers gain knowledge and prestige while communities bear risk with little return. The authors propose enhanced governance frameworks requiring community-level consultation for Indigenous samples, not just individual IRB review. In the justice context of this course, the case is paradigmatic: it illustrates how standard procedural ethics (IRB approval, documented consent) can be formally satisfied while substantive justice — respect for community self-determination — is violated.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (2019). Global Indigenous Data Alliance.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles were developed in 2019 by the **International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group** (within the Research Data Alliance) to address the inadequacy of purely data-centric standards like FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) for Indigenous data contexts. Where FAIR focuses on data properties, CARE is **people- and purpose-oriented**, rooted in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.

**C — Collective Benefit:** Data governance should produce tangible benefits for Indigenous collectives — inclusive innovation, improved community decision-making, and equitable outcomes — rather than extracting value for external researchers or institutions.

**A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples hold the right to govern how their cultures, lands, resources, knowledges, and identities are represented in data. This includes rights to access data relevant to their worldviews, to develop cultural governance protocols, and to lead the stewardship of Indigenous data and knowledge.

**R — Responsibility:** Those working with Indigenous data have an affirmative obligation to support Indigenous self-determination: providing resources to generate Indigenous-controlled data, building an Indigenous data workforce, and ensuring that creation, interpretation, and use of data is conducted respectfully and in consultation with communities.

**E — Ethics:** Data stewardship must prioritize the cultural, ethical, and social dimensions of communities — safeguarding well-being, respecting local values, and protecting vulnerable groups from harms that may arise from data use.

**Connection to course:** The CARE Principles operationalize the justice themes from throughout the course at the infrastructure level. They challenge the liberal individualist framing of informed consent (Mello & Wolf reading) by insisting on collective rights; they underpin the D4I TDR (Anderson et al.) as its governance backbone; and they speak directly to the course's sustained attention to who controls knowledge production about marginalized communities. In the Indigenous data context, justice is not procedural compliance but structural change in who owns, governs, and benefits from data.
