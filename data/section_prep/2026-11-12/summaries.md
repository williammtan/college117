## Readings Due (Previous Session: May 27, 2026)

### Reading 1: Anderson MZ et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" *(Nature Genetics, 2025)*

(external link, network-restricted — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [ASU search profile](https://search.asu.edu/profile/535281), [PubMed listing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41094212/), [Science News coverage](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/genetics-krystal-tsosie-indigenous-data-sovereignty))

This 2025 Nature Genetics article (vol. 57, pp. 2614–2619, DOI 10.1038/s41588-025-02382-0) introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository, described as the first federally funded repository purpose-built for Tribal Nation governance of biomedical data. Lead author Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) and a large multidisciplinary team of Indigenous researchers and community partners argue that existing data infrastructure — designed around FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) and university stewardship — is structurally inadequate for honoring Indigenous data sovereignty. The key argument is that data sovereignty is not achievable through consent forms alone; it requires Tribal-controlled physical and institutional infrastructure. The D4I repository operationalizes this by situating governance authority in Tribal Nations: researchers who seek to access Indigenous community data must operate under community-determined data-sharing protocols rather than unilaterally setting their own access conditions.

The evidence marshaled is partly historical (the persistent pattern of data extraction from Indigenous communities, including the Havasupai case discussed in Reading 2) and partly constructive: the architecture of the D4I system itself — with Tribal data governance boards, community-benefit requirements, and opt-in data sharing — demonstrates that an alternative infrastructure is feasible. The connection to the course's justice themes is direct: data sovereignty is framed not merely as a privacy interest but as a structural requirement for health justice. Unless Tribal Nations control the terms of data use, genomic and biomedical research will continue to generate knowledge that benefits outside institutions while leaving Indigenous communities without reciprocal benefit or meaningful autonomy.

### Reading 2: Mello MM & Wolf LE — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

(external link, network-restricted — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1626200), [IRB study PMC 3786163](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3786163/), and contextual knowledge)

This 2010 NEJM Perspective (363(3): 204–207, DOI 10.1056/NEJMp1005203) by Michelle Mello and Leslie Wolf dissects the most consequential bioethics case involving American Indian research participants. In the early 1990s, members of the Havasupai Tribe (Grand Canyon, Arizona) donated blood samples to Arizona State University researchers for a study on diabetes — a devastating health problem in their community. Without tribal knowledge or consent, those samples were subsequently used for unrelated research on schizophrenia, inbreeding patterns, and population migration history. When tribe members discovered this in 2004, they sued. Arizona State University ultimately settled for approximately $700,000 distributed among 41 plaintiffs, returned the DNA samples, and committed funds for a clinic and school.

Mello and Wolf draw several lessons for research ethics and law. First, broad consent language for "diabetes and related conditions" does not authorize researchers to pursue entirely different research questions; consent must be specific to the research purpose. Second, secondary use of stored biological samples — however scientifically valuable — requires renewed consent from communities, not merely individuals, particularly when the community itself may be identifiable. Third, the assumption that de-identification protects small, geographically bounded communities like the Havasupai is factually wrong: a sample set of 400 people cannot be meaningfully anonymized. Fourth, IRBs need clearer guidance on community-level harms, not just individual-level risks. The case connects directly to the course's core question of who is harmed by biotechnological research: Indigenous communities donated biological material in trust and experienced epistemic, spiritual, and political harm when that trust was violated.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)*

(external link, network-restricted — summarized from citation + secondary sources: [CODATA Data Science Journal listing](https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043), [VU Amsterdam RDM overview](https://rdm.vu.nl/topics/care-principles.html), and contextual knowledge)

The CARE Principles were developed by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group within the Research Data Alliance, in consultation with Indigenous scholars, non-profit organizations, and governments, and published in 2019. They are designed as a complement to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which focus on the technical properties of data but are silent on who controls data, who benefits, and who is harmed. CARE explicitly centers people and purpose.

The four principles:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Indigenous data ecosystems must enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit from their data. This encompasses inclusive development and innovation (data supports Indigenous-defined priorities), improved governance and citizen engagement (data enables Indigenous communities to self-govern effectively), and equitable outcomes (benefits flow back to communities, not only to external researchers or companies).

- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples hold rights over data pertaining to their communities, territories, and ways of knowing. Recognizing these rights strengthens Indigenous communities' authority to govern and control data about them, moving from a model of "access granted by researchers" to one of "access permitted by communities."

- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data bear reciprocal obligations — to share how data are used, to build community data literacy and capacity, and to ensure that research benefits outweigh burdens. Accountability runs toward communities, not only toward academic institutions or funders.

- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing are the primary concern at every stage of the data life cycle, from collection to archiving and reuse. Data governance frameworks should minimize harm, maximize benefit, and treat Indigenous data holders as rights-bearers rather than research subjects.

The CARE Principles connect directly to the course's justice themes by providing a normative vocabulary for evaluating whether any biomedical data practice respects Indigenous sovereignty. Applied to genomics and AI health technologies, they reveal the inadequacy of purely technical compliance (e.g., anonymization, IRB approval) as a substitute for genuine community authority.
