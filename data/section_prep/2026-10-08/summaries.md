### Reading 1: Anderson et al. (2025). "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics*, 57(11), 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ELSIhub, ASU News, UCSC Campus News, Fronteras Desk, Native BioData Consortium)*

This paper presents the design and rationale of the **RADx Tribal Data Repository (TDR)**, a first-of-its-kind Indigenous-led genomic and health data repository funded by a $9 million NIH award. The project unites six collaborative research institutions — with Stanford University as prime awardee, alongside Arizona State University, the Native BioData Consortium, Ohio State University, UC Santa Cruz, University of Washington, and UW-Madison — under the leadership of Indigenous scientists including co-author **Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation)**, who led a $1.2 million ethics sub-award.

The paper's central argument is that Indigenous communities must not merely participate in genomic research but exercise **genuine sovereignty** over their own ancient, genetic, environmental, and social data. The repository operationalizes this through tribal-controlled governance structures, ensuring that data collected from Indigenous communities stays under Indigenous authority rather than flowing into federal or commercial databases on external terms.

The **connection to course justice themes** is direct: the paper challenges the extractive model of biomedical research where Indigenous populations provide biological data but receive minimal benefit and no governance rights. It positions data sovereignty as a precondition for participatory justice (Week 2), not just a technical design choice. The authors link genomic self-determination to the broader CARE Principles framework, demonstrating how institutional infrastructure — not just ethical aspiration — is required to move from principle to practice.

### Reading 2: Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine*, 363(3), 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: GSU Law Repository, Stanford Law Publications, UMD DRUM Repository)*

This NEJM perspective piece examines the landmark 2010 settlement in which Arizona State University paid $700,000 to 41 Havasupai tribe members after university researchers used blood samples — donated for diabetes research in the 1990s — for a range of additional genetic studies the tribe never consented to, including research on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding. The case reached a $700,000 settlement in 2010 and stands as one of the most consequential informed-consent controversies in the history of biomedical research with Indigenous peoples.

Mello and Wolf's **key argument** is that the case exposes an unresolved foundational question: does consent given for one study extend to future, possibly unrelated research on stored samples? The settlement closed a painful chapter for both parties without answering that question, leaving what the authors call "a bedeviling question for genetic research." The paper documents the **competing values** at stake — scientific progress, community autonomy, data stewardship, and trust — and proposes policy strategies for navigating them, centering the importance of understanding the study population's perspectives before designing consent processes.

For this course, the case illustrates how the formal apparatus of research ethics (IRBs, consent forms) can be manipulated or interpreted in ways that violate substantive justice for communities with limited institutional power. It connects directly to Week 4 (Research & Justice) and to the broader arc of Week 9: the Havasupai case is precisely what the CARE Principles and tribal data repositories were designed to prevent from recurring.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (GIDA, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from publisher PDF citation + secondary sources: Wikipedia, CASRAI, Ada Lovelace Institute, ARDC)*

Released by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)** in September 2019, the CARE Principles provide a governance framework ensuring that Indigenous Peoples are included in — and in control of — data ecosystems that affect their lives. The acronym stands for four principles:

1. **Collective Benefit**: Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous Peoples can derive meaningful value from the data, through inclusive development, better governance and fairer outcomes. Data should serve community priorities, not just external scientific agendas.

2. **Authority to Control**: Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognized, and their power to control that data must be actively empowered — not just acknowledged in principle. This means governance structures, not just stated intentions.

3. **Responsibility**: Those who work with Indigenous data are accountable for being transparent about how those data are used and for nurturing relationships that advance Indigenous well-being.

4. **Ethics**: Indigenous rights and well-being should be the primary concern throughout the data life cycle, including minimization of harm, maximizing benefit, and respecting Indigenous values and knowledge systems.

The CARE Principles were drafted at an Indigenous Data Sovereignty workshop in Gaborone, Botswana (November 2018), grounded in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. They are designed to complement — not replace — the **FAIR Principles** (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) for open data: where FAIR addresses ease of access and reuse, CARE addresses *for whom* that access operates and on whose terms.

For this course, CARE represents one of the most actionable justice frameworks covered all quarter: it translates abstract principles of participatory justice (Week 2), data sovereignty (Week 7), and community-led justice (Week 9) into concrete institutional commitments that can be audited and enforced.
