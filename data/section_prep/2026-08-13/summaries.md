## Reading Summaries

### Reading 1 — Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619, 2025.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources including ASU News coverage and Nature Genetics abstract)*

This Nature Genetics article introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository** — described as the first federally funded Tribal data repository — and makes the case that it represents a new governance paradigm for Indigenous biomedical data. The central argument is that existing data infrastructure, even well-intentioned open-access frameworks, reproduces colonial dynamics by severing Indigenous data from the communities that generated it. The D4I repository is designed to invert this structure: researchers may access data from Indigenous groups, but only under rigorous Tribal Nation governance frameworks that honor each community's laws, policies, and interests.

The paper is led by Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo), whose framing characterizes the repository as a "safe harbor" — a space where communities can build datasets, control data narratives, and compete for research resources without ceding sovereignty to institutions. Anderson and co-authors detail how the repository embeds governance at the technical level, ensuring that data-sharing agreements reflect community self-determination rather than researcher convenience. The paper also situates this intervention against the historical record of Indigenous biospecimen misuse, explicitly invoking cases such as the Havasupai lawsuit in which DNA collected for diabetes research was repurposed for stigmatized studies on schizophrenia and inbreeding without tribal consent.

For the course's justice framework, this paper exemplifies the community-led justice model: rather than asking institutions to behave more ethically toward passive communities, it envisions Indigenous nations as active data stewards capable of setting the terms of research participation. This shifts the unit of ethical analysis from the individual research subject to the sovereign community — a meaningful conceptual advance with practical implications for how genomic and health AI research should be governed globally.

### Reading 2 — Mello, M.M., & Wolf, L.E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources including SSRN abstract, Stanford Law School publication record, and Seeds of Native Health coverage)*

This NEJM Perspective piece by bioethicist-lawyer Michelle Mello and public health law scholar Leslie Wolf uses the 2010 Arizona State University settlement with 41 Havasupai tribal members as a lens for analyzing a fundamental unresolved problem in biomedical research: what constitutes adequate informed consent when biological samples may be stored and used in future studies beyond their original scope?

The factual core: Havasupai members in Arizona donated blood samples in the 1990s for ASU-led studies on Type II diabetes, a pressing health concern for the community. Without meaningful disclosure or consent, researchers subsequently used the samples for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding — topics the tribe found culturally offensive and potentially harmful to their political and legal interests. ASU settled for $700,000 in April 2010 and returned the samples. Mello and Wolf argue the case illuminates "clashing values" at the heart of biospecimen research: the scientific interest in broad, longitudinal data reuse versus the community's right to define the terms of its own participation.

Their central lesson is that current broad-consent frameworks — often framed as researcher-friendly efficiency tools — may systematically fail populations with distinct cultural understandings of identity, ancestry, and the spiritual significance of biological material. The authors call for more culturally responsive consent processes that explain secondary use possibilities in concrete, comprehensible terms, and advocate for ongoing community engagement rather than one-time sign-off.

Connected to COLLEGE 117's justice themes, this case is a paradigm example of how research infrastructure designed without attention to power and cultural context perpetuates harm — even in the absence of malicious intent. It also illustrates why the CARE Principles and Tribal data repositories (Reading 1) are necessary structural responses, not merely best-practice recommendations.

### Reading 3 — CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from GIDA website description, Data Science Journal 2020 article abstract, and secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles — an acronym for **Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics** — were published in 2019 by members of the Global Indigenous Data Alliance as a complement to the widely adopted FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR focuses on technical standards for data sharing, CARE focuses on the people and communities whose data are at stake.

**Collective Benefit** asserts that data ecosystems must be designed and operated in ways that generate benefits for Indigenous communities, not merely for external researchers or institutions. Data should be able to create value that aligns with Indigenous worldviews and the knowledge economy on Indigenous terms.

**Authority to Control** recognizes Indigenous Peoples' inherent rights — grounded in political self-determination — to govern data about themselves, their territories, and their cultural heritage. This includes the right to grant, withhold, or condition access.

**Responsibility** places an obligation on those who work with Indigenous data to share how data are used, actively support Indigenous data governance capacities, and nurture respectful relationships with the communities whose data they handle.

**Ethics** requires that the creation, interpretation, and use of Indigenous data uphold Indigenous peoples' dignity, minimize harm, and reflect Indigenous values and rights rather than imposing external ethical frameworks.

Together, the CARE Principles shift the ethical frame from data-as-resource (to be made accessible to the research community) to data-as-relationship (embedded in community governance, reciprocity, and self-determination). This aligns with the course's argument that justice in biotechnology requires structural change — not only individual ethical behavior — and directly grounds the D4I Tribal data repository (Reading 1) in normative principle. The principles have been adopted by research councils and data standards bodies globally and increasingly shape policy conversations about AI governance in health contexts.
