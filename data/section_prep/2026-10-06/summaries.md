### Reading 1: Anderson et al. (2025) — A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ASU News, NIH press releases, Fronteras Desk, PubMed abstract)*

Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, Peters CL, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11):2614–2619 (2025). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-025-02382-0

This article introduces the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository**, the first federally funded repository specifically designed to house and govern biomedical data originating from U.S. Tribal Nations and Indigenous peoples. The repository was built through a $9 million NIH RADx grant spanning six institutions, with Krystal Tsosie (Arizona State University, Diné/Navajo Nation) as lead researcher and the Native BioData Consortium as overall project leader.

The **key argument** is that standard biomedical data infrastructure was built without Indigenous peoples in mind and therefore cannot be trusted to protect tribal sovereignty over genetic and health data. Rather than a centralized gatekeeping model, D4I uses federated and decentralized access approaches, embedding Tribal Nation governance directly into data-sharing agreements, analysis pipelines, and usage standards. This architecture allows communities to benefit from modern tools (including machine learning and AI) while retaining control over what data leaves their communities and under what conditions.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This article embodies the course's core question of *who benefits and who is left behind*. Indigenous communities have historically been research subjects — providing data — without receiving proportionate health benefits or maintaining control over how their genetic data is used. D4I attempts to rebalance this by treating data sovereignty as a prerequisite for justice, not an afterthought. It applies participatory justice principles from earlier in the course (cf. Benjamin's "real utopias") to the genomic data context, demonstrating that community-led structures can be built into research infrastructure itself.

### Reading 2: Mello & Wolf (2010) — The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources: NEJM abstract, Wikipedia, PMC, Semantic Scholar)*

Mello MM, Wolf LE. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3):204–207 (2010). https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1005203

This *Perspective* piece in the *NEJM* analyzes a landmark legal case in research ethics. In 1989–1990, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from members of the Havasupai Tribe, a small Indigenous nation in the Grand Canyon, ostensibly to study the tribe's high rates of type 2 diabetes. The samples were subsequently used — without tribal members' knowledge or consent — for research on schizophrenia, inbreeding, and population migration patterns, the last of which contradicted tribal origin stories that have deep cultural and spiritual significance. In 2010, ASU agreed to pay $700,000 to 41 tribe members to settle the resulting lawsuit.

Mello and Wolf use the case to examine the central ethical question: **what constitutes adequate informed consent for biospecimens that may be stored and reused in future, unrelated studies?** They argue that standard generic consent forms fail when research involves culturally sensitive data, when study populations hold collective rather than individual notions of risk and harm, and when downstream uses could stigmatize a community. The case also exposed how IRB oversight mechanisms, designed primarily around individual autonomy, can systematically miss the collective harms that matter most to Indigenous communities.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This case directly illustrates the research justice concerns raised throughout the course — particularly the failures of standard biomedical frameworks to account for collective rights and community-level harms. It foreshadows the justice arguments for data sovereignty structures like D4I (Reading 1) and the CARE Principles (Reading 3), showing through a concrete historical example why Indigenous communities have strong grounds for demanding governance control over their biological data.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (2019)

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources: GIDA/CODATA publication, Ada Lovelace Institute, Imperial College Open Access blog, CASRAI)*

"CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance." Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019. https://www.gida-global.org/s/CAREPrinciples_OnePagersFINAL_Oct_17_2019.pdf  
Peer-reviewed version: Carroll SR et al. *Data Science Journal* 19(1):43 (2020). https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043

The CARE Principles were developed by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (within the Research Data Alliance), in consultation with Indigenous scholars, communities, and governments, and published by GIDA in October 2019. They were designed as a **people- and purpose-oriented counterpart** to the existing FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which govern open data but say nothing about *who* should control data or *who* should benefit from it.

The four CARE principles are:
- **Collective Benefit** — Data ecosystems must enable Indigenous Peoples to derive tangible benefit from data about them, whether for governance, cultural revitalization, or economic development.
- **Authority to Control** — Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in data must be recognized; their authority to govern, access, and make decisions about such data must be respected, not merely consulted.
- **Responsibility** — Those who work with Indigenous data take on active responsibility for ensuring that data use supports Indigenous Peoples' self-determination and does not cause harm.
- **Ethics** — Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle — from collection through analysis, storage, and reuse.

**Connection to course justice themes:** CARE operationalizes the justice frameworks discussed throughout the course into concrete governance standards. While Rawlsian or utilitarian frameworks may ask whether a research benefit is *distributed fairly*, CARE asks the prior question: *who has the authority to make that determination in the first place?* The principles move sovereignty from an abstract ideal to a practical governance checklist, directly answering the course's discussion questions about what community data stewardship means and what technological and institutional conditions enable or undermine it.
