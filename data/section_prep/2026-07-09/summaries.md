## Reading Summaries (Week 9: Community-Led Justice)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" *(Nat Genet, 2025)*

(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)

This paper introduces the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository**, the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States, supported by a $9 million NIH RADx award. The repository was co-developed and is led by Krystal Tsosie (Arizona State University) and colleagues at the Native BioData Consortium (NativeBio), the first Indigenous-led biobank operating under the legal jurisdiction of a Tribal Nation (Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation).

The central argument is that existing biomedical data infrastructure systematically excludes Indigenous communities from meaningful governance of their own biological and health data. The D4I repository offers a structural counter-model: a "safe harbor" where federally funded biomedical data originating from U.S. tribal nations is held under rigorous Tribal Nation governance rather than university or federal authority alone. Data access is federated and decentralized, enabling modern analytic tools (including machine learning) while preserving tribal control over who accesses data, for what purpose, and under what conditions consistent with tribal law and policy.

The paper is significant for the course's justice themes because it enacts a shift from treating Indigenous communities as *subjects* of health research to recognizing them as *stewards* and *sovereigns* of their own data. It directly addresses the extractive patterns that led to cases like the Havasupai scandal, proposing governance architecture — not merely ethics guidelines — as the remedy. The authors operationalize CARE Principles within a funded, real-world institution, demonstrating that Indigenous data sovereignty is both a justice aspiration and a technically achievable design choice. The paper also underscores that health disparities cannot be addressed without data equity: excluding Indigenous populations from biomedical databases is itself a form of structural injustice.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)

This New England Journal of Medicine perspective piece analyzes a landmark breach of research ethics involving the Havasupai Tribe of northern Arizona. Beginning in 1989, ASU researchers collected blood samples from Havasupai members with consent stated for type 2 diabetes research — a disease disproportionately affecting the community. Without additional consent, the samples were subsequently used for studies on schizophrenia, ethnic migration patterns, and population inbreeding — topics that are culturally sensitive or taboo in Havasupai culture, and that the community would have refused. When tribe member Carletta Tilousi discovered the unauthorized uses in 2003, litigation followed; in 2010 ASU settled for $700,000 paid to 41 tribal members and — most meaningfully to the tribe — returned the blood samples, which hold sacred status under Havasupai beliefs.

Mello and Wolf's key argument is that the case exposes a fundamental flaw in how biomedical research handles stored biological samples: broad, vague consent forms (here, permitting "behavioral/medical problems" research) can appear to authorize uses that participants never contemplated or would have refused. The problem is compounded when consent forms are administered in a second language (English for many Havasupai members) and when researchers treat biological material as institutional property rather than something belonging to the source community. The authors call for tiered/dynamic consent models, stronger IRB oversight of secondary data use, and community-specific protections.

In the course's justice framework, the case illustrates how institutional structures of research — consent regimes, property law applied to biospecimens, lack of community governance — can operationalize injustice even when individual researchers do not intend harm. The Havasupai case became a watershed that shaped federal regulations (the 2018 Revised Common Rule) and galvanized Indigenous scientists and communities to demand data sovereignty rather than simply procedural consent improvements.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(GIDA, 2019)*

(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources; full principles document available at gida-global.org)

The CARE Principles — **Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics** — were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in 2019 and published in scholarly form by Carroll, Hudson, Kukutai, and colleagues in the *Data Science Journal* (2020). They were designed as a *people-and-purpose* complement to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which had been criticized for prioritizing openness in ways that harm Indigenous communities whose data is already subject to extraction and misuse.

Each of the four principles addresses a distinct dimension of data justice:

- **Collective Benefit**: Data ecosystems must produce tangible, equitable outcomes for Indigenous peoples themselves — not only for external researchers. This includes inclusive economic development, improved community governance, and equitable sharing of any value generated.
- **Authority to Control**: Indigenous peoples have the inherent right to govern collection and use of data about themselves, their territories, and their cultures — at the individual, community, and nation level. This is data sovereignty in its strongest sense.
- **Responsibility**: Those who work with Indigenous data bear positive obligations: to nurture respectful relationships, support Indigenous data capacities, share back results in usable forms, and actively enable self-determination rather than merely avoiding harm.
- **Ethics**: Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary consideration across the entire data lifecycle — from collection through storage, analysis, and dissemination — including cultural integrity, minimization of harm, and recognition of Indigenous intellectual property.

CARE's key intervention for the course is conceptual: it shifts the default question from "can these data be made open?" to "who governs these data and for whose benefit?" This reframes data governance as a justice issue, connecting directly to the course's inquiry into who benefits and who is left behind by biotechnology. CARE is not merely a set of procedural guidelines; it is a framework for redistributing epistemic and political authority over health knowledge production.
