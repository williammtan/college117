This lecture, delivered by Dr. Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), brought the course's justice arc to bear on the question of data ownership in Indigenous communities. The session examined how centuries of extractive research practices—collecting biological samples, genomic data, and community knowledge with minimal reciprocity or community control—have generated lasting harms to Indigenous peoples, from privacy violations to the reinforcement of colonial power structures. Dr. Tsosie situated data sovereignty as an expression of Tribal Nation political sovereignty: just as nations control territory, Indigenous peoples have the right to govern the collection, curation, and use of data *about* them. The session drew on concrete case studies (the Havasupai blood-sample scandal and the emerging DI3 Tribal Data Repository) to show both the failure modes of standard research ethics and what a sovereignty-centered alternative looks like. Key themes included the limits of individual informed consent when community rights are at stake, the CARE Principles as a governance framework, and the question of how institutions can build trust with communities they have historically harmed.

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al., "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This paper reports the design and launch of the **DI3 Tribal Data Repository** (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations), described as the first federally funded repository of this kind. Funded by a $9 million NIH RADx award, the project was catalysed by the disproportionate impact of COVID-19 on Indigenous populations—a stark illustration of how data gaps rooted in exclusionary research infrastructure translate directly into health disparities. The repository's central innovation is governance: rather than following the dominant model in which universities or federal agencies hold and control data, DI3 situates authority with Tribal Nations as sovereign entities. Community members can opt in or out of specific downstream research projects, and data sharing occurs under tribal governance protocols rather than standard IRB frameworks.

The paper's key argument is that Indigenous health equity cannot be achieved by simply diversifying participation in existing research pipelines; it requires rebuilding the infrastructure of data collection, curation, and access around the principle of community self-determination. The authors draw directly on the CARE Principles and the history of abuses (including the Havasupai case) to make the case that trust is a precondition of valid data—and trust must be earned through structural, not merely procedural, change. The connection to COLLEGE 117's justice themes is direct: the paper operationalizes participatory justice (Week 2) and Indigenous data sovereignty (Week 9) in a concrete institutional form, showing how research infrastructure can either entrench or redress systemic inequity.

### 2. Mello, M. M. & Wolf, L. E., "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *NEJM* 363(3): 204–207 (2010)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This perspective piece unpacks one of the most consequential bioethics cases in U.S. research history. Between 1990 and 1994, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 members of the Havasupai tribe, ostensibly for research on Type II diabetes—a disease with devastating prevalence in the community. Participants signed broad consent forms authorizing research on "behavioral/medical disorders," but most understood their contribution as diabetes-specific. Without further consent, the samples were subsequently used in studies of schizophrenia, inbreeding, and prehistoric human migration—topics the Havasupai considered deeply offensive violations of tribal cultural beliefs about their origins. When a tribal member discovered the unauthorized studies in 2003, the tribe filed suit; in 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 and agreed to return the samples.

Mello and Wolf identify two structural failures: first, that overly broad consent language obscured the true scope of research, exploiting a power imbalance between researchers and a community with limited formal education and English literacy; second, that existing institutional review frameworks treat individuals as the unit of consent, missing the community-level interests that are salient for Indigenous peoples. The lessons they propose—specific consent for each research use, genuine engagement with community values, and mechanisms for sample return—anticipate the governance principles in the CARE framework and the DI3 repository. For COLLEGE 117, the case concretely illustrates how formal consent can coexist with substantive injustice, and why procedural ethics must be supplemented by structural accountability.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (GIDA, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Developed in 2019 by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance at a workshop in Gaborone, Botswana, the **CARE Principles** are a governance framework designed to complement the technically-oriented FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) with an explicitly people- and purpose-centred logic. Where FAIR focuses on maximising data openness and interoperability for the research community, CARE insists that openness without community control is a vehicle for exploitation, not equity.

The four principles:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems must be designed so that Indigenous Peoples can actually derive benefit from data about them. Benefit is defined by communities, not researchers; it includes economic benefit, governance capacity, and cultural self-determination.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples possess the right to govern data about their peoples, territories, and resources. This means institutions must actively empower community leadership in data stewardship rather than treating Indigenous participation as advisory.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data are responsible for ensuring its use supports Indigenous self-determination. This extends to building Indigenous data-science capacity and ensuring that data creation, interpretation, and use are done respectfully and in genuine partnership.
- **E — Ethics:** Data governance must maintain ethical standards that prioritize Indigenous wellbeing across the entire data lifecycle—from collection to archiving. Data should not portray Indigenous peoples, cultures, or knowledge systems in terms of deficit, and should be governed by norms derived from Indigenous ethical frameworks, not only Western IRB standards.

The document is short (a one-pager per principle), but its implications are far-reaching: it reframes data governance as a justice issue, connecting it to the course's broader arc from Rawlsian fairness (Week 1) through participatory justice (Week 2) to community-led sovereignty (Week 9).
