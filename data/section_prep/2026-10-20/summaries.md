## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson et al. (2025) — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty"

*Nature Genetics, 57(11): 2614–2619. DOI: 10.1038/s41588-025-02382-0*
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository—the first federally funded repository of this kind, built on a $9 million NIH award announced in December 2023. The project was led by the Native BioData Consortium (NativeBio), an Indigenous-led 501(c)(3) nonprofit biodata repository operating within the legal jurisdiction of the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation, in partnership with Stanford, Arizona State University, Ohio State University, UC Santa Cruz, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison.

The paper's central argument is that Indigenous communities must govern health data about themselves. The motivating context is the COVID-19 pandemic: data collected from tribal members by federal programs ended up under federal control without meaningful tribal governance, replicating a long history of data extraction. The repository is designed to give Tribal Nations control over who accesses data, under what conditions, and for what purposes—centering sovereignty at the infrastructure level rather than treating it as an afterthought.

The governance model draws on years of tribal consultation (starting in May 2020), and the ethical framework for data sharing was led by Krystal Tsosie (ASU), one of the same authors who appears as a guest lecturer in this session. Key structural features include data stored within Tribal Nation jurisdiction and access policies governed by community-defined protocols.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This reading connects directly to participatory justice (communities shaping the research infrastructure that affects them), distributive justice (ensuring Indigenous communities receive benefit from their own data), and self-determination as a prerequisite for equitable biotechnology. It offers a constructive model—data sovereignty as an institutional form—after weeks spent diagnosing harm.

### 2. Mello & Wolf (2010) — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples"

*New England Journal of Medicine, 363(3): 204–207. DOI: 10.1056/NEJMp1005203*
*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective article examines a landmark case in research ethics: the misuse of blood samples collected from approximately 400 members of the Havasupai tribe. In 1989, Havasupai leaders invited Arizona State University researchers to study the tribe's high rates of Type 2 diabetes. Tribal members gave blood under the understanding that it would be used for diabetes research. Without the tribe's knowledge or consent, ASU researchers subsequently used the samples for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding—topics with significant cultural and political sensitivity for the community. The misuse came to light in 2003 when a tribal member attended a doctoral defense presenting migration research built on the blood samples. In April 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 paid to 41 tribe members and agreed to return all remaining blood samples.

Mello and Wolf use the case to analyze the unresolved controversy over informed consent for biospecimen storage and future research use. The consent forms were written broadly and in English at a time when many tribal members had not completed high school and English was a second language; oral consent for diabetes research was obtained, but the written form's language covered far more. The authors argue that "the written form is only one part of the consent process"—it records agreement but does not exhaust it—and that researchers must understand the community's values and perspectives when designing consent procedures.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The case illustrates how standard research ethics frameworks—built around individual informed consent—fail Indigenous communities whose identities, cultural narratives, and political sovereignty are implicated at the group level, not just the individual. It also illustrates how participation in biomedical research has historically extracted value from marginalized communities while leaving them without recourse or benefit, a recurring justice failure the course tracks across genomics, AI, and neurotechnologies.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (GIDA, 2019)

*Global Indigenous Data Alliance. Published September 2019.*
*(external link, paywalled/access-restricted — summarized from citation + secondary sources; full one-pagers available at gida-global.org/care)*

The CARE Principles were formally articulated by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in September 2019, with their most-cited scholarly formulation published by Carroll et al. in *Data Science Journal* (2020). The principles were developed in direct response to the limitations of open-data norms like the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable): while FAIR improves data infrastructure, it was designed without accounting for the power differentials and historical contexts that determine whether data sharing benefits or harms Indigenous communities.

The four CARE principles are:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit from data. This includes inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and citizen engagement, and equitable outcomes.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognized and their authority to control such data be empowered. This means acknowledging Indigenous data governance structures and supporting Indigenous peoples' data capabilities.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those working with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how those data are used to support Indigenous Peoples' self-determination and collective benefit. This includes nurturing respectful relationships with the peoples and lands from which the data originate.
- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle and across the data ecosystem.

**Connection to course justice themes:** CARE operationalizes justice as institutional practice. Where earlier readings diagnosed exploitation (the Havasupai case) and proposed infrastructure (the D4I repository), CARE offers the normative vocabulary for evaluating both. It connects directly to the course's recurring examination of who benefits from biotechnology data ecosystems, centering the answer in community self-determination and collective wellbeing rather than individual researcher autonomy.
