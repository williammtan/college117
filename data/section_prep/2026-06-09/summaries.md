## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al., "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" — *Nature Genetics*, 2025

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ASU News, EurekAlert!, UCSC Genomics Institute coverage)*

This article introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States, supported by a $9 million NIH RADx award. Led by Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) and Matthew Anderson (Eastern Band of Cherokee descent), along with a broad team of Indigenous faculty and Tribal Nation partners, the repository is designed as a "safe harbor" for biomedical data originating from U.S. Tribal Nations and Indigenous peoples. The central argument is that existing federal data repositories operate under broad or blanket consent frameworks that allow indefinite reuse of community-contributed data far from the communities themselves — a structural form of data extraction. The D4I repository counters this by embedding rigorous Tribal Nation governance from the ground up: participating Tribes control data access, define permissible uses, and retain stewardship authority. The paper situates this model within the context of COVID-19, during which Indigenous communities suffered disproportionate health burdens while contributing data to research without commensurate benefit. By centering Indigenous data sovereignty technically and ethically — nearly half of the university team leads are Indigenous faculty scientists — the repository operationalizes principles like CARE into actionable infrastructure. The authors argue this model is replicable and necessary for addressing health disparities and epistemic injustice simultaneously, demonstrating that just data governance is not merely an ethical aspiration but an achievable institutional design.

### 2. Mello, MM & Wolf, LE, "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" — *NEJM*, 2010

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: AMA Journal of Ethics, Stanford Law School blog, Genomic Justice PMC article)*

This NEJM Perspective uses the landmark settlement between the Havasupai Tribe and Arizona State University (ASU) as a springboard for reforming informed consent practices in genetic research. Between 1990 and 1994, ASU researchers collected blood samples from ~400 Havasupai members ostensibly to study the genetic basis of type 2 diabetes. In 2003, a tribe member discovered the samples had been repurposed for studies on schizophrenia, evolutionary migration, and inbreeding — research the Tribe found profoundly offensive to their cosmology and communal identity. In April 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 and agreed to return all remaining samples. Mello and Wolf identify the core failure as the use of deliberately vague broad consent language that research institutions rely on to maximize future flexibility at the expense of participant understanding. They argue this model is structurally inadequate for communities — especially Indigenous communities — whose relationship to biological materials is collective and culturally embedded rather than individually transactional. The article's key recommendations include: specificity in consent forms (naming anticipated secondary uses), ongoing notification of participants when new uses are contemplated, and mandatory community consultation when research involves culturally sensitive populations. In the course's justice framework, the Havasupai case exemplifies how procedural injustice (inadequate consent) enables substantive injustice (violation of cultural sovereignty). The case has become a touchstone for discussions of research ethics, genomic justice, and the limits of individual-consent frameworks when the affected unit is a community.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (GIDA, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled/gated PDF — summarized from citation + secondary sources: CODATA Data Science Journal, ARDC, Ada Lovelace Institute, Wikipedia)*

The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics — were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in 2018–2019 as a deliberately people- and purpose-oriented counterpart to the data-technical FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). The core argument is that FAIR data standards, while valuable, are agnostic about power: they say nothing about who should control data, who should benefit, or how data ecosystems should be aligned with the rights of the communities from whom data originate. CARE corrects this deficit.

- **Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems must generate tangible benefits for Indigenous collectives — through inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and citizen engagement, and equitable outcomes that extend beyond the research community.
- **Authority to Control:** Grounded in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), this principle holds that Indigenous Peoples must determine data governance protocols and be actively involved in stewardship decisions over Indigenous data held by other entities.
- **Responsibility:** Researchers and institutions working with Indigenous data have an ongoing responsibility to nurture respectful relationships, build community data capacity, and embed data practices within Indigenous languages and cultural frameworks.
- **Ethics:** Indigenous rights and wellbeing must be foregrounded across the entire data lifecycle — minimizing harm, maximizing benefits, promoting justice, and enabling future use aligned with community values.

In the course's justice context, CARE represents an attempt to encode participatory and epistemic justice directly into data infrastructure standards. It challenges the assumption that open science is inherently equitable and insists that sovereignty — not merely access — is the relevant metric for just data governance.
