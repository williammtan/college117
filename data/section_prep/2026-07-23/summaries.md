## Readings Due — May 27, 2026 (Week 9: Community-Led Justice)

### Reading 1: Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57, no. 11 (2025): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: web search results including NIH announcement, UCSC Genomics Institute release, ASU News, and d4itdr.org)*

This article introduces the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository**, described as the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. Funded by a $9 million NIH RADx-UP award to a Stanford–Native BioData Consortium partnership (with UC Santa Cruz as sub-awardee), D4I was created to address the urgent need for a sovereignty-aligned infrastructure for Indigenous health data—needs that became especially visible during COVID-19. Krystal Tsosie leads the ethical design component, grounding the repository explicitly in the **CARE Principles** (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics).

The repository's key innovation is governance: participating Tribal Nations retain control over who accesses their data, for what purposes, and under what conditions. Data are not made available by default to outside researchers; instead, tribes set the terms—a direct inversion of the extractive model in which academia collects Indigenous biospecimens and decides downstream uses without community oversight. The authors argue that sovereignty-aligned repositories enable communities to "take control over their own data stories and data narratives" while simultaneously enabling them to generate health insights that standard (non-Indigenous) biobanks systematically lack.

The article connects directly to the course's justice themes: existing research and data infrastructures are not neutral—they encode power relations. Building infrastructure that enacts community authority, rather than only promising it in consent forms, is a structural prerequisite for any meaningful form of health justice for Indigenous peoples.

### Reading 2: Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363, no. 3: 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: web search results including FindLaw, Stanford Law School blog, Wikipedia, NCAI case study, and PMC commentary)*

This NEJM Perspective documents and analyzes a landmark bioethics case that became a touchstone for discussions of Indigenous research ethics and the limits of informed consent. Between **1990 and 1994**, researchers at Arizona State University collected blood samples from approximately 400 members of the Havasupai tribe—a small Grand Canyon community with very high rates of type 2 diabetes—ostensibly to study the genetic basis of the disease. Tribal members understood and consented to diabetes research.

Subsequently, however, the samples were used—without re-consent—for studies on **schizophrenia, human migration, and inbreeding**. These topics were not merely scientifically off-topic; they were culturally taboo and directly conflicted with the Havasupai's own cosmological and historical self-understanding (migration studies, for instance, challenged their belief in their ancient, continuous presence in the Grand Canyon). When tribal member Carletta Tilousi discovered the unauthorized uses in 2003, the tribe sued. A 2010 settlement awarded **$700,000** to 41 tribal members, along with funds for a community school and clinic and return of the DNA samples.

Mello and Wolf identify the central unresolved question: *What constitutes adequate informed consent for stored biospecimens that may be used in future, unrelated studies?* Their lessons include: (1) consent forms must be far more specific about possible secondary uses; (2) researchers must take seriously the perspectives and values of study populations—especially communities with historically grounded reasons to distrust Western science; and (3) existing oversight mechanisms (IRBs) are insufficient for protecting communally held interests. For this course, the case is emblematic: biotechnology development routinely produces benefits for some (genetic knowledge, publications, careers) while leaving study populations—often already marginalized communities—bearing all the risk with little control or benefit.

### Reading 3: CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), October 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: web search results including CODATA Data Science Journal publication, Ada Lovelace Institute, ARDC, and Nature Scientific Data)*

Published in 2019 by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)**, the CARE Principles articulate a framework for Indigenous data governance that complements the widely used FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR focuses on technical data quality and openness, CARE asks a prior question: **open to whom, for whose benefit, and on whose terms?**

The four CARE principles are:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous Peoples—not only external researchers or institutions—can derive tangible benefit from data about them, their lands, and their knowledge systems.
- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their authority to control access and use of that data must be actively empowered, not merely acknowledged in policy statements.
- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data have an ongoing responsibility to disclose how those data are used, and to support the capacity of Indigenous communities to engage meaningfully with data governance.
- **E — Ethics:** All uses of Indigenous data must uphold the dignity of Indigenous nations and communities; relationships must be built on respect, reciprocity, trust, and mutual understanding—as defined by the communities themselves, not by outside researchers or institutions.

The CARE framework is explicitly **people- and purpose-oriented**, in contrast to FAIR's data-centric orientation. It has been adopted or referenced by institutions, governments, and journals worldwide. Its connection to the course's justice themes is direct: CARE operationalizes the idea that justice in biotechnology requires shifting who holds power over data—from researchers and institutions toward the communities those data represent. Without CARE-like principles, data sovereignty remains rhetorical rather than structural.
