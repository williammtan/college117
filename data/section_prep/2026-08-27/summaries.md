This session brought a guest lecture from Dr. Krystal Tsosie, an Indigenous bioethicist and geneticist at ASU who co-founded the Native BioData Consortium — the first Indigenous-led biological data repository operating within U.S. Tribal jurisdictional bounds. The lecture situated data ownership within Week 9's broader Community-Led Justice theme. The lecture introduced concepts of Tribal data sovereignty — the right of Indigenous nations to govern collection, access, and use of data about their members and territories — and examined how emerging infrastructure (federated repositories, Tribal IRBs, sovereignty-preserving data-sharing agreements) can operationalize this right. The session set up the readings (Anderson et al.; Mello & Wolf; CARE Principles) as concrete instantiations of these problems and emergent solutions.

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty" *(Nat Genet, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This Nature Genetics perspective (2025) describes the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository, the first federally funded data repository built under rigorous Tribal Nation governance. The core argument is that existing research infrastructure concentrates control over Indigenous health and genomic data within universities and federal agencies, perpetuating extractive research relationships that have characterized genomic science since its inception. D4I inverts this model: participating Tribes and Indigenous individuals retain ownership of and access to their own data, with researchers required to engage under governance structures determined by the communities themselves.

The paper's key evidence is the design and initial deployment of D4I, which grew out of NIH-funded work during the COVID-19 pandemic and was built by a coalition of Indigenous academics and Tribal nations. The governance architecture enforces community data-sharing interests at every layer — access controls, data-use agreements, and benefit-sharing — rather than treating sovereignty as a bureaucratic checkbox. The article explicitly situates D4I as a response to decades of violations, including the Havasupai case, in which sample collection for diabetes research became unauthorized studies on schizophrenia, migration, and inbreeding.

**Connection to justice themes:** The paper exemplifies the course's participatory and community-led justice frameworks: justice is not achieved by researchers acting on behalf of communities, but by communities holding structural authority over research affecting them. It also illustrates the limits of individual informed consent as the sole protector against harm — collective, institutional sovereignty is required.

### 2. Mello, MM & Wolf, LE — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM perspective analyzes a landmark 2010 settlement in which Arizona State University paid $700,000 to 41 Havasupai tribal members whose blood samples — donated in the 1990s for Type 2 diabetes research — were subsequently used for unauthorized studies on schizophrenia, population migration, and inbreeding. Mello and Wolf's central argument is that the case exposes a structural flaw in how biospecimen consent is typically obtained: individual consent forms authorize collection for a stated purpose but do not clearly delineate what happens to samples and data afterward, particularly across secondary studies. The Havasupai participants believed they had consented to diabetes research only; the researchers interpreted their consent as extending to other uses.

The authors review the ethical and legal dimensions — the absence of a clear legal precedent established by the settlement notwithstanding — and argue for prospective reform: consent forms should be disease-specific and use-limited; institutional review boards must scrutinize secondary-use requests rather than treating existing consent as blanket authorization; and communities (not just individuals) should have standing to negotiate data-use terms, especially when research touches group identity, ancestry, or cultural belief.

**Connection to justice themes:** The case is a paradigmatic example of how biomedical research's tendency to treat data as a neutral technical resource erases the social and cultural embeddedness of biological material. For Indigenous communities, research on ancestry and migration can contradict sacred origin beliefs, producing cultural harm that conventional research ethics frameworks — oriented around individual privacy — are poorly equipped to recognize.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(GIDA, 2019)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from publisher URL + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance in consultation with Indigenous scholars, governments, and communities, and presented as a companion framework to FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles. While FAIR emphasizes technical interoperability and open access, CARE argues that these goals can be actively harmful when applied to Indigenous data without attention to power, sovereignty, and cultural context.

**Collective Benefit** holds that data ecosystems must enable Indigenous peoples — not only outside researchers — to derive tangible benefit from data about them; institutions must actively facilitate Indigenous-led data use. **Authority to Control** recognizes Indigenous peoples' right to govern how their cultures, lands, knowledges, and identities are represented in data, including the right to determine access and the right to restrict it. **Responsibility** calls on researchers and institutions to nurture respectful, capacity-building relationships rather than extractive ones. **Ethics** requires that data practices prioritize Indigenous rights and self-determination, with Indigenous ethical standards — not only Western IRB review — governing what is permissible.

**Connection to justice themes:** The CARE Principles operationalize the participatory and community-led justice frameworks running throughout the course. They reframe data governance from a technical problem (how to share data efficiently) to a political one (who has the authority to decide whether and how data is shared). In doing so, they challenge the tacit assumption that openness and accessibility are inherently just — a direct critique of how "open science" can reproduce colonial knowledge extraction.
