## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" *(Nature Genetics, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ASU News; Science News; Nature Genetics abstract)*

This 2025 *Nature Genetics* perspectives piece introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded repository of its kind, backed by $9 million in NIH funding and led by ASU professor Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation). The article's central argument is that existing data infrastructure systematically excludes Indigenous communities from control over their own health data, perpetuating a colonial dynamic in which biological specimens and genomic information flow out to researchers and institutions while benefits rarely flow back. The D4I repository is offered as a structural remedy: a "safe harbor" where tribal nations govern access under their own laws and policies, researchers engage with Indigenous data under rigorous Tribal Nation oversight, and communities can build longitudinal datasets that reflect their own health priorities and narratives.

The paper's evidence draws on decades of documented harms from unconsented secondary use of Indigenous genetic data — harms that the authors argue existing IRB frameworks and FAIR data principles alone cannot address. The repository is designed to be sovereignty-aligned from the technical architecture up, enabling communities to control not just access but the interpretive framing ("data stories") of their information. 

In the context of the course's justice themes, the D4I repository operationalizes participatory justice (Week 2), community-led governance (Week 9), and the challenge of building just research infrastructures globally (Week 4). It moves beyond mere consent reforms to assert that Indigenous nations possess inherent data sovereignty and that federal funding should support, not undercut, that sovereignty.

### 2. Mello, M.M. & Wolf, L.E. — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: NEJM abstract; SSRN; Stanford Law School blog; PMC commentary; Wikipedia; Semantic Scholar)*

This 2010 *NEJM* Perspectives piece uses the Havasupai settlement as a lens to examine foundational weaknesses in the ethics of biospecimen research. In the early 1990s, more than 100 members of the Havasupai tribe — a small community of approximately 650 people with unusually high rates of Type 2 diabetes — donated blood samples to Arizona State University researchers under a consent form authorizing "study of the causes of behavioral/medical disorders." In 2003, a tribal member discovered that the samples had been used for at least a dozen unrelated studies on schizophrenia, population inbreeding, and migration patterns — topics deeply taboo in Havasupai culture and never disclosed to participants. After six years of litigation, ASU settled in 2010 for $700,000 paid to 41 tribal members; the samples were returned and ceremonially buried.

Mello and Wolf argue that the case exposes three structural failures: (1) overly broad consent language that is unintelligible to participants who understand their donation as purpose-specific; (2) IRB processes that permit secondary use of stored samples without re-consent; and (3) a misalignment between the individualistic model of informed consent and the collective, relational values of Indigenous communities. Their proposed reforms include tighter, purpose-specific consent; mandatory re-contact when research purposes change materially; and community-level consultation for studies involving tribal populations.

For the course, this reading grounds abstract justice frameworks in a concrete case of research injustice. It demonstrates how institutional structures (IRBs, consent forms, federal research regulations) can enable harm to marginalized communities despite legal compliance — a recurring theme connecting Weeks 3, 4, and 9.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)*

*(external link, paywalled/restricted PDF — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Wikipedia; CODATA Data Science Journal; ARDC; Carroll et al. 2020)*

The CARE Principles were developed in 2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group, a working group within the Research Data Alliance, and formalized by Stephanie Russo Carroll and colleagues in the *Data Science Journal* (2020). They were created explicitly to complement the technical FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) by addressing the people and purpose dimensions that FAIR leaves unspecified — dimensions that matter acutely for Indigenous communities with histories of exploitative research.

CARE is an acronym for four principles:
- **Collective Benefit**: Data ecosystems must be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous peoples to derive benefit from their data — through inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and citizen engagement, and equitable outcomes.
- **Authority to Control**: Indigenous peoples have the right to govern the collection, ownership, and application of data about their communities, territories, and peoples.
- **Responsibility**: Those who work with Indigenous data take on a responsibility to share how those data are used in ways that support Indigenous peoples' self-determination and collective benefit.
- **Ethics**: Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle, from collection through analysis and publication, foregrounding Indigenous worldviews and minimizing harm.

The CARE Principles reject the premise that "open" access is inherently good, arguing that unrestricted data openness can reproduce extractive colonial relationships. Together with FAIR, they provide a dual framework: technical accessibility paired with ethical accountability to communities. For the course, CARE directly enacts the justice frameworks of Weeks 1–3 (ethical frameworks, participatory justice, social determinants) at the level of data infrastructure, connecting the abstract language of justice to practical governance tools that Indigenous communities are implementing now.
