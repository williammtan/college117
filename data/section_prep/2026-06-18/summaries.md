## Previous Lecture: Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples) — 2026-05-27

> **Note:** No lecture deck was found for this session in the repository. The following summary is reconstructed from the syllabus context and the session's required readings.

The May 27th lecture, guest-taught by Krystal Tsosie, PhD, MPH, MA (Diné/Navajo Nation), focused on data ownership as a form of community-led justice, with Indigenous Nations as the central case study. The session asked students to examine what it means for a community to exercise sovereignty and stewardship over its own data — particularly genomic and health data collected through biomedical research. Drawing on examples from tribal governance, the lecture explored how structural inequities in biomedical research have historically allowed outside researchers to collect Indigenous biological samples and then repurpose them for studies far beyond their originally consented scope, causing material and cultural harm. The session introduced the concept of Indigenous Data Sovereignty — the right of Indigenous peoples to control data about themselves, their territories, and their nations — and asked how emerging frameworks and technical architectures can make that sovereignty real rather than merely aspirational. Guest lecturer Tsosie connected her own scholarship (the D4I Tribal Data Repository) to the historical harms illustrated by the Havasupai case, and to the normative scaffolding provided by the CARE Principles. Core justice themes: participatory justice, community control over knowledge production, and the structural conditions that render informed consent meaningful or hollow.

## Readings — Previous Session (2026-05-27)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article presents the first federally funded Tribal data repository designed specifically to operationalize Indigenous data sovereignty in biomedical research. Known as the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository, it was funded with $9 million from the NIH's RADx initiative and led by an Indigenous scientific team — including Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation) and Matt Anderson (Eastern Band of Cherokee) — across six collaborating institutions including Stanford, ASU, Native BioData Consortium, UCSC, University of Washington, and University of Wisconsin.

The repository's core innovation is architectural and political, not merely technical: it centers Tribal Nation governance frameworks, ensuring that data originating from U.S. tribal nations and Indigenous peoples are housed and governed according to tribal laws and policies rather than federal or institutional defaults. Rather than asking tribes to adapt to existing data infrastructure, the D4I repository builds the infrastructure around tribal sovereignty. The project is paired with community-accessible explainer videos translating technical and governance concepts into approachable learning modules, and its adoption approach is deliberate — described as "finishing the house before welcoming guests."

**Course justice connection:** The D4I repository is a concrete institutional response to the power asymmetries that have historically characterized genomic research with Indigenous communities. It demonstrates how participatory and community-led justice can be embedded into technical and legal architecture, not just stated as aspiration. It directly counters the pattern exposed by cases like the Havasupai — where data collected for one purpose was repurposed without community consent — by making community data control the starting condition rather than an afterthought.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207 (2010).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This NEJM Perspective analyzes one of the most consequential informed-consent failures in modern biomedical research. Beginning in 1989, researchers from Arizona State University partnered with the Havasupai Tribe — a small Native American community in the Grand Canyon with high rates of Type II diabetes — to collect blood samples for genetic association research. Tribe members signed a broad consent form permitting study of "the causes of behavioral/medical disorders." When diabetes-gene searches came up empty, researchers repurposed the stored biological specimens for studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding — topics the tribe considered culturally taboo — without any additional consent. A tribe member discovered the unauthorized use in 2003 at an ASU lecture, leading to a landmark lawsuit. In 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 paid to 41 tribe members, the return of all biological samples, and additional community benefits including scholarships and funding assistance for a health clinic.

Mello and Wolf use the case to interrogate what "adequate informed consent" means when biospecimens are stored for potentially indefinite future use. They argue that generic broad-scope consent forms are insufficient when research communities have specific cultural, spiritual, or political interests in how their biological material may be used. The piece calls for consent to be understood as an ongoing process rather than a one-time signature, and for researchers to actively investigate the values and concerns of study populations before designing consent procedures.

**Course justice connection:** The Havasupai case is a paradigm example of participatory justice failure: a marginalized community's biological material was used to produce knowledge that violated their values, without their meaningful participation in defining the research agenda. It also illustrates structural inequities — the consent form was written in English for a community where English was often a second language, and most signatories had not completed high school. The case anchors broader debates about data ownership, secondary use, and who controls the stories genomic data tells about communities.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Developed in 2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group within the Research Data Alliance, and published by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), the CARE Principles provide a governance framework designed to make Indigenous data self-determination operational. The acronym stands for **Collective Benefit**, **Authority to Control**, **Responsibility**, and **Ethics**.

- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data governance should produce tangible outcomes for Indigenous collectives, including inclusive development and innovation, improved governance and citizen engagement, and equitable outcomes. Data must flow back into the communities from which it originates.
- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples must have recognised rights to control, govern, and make decisions about data relating to themselves, their lands, and their nations. This includes rights to participate in data governance processes at all stages.
- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data bear an active responsibility to share how those data are being used in ways that support Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit, not merely to avoid harm.
- **Ethics (E):** Data must be collected and used in ways that align with Indigenous ethical frameworks and with rights affirmed in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP).

The CARE Principles were designed explicitly to complement the FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) that govern open data movements — arguing that openness without Indigenous authority to control is a new form of extraction.

**Course justice connection:** CARE provides the normative vocabulary for the entire session's theme of community-led justice. Where FAIR emphasizes data utility for the scientific community, CARE insists on Indigenous peoples' right not only to benefit from data but to govern it. The framework directly addresses the power asymmetry at the heart of the course: who decides what research gets done, with whose materials, toward whose ends. It offers a structured alternative to extractive research paradigms and connects directly to the D4I repository as a technical implementation of CARE-aligned governance.
