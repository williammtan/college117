## Readings Due (Session: May 27, 2026)

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" *(Nature Genetics, 2025)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ASU news coverage, PubMed abstract, Science News profile of Tsosie, and search results from jglobal.jst.go.jp)*

**Key argument:** Anderson and Tsosie describe the D4I (Data for Indigenous) Tribal Data Repository — the first federally funded data repository governed directly by tribal nations — as a structural solution to the persistent failure of mainstream biomedical infrastructure to protect Indigenous data sovereignty. Rather than asking tribes to entrust their genomic and health data to university- or federal-controlled databases, D4I inverts the governance model: Tribal Nations set the terms under which researchers access data, and tribal law takes precedence over federal data-sharing defaults.

**Evidence and design:** The repository uses federated, decentralized architecture so no single administrator holds a master key over all tribal data. Community data-sharing preferences are encoded as governance requirements at the point of access, not as optional annotations. The article describes the system as a "safe harbor" aligned with tribal sovereignty — explicitly designed so that data collected under federal research grants remains under tribal stewardship rather than reverting to university control at grant close-out.

**Connection to course themes:** This piece represents the affirmative case for community-led justice: it does not merely critique the existing system but proposes an institution that embeds the CARE principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) into technical infrastructure. It answers the course's recurring question — "who benefits, and who is left behind?" — by re-engineering the answer: communities that historically supplied data but received few benefits become the decision-makers about future benefit flows.

### 2. Mello MM & Wolf LE — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(NEJM, 2010)*

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Stanford Law repository listing, GSU Law repository, Turtle Talk blog coverage of the case, and search results)*

**Key argument:** Mello and Wolf use the Havasupai settlement as a lens for examining a fundamental unresolved problem in research ethics: standard informed consent frameworks, designed around individual autonomy and discrete studies, cannot adequately protect communities whose cultural identity and collective interests are implicated in the downstream uses of stored biospecimens. The authors argue that consent must be understood not as a one-time transaction but as an ongoing relationship between researchers and community.

**Evidence:** The Havasupai tribe provided blood samples to Arizona State University researchers in the 1990s to study the genetic basis of diabetes, which was devastating the community. Without community knowledge or additional consent, the same samples were later used for studies on schizophrenia, population migration, and theories of inbreeding — research the tribe found culturally offensive and harmful to their collective self-understanding. When the tribe discovered this in 2003, they sued; ASU settled in April 2010 for $700,000 to 41 members. The legal resolution, however, left the underlying ethical question open: existing consent law treated the reuse as permissible because a blanket form had been signed.

**Connection to course themes:** The case is a pivot point in the course's argument about participatory justice and research ethics. It shows that procedural consent alone cannot substitute for genuine engagement with the study population's values, and that Western individualist consent frameworks impose epistemic injustice on communities whose identity is collective. The lesson points forward to the CARE Principles and tribal data repositories as structural corrections to the framework exposed as inadequate here.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(GIDA, 2019 — One-Pager PDF)*

*(external link, paywalled/access-restricted — summarized from citation + secondary sources: Wikipedia article on CARE Principles, CASRAI explainers, Ada Lovelace Institute analysis, and GIDA organizational materials)*

**Key argument:** The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics — articulate a people- and purpose-oriented framework for Indigenous data governance, explicitly designed to complement the data-oriented FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) while correcting FAIR's silence on power and rights. Where FAIR governs how data move and connect technically, CARE governs the relationships and rights that determine whether, and on whose terms, data move at all.

**The four principles:**
- **Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit from the data. This includes data for governance, data that supports community aspirations, and equitable distribution of benefits across communities.
- **Authority to Control:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognized, and their authority to control such data must be affirmed and supported. This encompasses data about their territories, lands, resources, knowledge, and cultural practices.
- **Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how those data are used to support Indigenous Peoples' self-determination and collective benefit. Accountability requires proactive measures and demonstrated ethical obligations.
- **Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data life cycle. Minimizing harm, maximizing benefit, and obtaining free, prior and informed consent are minimum standards; the framework draws on the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.

**Connection to course themes:** CARE functions as the normative scaffold underlying both the Havasupai analysis and the D4I repository. It translates the course's justice frameworks — Rawlsian fairness, participatory justice, data justice — into operational principles for data governance, demonstrating that justice in biotechnology requires not only equitable distribution of benefits but Indigenous authority over the very knowledge systems that generate those benefits.
