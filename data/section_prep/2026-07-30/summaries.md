### 1. Anderson et al. (2025) — A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty

**Citation:** Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, Peters CL, Trinidad SB, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57, no. 11 (2025): 2614–2619. <https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-025-02382-0>

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary search results)*

This Nature Genetics perspective describes the creation of the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations Tribal Data Repository (Di³TDR)** — the first federally funded Tribal data repository specifically designed to advance Indigenous health research under Indigenous governance. The article's central argument is that existing biomedical data infrastructure systematically disempowers Indigenous communities by placing control of sensitive genomic and health data with academic and government institutions, perpetuating extractive research practices that extract biological materials and data without meaningful community governance or reciprocal benefit.

The repository model reverses this paradigm by centering **Tribal Nation governance**: researchers must engage with Indigenous data under frameworks established by and for Indigenous communities, rather than having data governance dictated by non-Indigenous institutions. The paper builds on Krystal Tsosie's broader advocacy for Indigenous data sovereignty and on the Native BioData Consortium — the first Indigenous-led biorepository operating within U.S. Tribal jurisdictional bounds — as proof that community-controlled infrastructure is feasible. Key evidence includes the documented history of Indigenous genetic data being repurposed without consent for studies far beyond original agreements (a pattern exemplified by the Havasupai case). The repository architecture encodes community data-sharing preferences structurally, not as an afterthought.

**Connection to course justice themes:** This article operationalizes data justice and Indigenous sovereignty at the infrastructure level. It applies participatory justice (Benjamin's *People's Science*) by making Indigenous communities architects — not merely subjects — of the research enterprise. It responds directly to the data ownership and surveillance concerns from Weeks 7–9 by proposing a structural alternative rather than simply cataloguing harms.

### 2. Mello & Wolf (2010) — The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case

**Citation:** Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363, no. 3 (2010): 204–207. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1005203>

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from NEJM abstract, case records, and secondary sources)*

This NEJM perspective uses a landmark legal settlement to examine critical failures in the governance of biological samples in research. Beginning in the late 1980s, members of the Havasupai Tribe of Arizona donated blood samples to Arizona State University researchers specifically for diabetes research — a disease their community urgently needed to understand. Researchers subsequently used those samples without new consent for studies on schizophrenia, evolutionary migration history, and population inbreeding — topics the tribe found not only outside the scope of their consent but culturally offensive and harmful. In 2010, the Arizona Board of Regents settled for $700,000 distributed to 41 tribal members, one of the largest research-ethics settlements of its kind.

Mello and Wolf extract three core lessons. First, **informed consent must explicitly specify permissible future uses** of biospecimens: broad, vague consent language enables "scope creep" that violates community trust even when technically legal. Second, **Institutional Review Boards must rigorously scrutinize proposals for secondary use** of stored samples, especially when the population is Indigenous or otherwise vulnerable. Third, **tribal authority over research involving tribal members must be structurally respected** — not as professional courtesy but as a matter of sovereignty. The case reveals the gap between formal legal compliance and genuine ethical consent, and the particular vulnerability of communities that have historically been subjected to research exploitation.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The Havasupai case is a paradigmatic illustration of research injustice (Week 4) and the ways that formally neutral scientific infrastructure — biospecimen banking, IRB review, consent forms — can function as tools of extraction and harm against marginalized communities. It also illustrates the limits of individual consent frameworks when the community as a collective is the affected party — a problem the CARE Principles directly address.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (2019)

**Citation:** Carroll, S. R., et al. "The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance." *Data Science Journal* 19, no. 1 (2020): 43. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA). One-pager: <https://www.gida-global.org/s/CAREPrinciples_OnePagersFINAL_Oct_17_2019.pdf>

*(external link, paywalled PDF — summarized from GIDA documentation, academic commentary, and training-data knowledge)*

The CARE Principles were developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance in 2019 as a people- and purpose-oriented complement to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR focuses on technical data properties, CARE centers Indigenous peoples' rights, relationships, and self-determination in data governance. The framework emerged in response to decades of research in which Indigenous communities were data sources but not data governors — a structural injustice with direct health and cultural consequences.

**Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems must be designed so Indigenous peoples can actually benefit. This requires inclusive participation in knowledge production (not just subject status), data systems that honor Indigenous decision-making processes, and research designs that return equitable outcomes to communities rather than concentrating benefits in academic institutions.

**Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples hold authority to govern their own data. This means recognition of Indigenous data rights and interests, access to data relevant to community governance (e.g., health and census data), and the right to design and administer data governance systems on their own terms.

**Responsibility (R):** Non-Indigenous researchers and institutions working with Indigenous data bear affirmative duties: nurturing positive reciprocal relationships with communities, building Indigenous data governance capacity rather than fostering dependence, and respecting Indigenous languages and worldviews in how data categories and descriptions are constructed.

**Ethics (E):** Data use must minimize harm (avoid stigmatizing communities or exposing them to reputational risk), advance justice by acknowledging colonial history and structural power imbalances, and attend to future uses — anticipating how data collected today might be used in ways communities cannot yet foresee.

**Connection to course justice themes:** The CARE Principles translate the course's theoretical frameworks — data justice (Dencik, Week 7), participatory justice (Benjamin, Week 2), structural critique of biomedical research (Weeks 4 and 9) — into operational standards. They provide the affirmative vision of what just Indigenous data governance looks like in practice, responding directly to the extractive patterns illustrated by the Havasupai case. The emphasis on collective benefit and equitable outcomes also connects to Rawlsian distributional concerns and the environmental justice thread from Week 8.
