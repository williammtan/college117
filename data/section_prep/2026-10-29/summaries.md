### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty" *(Nature Genetics, 2025)*

*(external link, blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This article announces the establishment of the first federally funded, Indigenous-led Tribal Data Repository, the RADx Tribal Data Repository: Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (RADx TDR D4I). The project, supported by approximately $9 million in NIH funding, is organized around six collaborative research awards, with the lead institution being a 501(c)(3) nonprofit biodata repository—NativeBio—housed on the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation. Krystal Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), a co-author, heads a sub-award developing ethical data-sharing and governance frameworks.

The central argument is that Indigenous health data has historically been extracted and used without adequate community oversight, leading to harms documented in cases like the Havasupai dispute. The repository corrects this by operating under rigorous Tribal Nation governance structures, centering community data-sharing interests, and developing an Indigenous Data Sovereignty framework for culturally responsive access. Tsosie frames data rights explicitly as self-determination: the right of Indigenous peoples to exercise authority, agency, autonomy, and self-direction over decisions about their own data. The paper connects directly to the course's justice themes by demonstrating that biotechnology infrastructure—who controls data, who governs its reuse—is not a technical question but a political and ethical one with direct implications for Indigenous sovereignty and health equity.

### 2. Mello, Michelle M. & Wolf, Leslie E. — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples" *(New England Journal of Medicine, 2010)*

*(external link, blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This Perspective piece analyzes the landmark 2010 settlement between the Havasupai Indian Tribe and Arizona State University (ASU), which paid $700,000 to 41 tribal members and agreed to return all blood samples. The samples had been collected in the 1990s for diabetes research, with oral consent from tribal members. Participants were then asked to sign English-language informed consent forms—English being a second language for many Havasupai—that, critics argue, granted broader research permissions than participants understood. Researchers subsequently used the samples for genetic studies on schizophrenia, migration patterns, and population history, topics the tribe found culturally harmful and explicitly contrary to their worldview.

Mello and Wolf use this case to highlight a fundamental gap in research ethics: the consent framework developed for clinical research was designed around physical risks, not the cultural, dignitary, and epistemic harms that can arise from genetic analysis of stored biospecimens. They argue for clearer consent procedures that communicate the full spectrum of potential future uses, and for institutional mechanisms to ensure that community values and priorities—not just individual signatures—inform how genetic data may be repurposed. In the course's justice framework, the Havasupai case illustrates how procedural injustice (inadequate consent) combined with substantive injustice (research that violated cultural integrity) can compound harm for already-marginalized communities. The case is a foundational reference for the argument that research ethics must incorporate Indigenous sovereignty, not just individual autonomy.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance *(Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)*

*(external link, blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles—Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics—were developed in 2018 at a workshop in Gaborone, Botswana, during International Data Week, and published in 2019 by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) as a complement to the FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR focuses on data infrastructure and openness, critics pointed out that FAIR inadvertently extended the logic of open-science extractivism to Indigenous data, which had historically been collected without community benefit. The CARE principles reframe the question: from "how can data be made more accessible to researchers?" to "how can data systems serve Indigenous peoples' own self-determined goals?"

Each principle addresses a different dimension of justice: **Collective Benefit** requires that data systems be designed so Indigenous peoples can derive equitable outcomes, not only external researchers; **Authority to Control** recognizes Indigenous rights to govern data from collection through reuse; **Responsibility** places obligations on anyone handling Indigenous data to support self-determination; and **Ethics** centers Indigenous rights and wellbeing at every stage of the data life cycle. The principles have been operationalized in the STARDIT standard and in protocols adopted by repositories like NativeBio. In the context of the course, CARE represents a structural intervention in the knowledge production pipeline—arguing that justice in biotechnology requires not just better consent forms, but a redesign of who owns, interprets, and benefits from biological and health data.
