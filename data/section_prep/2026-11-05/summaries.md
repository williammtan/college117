### 1. Anderson et al. (2025) — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty"

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources including PubMed abstract, ASU news coverage, and ScienceNews)*

This Nature Genetics paper (vol. 57, pp. 2614–2619, Nov. 2025) introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository** — described as the first federally funded Tribal data repository in the United States. Led by bioethicist and geneticist Krystal S. Tsosie (Diné/Navajo Nation), the repository is designed to give Tribal Nations sovereign governance over federally funded biomedical data originating from Indigenous communities.

The core argument is that existing data infrastructure systematically disadvantages Indigenous communities: data collected from them flows outward to researchers and institutions, while the communities themselves have little control over its use or access to its benefits. The D4I model addresses this by architecturally embedding Tribal authority — requiring that data use agreements reflect community interests and that research benefits flow back to participating nations.

The paper connects to the course's justice themes by illustrating what structural data equity can look like in practice: not simply expanding access to existing data systems, but redesigning governance from the ground up to reflect Indigenous self-determination. It responds directly to the historical pattern of extractive research (exemplified by the Havasupai case, the companion reading) with a constructive alternative that centers community sovereignty. The NIH awarded approximately $9 million to fund this work, signaling institutional recognition that participatory and sovereignty-respecting research infrastructure requires dedicated investment.

### 2. Mello & Wolf (2010) — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples"

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources including SSRN abstract, AMA Journal of Ethics, and PMC secondary literature)*

This NEJM Perspectives piece (vol. 363, pp. 204–207) analyzes a landmark case in research ethics involving the Havasupai Tribe of Arizona. Beginning in the early 1990s, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from Havasupai members ostensibly for genetic diabetes research. By 2003, tribal members discovered the samples had been used without their knowledge or approval for studies on schizophrenia, population migration patterns, and inbreeding — topics that conflicted with Havasupai cultural and spiritual values. In April 2010, ASU settled for $700,000 paid to 41 tribe members, and researchers were required to retrieve and return all samples to the tribe.

Mello and Wolf's central argument concerns the inadequacy of existing informed consent frameworks for stored biological samples. The standard approach — broad consent at collection — fails to anticipate repurposing and fails to reflect the values of community-based populations who understand consent not merely as individual autonomy but as collective dignity and cultural protection. The case is not simply a procedural failure; it represents a deeper failure to understand the population being studied.

The piece is pivotal for the course's justice themes because it illustrates how research infrastructure can perpetuate harm even under formal compliance: the original consent forms were technically signed, but the relational and cultural dimensions of consent were ignored. It makes the case for ongoing community engagement, culturally sensitive IRB review, and legal protections that recognize group rights — prefiguring both the CARE Principles and the tribal data sovereignty frameworks discussed in the other readings.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (GIDA, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled/redirected — summarized from publisher page content via RDA/CASRAI secondary sources and the 2020 Data Science Journal publication by Carroll et al.)*

The CARE Principles were released in September 2019 by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)** as a complement to the existing FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles. Where FAIR is data-centric, CARE is people- and purpose-centric, addressing the specific justice needs of Indigenous communities in relation to their data.

The four principles are:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems should be designed so Indigenous peoples can derive value from their own data. This means that data use agreements and research designs must translate into tangible benefits for the community (e.g., improved health outcomes, economic returns, cultural preservation), not merely benefits for researchers or institutions.

- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their authority to control that data empowered. This goes beyond individual consent to recognize Indigenous nations and communities as legitimate data governors with the right to determine access, use, and sharing terms.

- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data bear responsibility for explaining how those data support Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit. Researchers and institutions must be accountable, not just compliant.

- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at all stages of the data lifecycle. This embeds an ethical obligation to anticipate and prevent harm, centering Indigenous worldviews in the design of data systems.

The CARE Principles are directly relevant to the course's arc from ethical frameworks (Week 1) through participatory justice (Week 2) to this week's focus on community-led governance. They operationalize the abstract concept of justice into actionable data governance norms, and serve as a normative framework against which students can evaluate emerging biotechnology infrastructure — including the tribal data repository described in the Anderson et al. paper.
