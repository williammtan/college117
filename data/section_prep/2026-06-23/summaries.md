## Previous Lecture Summary — Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples)

*Wednesday, May 27th · Week 9: Community-Led Justice · Guest Lecturer: Krystal Tsosie, PhD, MPH, MA (Diné/Navajo Nation)*

This lecture examined what it means for Indigenous nations to claim ownership, stewardship, and sovereignty over data generated from their communities — particularly genomic and health data. Delivered by Krystal Tsosie, a Diné/Navajo Nation scholar who is herself a co-architect of the first federally funded Tribal data repository, the session grounded abstract questions of data justice in the lived experience of Native peoples.

The lecture traced how historical exploitation — from extractive anthropological sampling to the Havasupai case — has shaped Indigenous communities' deep mistrust of outside researchers. It then pivoted to ask: what does redress look like? The answer involves structural transformation rather than procedural reform alone. Community data sovereignty means that tribes do not merely consent to research; they govern it — setting terms of access, retaining the right to withdraw data, and ensuring that any value produced flows back to the community. The Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository was presented as a concrete instantiation of these principles: a NIH-funded, Indigenous-led infrastructure that replaces extractive data pipelines with federated governance anchored in the CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics).

The lecture also raised harder questions: How can community governance structures be designed to last across political transitions? When communities are internally divided, whose voice counts? And how does Indigenous data sovereignty interact with — and challenge — the dominant open-science ethos of federally funded research? These tensions connected directly to the course's broader justice themes: Who benefits? Who controls the tools that generate benefit? And what institutional forms are required to make justice durable rather than rhetorical?

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* (2025)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This paper introduces the first federally funded Tribal data repository — the D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository — as an institutional innovation that advances both Indigenous health and data sovereignty simultaneously. The core claim is that data governance and health equity are not separable problems: communities cannot achieve health justice if control over the data that shapes research agendas, clinical tools, and policy decisions remains outside their hands.

**Evidence:** The repository was established through a $9M NIH RADx initiative involving Stanford University (prime awardee), Arizona State University, the Native BioData Consortium, Ohio State University, UC Santa Cruz, University of Washington, and the University of Wisconsin-Madison. The system is designed around federated and decentralized access models — meaning no single external key unlocks tribal data — and incorporates Tribal Nation governance structures that determine who may access data, for what purposes, and under what conditions. Tsosie's $1.2M sub-award specifically led the design of ethical approaches to data sharing, analysis, and implementation.

**Connection to justice themes:** The paper positions Indigenous Data Sovereignty (IDS) not as a courtesy but as a precondition for legitimate science. By embedding the CARE Principles into the repository's technical architecture — rather than appending them as an IRB checkbox — the D4I model embodies Collective Benefit (data ecosystems must benefit the rights-holders), Authority to Control (Indigenous peoples must govern their own data), Responsibility (researchers have affirmative duties to communities), and Ethics (data use must align with Indigenous values and rights). This challenges the course's central question — *who benefits?* — by arguing that structural control, not just downstream benefit-sharing, is required for justice. The paper also illustrates how community-led biotechnology infrastructure can itself be a justice intervention.

### 2. Mello, M.M. & Wolf, L.E. — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *NEJM* 363(3), 2010

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This NEJM Perspective uses the 2010 settlement of the Havasupai Tribe v. Arizona Board of Regents lawsuit to argue that the existing informed consent framework is structurally inadequate for governing stored biologic samples used in secondary genomic research. The case reveals how individually focused consent models fail Indigenous communities whose interests are collective, relational, and cultural — not merely personal.

**Evidence:** Between 1990 and 1994, ASU researchers collected DNA samples from approximately 400 Havasupai members under consent forms authorizing research on "behavioral/medical disorders" — framed primarily around Type 2 diabetes. Without additional consent, the samples were then used in studies of schizophrenia, ethnic migration patterns, and population inbreeding, all topics carrying deep cultural and spiritual significance for the Havasupai. In 2004, tribe members discovered these secondary uses. The April 2010 settlement awarded $700,000 in compensation, funds for a tribal clinic and school, and — most symbolically significant to many members — the physical return of the DNA samples. Crucially, the settlement set no legal precedent and left the fundamental consent question unresolved.

**Connection to justice themes:** Mello and Wolf identify the unresolved tension at the case's core: current IRB-governed consent models assume individual, informed, forward-looking agreement — but genomic data extracted from community members encodes information about the entire community across time. The Havasupai case illustrates that when researchers treat consent as a one-time transaction rather than an ongoing relationship, communities with histories of exploitation bear the greatest risk. The article calls for broader consent frameworks, community-level review, and tribal oversight mechanisms — anticipating the governance structures that the D4I repository would later institutionalize. For the course, the case is a paradigmatic example of how procedural compliance can mask substantive injustice: the researchers believed they had valid consent; the community experienced a profound violation. Justice requires attending to the gap between.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (GIDA, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** The CARE Principles — Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics — articulate a people-centered framework for Indigenous data governance that complements and corrects the dominant FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR focuses on data characteristics for open science, CARE centers the rights and interests of the people to whom data relate. The core claim is that data justice requires putting *purpose* and *people* before *access*.

**Evidence:** Developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) and published in October 2019, the CARE Principles specify four governing commitments. *Collective Benefit* requires that data ecosystems enable Indigenous peoples to derive benefit from data — including for governance, culture, and self-determined development, not only health research. *Authority to Control* recognizes Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data and demands that their authority to govern it be operationally empowered, not just symbolically acknowledged. *Responsibility* holds that those working with Indigenous data have an affirmative obligation to share how data are used to support Indigenous self-determination and community wellbeing. *Ethics* requires that Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary concern at all stages of the data lifecycle, consistent with Indigenous frameworks rather than only Western bioethical norms. The principles are operationalized through twelve sub-principles covering areas including disaggregated data rights, culturally appropriate algorithms, and reflexive accountability mechanisms.

**Connection to justice themes:** The CARE Principles are the normative architecture underlying the D4I repository and provide the conceptual vocabulary for the course's Week 9 discussion. They embody a crucial distinction the course returns to throughout: the difference between *inclusion in existing systems* (being given access, being studied, being represented in datasets) and *self-determination* (controlling the systems themselves). The CARE framework argues that inclusion without authority reproduces colonial extraction in new technical forms. For a course on who benefits and who is left behind in biotechnology, CARE offers both a diagnostic lens — identifying how conventional open-data norms can harm Indigenous communities — and a constructive alternative built on sovereignty rather than charity.
