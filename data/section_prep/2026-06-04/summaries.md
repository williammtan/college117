## Previous Lecture Summary

### Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples) — May 27, 2026

Guest lecturer Krystal Tsosie, a Diné/Navajo Nation geneticist and bioethicist, anchored this session in the principle that Indigenous communities have historically been treated as research subjects rather than partners—with data generated from them flowing outward, under the control of institutions far removed from the communities themselves. The session examined how genetic and health data collected from Indigenous peoples has been routinely repurposed without community consent, covering everything from diabetes research to population-genetics studies touching on highly sensitive topics like migration origins and mental health.

Tsosie connected historical exploitation—including the Havasupai case—to present-day structural inequities in data governance. She introduced the concept of Indigenous data sovereignty: the right of Indigenous peoples to govern the collection, ownership, and application of data about their communities. Central to the lecture was the argument that sovereignty over data is an extension of political and cultural sovereignty more broadly. The session also foregrounded the tension between open-data norms in science (epitomized by the FAIR principles) and the need for community-controlled, protective governance structures. Students were asked to consider what it means for a community to truly exercise stewardship over its own data, what technological challenges arise when data storage shifts toward communities, and how "community engagement" can be co-opted to perpetuate rather than dismantle injustice.

## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57, 2614–2619 (2025).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository, described as the first federally funded data repository explicitly designed to advance both Indigenous health and data sovereignty. Funded by a $9 million NIH grant, the repository was created in direct response to a well-documented problem: biomedical data collected from Indigenous communities under broad or blanket consent forms are routinely stored in institutional repositories, reused by researchers with no ongoing relationship to the originating communities, and put to purposes tribal members never envisioned or approved.

The D4I repository inverts this model. Rather than data flowing from communities into institutional systems, the repository is designed so that participating Tribal Nations retain governance authority over their own data. Researchers who wish to access data must engage with the relevant community and obtain approval under Tribal governance frameworks. Community members can opt in or out of specific studies through direct communication mechanisms built into the system. The repository centers Indigenous data and digital sovereignty "from the ground up, technically and ethically."

A critical justice argument runs throughout: data extracted from historically marginalized communities should serve those communities first. The authors explicitly tie data sovereignty to health equity—arguing that until Indigenous communities control the research agenda, precision medicine and genomics will continue to reflect the priorities of well-resourced institutions rather than the health needs of Indigenous peoples. Crucially, the paper links data sovereignty to political sovereignty, framing this not merely as a data-governance technical fix but as an assertion of tribal self-determination rights.

The paper connects directly to the course's sustained engagement with who controls the knowledge generated by biotechnologies and whose health priorities are served. It exemplifies community-led justice in practice.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case—Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3), 204–207 (2010).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

Mello and Wolf use the Havasupai case as a lens for examining one of the most contested questions in biomedical ethics: what constitutes adequate informed consent when biological specimens are collected for one purpose but stored and potentially repurposed for others?

The facts of the case are stark. Beginning in 1990, the Havasupai Tribe agreed to provide blood samples for diabetes research—a disease causing serious harm in their community. Unbeknownst to most participants, the consent form they signed was written in English (the second language of many members) and included language permitting use of samples for research into "behavioral/medical disorders." Over the following decade, researchers used the samples to study schizophrenia, migration patterns, and inbreeding—topics deeply taboo within Havasupai culture. When tribe members discovered the broader uses in 2003, they filed suit against Arizona State University. A 2010 settlement awarded the tribe $700,000 and returned their blood samples.

Mello and Wolf identify two layers of ethical failure: procedural (the consent form was ambiguous, not explained in accessible language, and did not account for cultural context) and substantive (the research violated the community's values and caused dignitary harm regardless of whether any physical harm resulted). The case illuminates the limitations of individual informed consent as a protection for communities with collective cultural interests in data and biological material.

Their recommendations include prospective community consultation before designing consent processes, culturally tailored consent procedures, clear limitations on future use rather than blanket permissions, and mechanisms for communities to withdraw consent. This reading connects directly to the course's themes of justice in research infrastructure and whose interests biomedical research serves.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA), 2019.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

The CARE Principles were developed in 2019 by the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group (part of the Research Data Alliance) as a direct response to what GIDA identified as a critical deficit in the widely adopted FAIR Principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). While FAIR focuses on the technical properties of data that support broad sharing and reuse, it is largely silent on the rights and interests of the people and communities from whom data originate. CARE corrects this asymmetry.

**Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems should be designed so that Indigenous peoples derive benefit from data about them—economically, socially, and culturally. Data collection must serve community priorities, not only external research agendas.

**Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples have inherent rights and interests in data about themselves and their territories. These rights must be recognized and their authority to govern, access, and use those data must be actively empowered—not merely consulted.

**Responsibility (R):** Anyone working with Indigenous data bears a responsibility to nurture reciprocal relationships with originating communities, to support Indigenous data governance capacity, and to ensure data use strengthens rather than undermines self-determination.

**Ethics (E):** Indigenous Peoples' rights, wellbeing, and worldviews must be centered at all stages of the data lifecycle. This includes minimizing harm, maximizing benefit, and building governance systems grounded in the ethical traditions of the relevant Indigenous communities.

The CARE Principles are explicitly people- and purpose-oriented rather than merely technical. They connect to the course's justice frameworks by insisting that data governance is not a neutral administrative matter but a site of power, rights, and self-determination. The principles offer a practical framework for operationalizing the Indigenous data sovereignty argued for by Tsosie and the D4I repository.
