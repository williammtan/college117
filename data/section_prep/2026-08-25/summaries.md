## Reading Summaries

### 1. Anderson MZ et al. (2025). "A Tribal data repository to advance Indigenous health and sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This paper, led by Krystal Tsosie and co-authored by a broad coalition of Indigenous health researchers and tribal representatives, proposes the **D4I (Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations) Tribal Data Repository** — the first federally funded, Indigenous-governed biomedical data repository for U.S. tribal nations. The central argument is that existing data infrastructures systematically exclude Indigenous communities from controlling data that originates with them: their genomic, health, and community data flow into research ecosystems controlled by universities and federal agencies, generating academic outputs and medical advances that rarely return to source communities. The proposed repository is designed to invert this by placing data governance under Tribal Nation sovereignty, incorporating federated and decentralized access models, and adhering rigorously to tribal laws and policies.

**Evidence and design:** The repository draws on Tsosie's prior work on Indigenous data sovereignty and responds to documented harms — including the Havasupai case — as well as ongoing structural inequities in NIH-funded research. Key design features include community-controlled access, machine-learning-ready infrastructure built for Indigenous health questions, and protocols that require researchers to engage with communities under tribal governance rather than standard IRB review alone. A core empirical motivation is that Indigenous populations remain dramatically underrepresented in genomic and biomedical databases, meaning precision medicine advances benefit them least while research exploits their communities most.

**Connection to justice themes:** The paper directly operationalizes the course's community-led justice framework. It moves beyond critique of extractive research to a concrete sovereignty-aligned infrastructure, illustrating how data ownership is not merely an ethical aspiration but a technical and institutional design challenge. It connects to Week 7's surveillance and data-justice discussions and to the CARE Principles, demonstrating how those abstract principles translate into repository architecture. The authors frame Indigenous data sovereignty as inseparable from Indigenous health equity: without control over data, communities cannot shape the research questions, benefit from findings, or correct harmful narratives.

### 2. Mello, M. M., & Wolf, L. E. (2010). "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207.

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This NEJM perspective is a landmark bioethics analysis of one of the most consequential research-ethics cases involving an Indigenous community. Mello and Wolf argue that the Havasupai case exposed a fundamental structural problem in biomedical research: the practice of collecting biological samples for one stated purpose and then reusing them — with IRB approval but without renewed tribal consent — for entirely different research agendas. The case exemplifies how "scope creep" in research design violates not merely procedural consent requirements but the deeper trust relationship between researchers and vulnerable communities.

**Evidence/case facts:** Between 1990 and 1994, Arizona State University researchers collected DNA samples from approximately 400 Havasupai tribal members, obtaining consent specifically for diabetes research. When the diabetes studies yielded negative results, researchers used the stored samples — under new IRB approvals — for studies on schizophrenia, alcoholism, inbreeding, and population migration patterns. Tribal members were never informed of these secondary uses. In 2010, the Arizona Board of Regents settled with 41 tribal members for $700,000, return of DNA samples, and infrastructure support. The case turned on the inadequacy of the original consent form: broad language about "behavioral and medical disorders" was interpreted by researchers as blanket permission, while tribal members — many for whom English is a second language — understood it as authorization only for diabetes research. Mello and Wolf identify the core tension: what constitutes adequate informed consent for biospecimens that will be stored and potentially repurposed?

**Connection to justice themes:** This case is a canonical illustration of research injustice at the intersection of race, indigeneity, and institutional power. It demonstrates how procedural compliance (IRB approval) can mask substantive violations of community rights and cultural dignity — directly challenging a purely procedural understanding of research ethics. For this course, it bridges the Week 4 discussion of research infrastructure and justice with Week 9's focus on data sovereignty: the Havasupai case shows precisely why Indigenous communities are right to demand governance structures like the CARE Principles and repositories like D4I. The "scope problem" it identifies remains unresolved in genomic medicine.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (2019). Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA).

*(external link, paywalled/blocked — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** The CARE Principles — **Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics** — constitute a normative framework designed to complement the technical FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) when data involves Indigenous peoples, communities, lands, or knowledge. The GIDA's central argument is that FAIR alone is insufficient: making data more findable and accessible can accelerate extractive research unless governance principles also ensure that data use serves Indigenous interests, respects Indigenous authority, and is grounded in ethical commitments to Indigenous dignity. CARE shifts the frame from data-as-resource to data-as-relationship.

**The four principles:**
- **Collective Benefit (C):** Data ecosystems must enable Indigenous peoples to derive benefit from their data — through inclusive development, equitable value creation, and improved governance outcomes for communities.
- **Authority to Control (A):** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their data must be recognized, and their authority to control those data — including restricting access, setting terms of use, and withdrawing consent — must be empowered at individual, community, and nation levels.
- **Responsibility (R):** Those who work with Indigenous data must account for how those data are used, expanding capacity and capability in Indigenous communities, and actively nurturing respectful relationships.
- **Ethics (E):** The creation, interpretation, and use of data must uphold the dignity, rights, and worldviews of Indigenous nations — including protections that may go beyond standard research-ethics review.

**Connection to justice themes:** The CARE Principles synthesize the course's justice frameworks into actionable governance norms. They apply Rawlsian concerns for fair benefit and Daniels's questions of social justice to the specific domain of data — asking whose interests data systems serve and who has the authority to shape them. Crucially, CARE extends beyond individual rights (the consent-based model Mello and Wolf critique) to community and collective rights, reflecting Indigenous epistemologies in which data about a person is also data about their kin, community, and land. For the course, CARE represents a worked example of how justice frameworks can be translated into institutional infrastructure — a bridge between ethical theory and the governance design advocated by Anderson et al.
