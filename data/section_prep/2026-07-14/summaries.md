### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. — "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: ASU News coverage, R Discovery abstract, prior literature on D4I and Native BioData Consortium)*

This paper introduces the **Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository**, the first federally funded genomic data repository built explicitly under Tribal Nation governance. The repository was created to address a longstanding injustice in biomedical research: Indigenous communities have historically donated biological samples and health data that were then controlled, analyzed, and monetized by outside institutions, yielding few benefits and sometimes serious harms to the communities themselves.

The D4I repository inverts this model by keeping data under Tribal law and policy. Researchers who want access must engage with Indigenous governance structures and data-sharing protocols established by Tribal Nations — not the other way around. Co-author Krystal Tsosie (who delivered the May 27 guest lecture) describes it as "a safe harbor" that maintains Indigenous sovereignty while enabling responsible scientific collaboration. The repository is designed to function according to the CARE Principles (the third assigned reading), centering Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics rather than defaulting to the FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) standards designed for open-science contexts.

The paper's core justice argument is that sovereignty over health data is an extension of Indigenous political sovereignty more broadly. Without control over how data about their communities is generated, stored, and used, Indigenous peoples remain objects of research rather than agents of their own health futures. The D4I model offers a concrete institutional infrastructure — not just principles — for making data sovereignty operational. This connects to the course's broader inquiry into who benefits from biotechnology and why structural changes to research governance are necessary for justice.

### 2. Mello MM & Wolf LE — "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207 (2010)

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources: PMC article PMC5310710, Stanford Law Blog, NCAI Genetics case study, Seeds of Native Health, Wikipedia)*

This NEJM perspective piece analyzes one of the most consequential cases in the history of research ethics involving Indigenous communities. Between 1990 and 1994, Arizona State University researchers collected blood samples from approximately 400 members of the **Havasupai Tribe** — a small Native American community living at the bottom of the Grand Canyon — for genetic research into Type 2 diabetes, which was devastating the community. Tribe members consented to diabetes research. Their samples were then used, without further consent, in studies on schizophrenia, evolutionary migration patterns, and population inbreeding — topics that were not only outside the original consent scope but deeply culturally taboo and potentially harmful to Havasupai identity and sovereignty.

The tribe discovered the unauthorized use in 2003 and filed suit in 2004. In April 2010 — the same month this article was published — ASU settled for **$700,000** paid to 41 tribe members and agreed to return the physical blood samples. No binding legal precedent was set, however.

Mello and Wolf use the case to illuminate the structural failures in standard informed consent practices when applied to community-based, culturally distinct populations. Their key arguments: (1) consent forms designed for individual clinical patients are inadequate for community settings where collective interests and cultural beliefs shape how research is understood; (2) stored samples must not be treated as freely available for future unrelated research — each new use requires renewed community engagement; (3) the failure to account for Havasupai members' limited English proficiency and the researchers' failure to explain the breadth of permitted uses represent an abuse of the power differential inherent in research relationships. The case directly prefigures the CARE Principles and the D4I repository as corrective frameworks: the harm here arose precisely from the absence of Indigenous authority to control data, genuine consent, and accountability for community wellbeing — all elements CARE and D4I now formalize.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Global Indigenous Data Alliance, 2019)

*(external link, paywalled PDF — summarized from citation + secondary sources: GIDA documentation, Data Science Journal open-access article DOI 10.5334/dsj-2020-043, Ada Lovelace Institute, ARDC, CASRAI, Wikipedia)*

The CARE Principles are a governance framework for research data involving Indigenous peoples, published in 2019 by the **Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)** and developed through an international, Indigenous-led collaborative process (including Stephanie Russo Carroll, Donna Rodriguez-Lonebear, and other Indigenous researchers and governance experts). They are designed to complement — and where necessary correct — the widely adopted **FAIR Principles** (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which optimize data for open sharing but do not address Indigenous rights, community interests, or the political dimensions of data sovereignty.

The four CARE principles are:

- **C — Collective Benefit:** Data ecosystems must be designed so that Indigenous peoples can derive benefit from data about themselves, their territories, and their knowledge. This challenges the extractive model in which research benefits flow primarily to universities and industry. Sub-principles specify benefit in inclusive development, improved governance, and equitable outcomes.

- **A — Authority to Control:** Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in their own data must be recognized, and their authority to govern that data — including the right to refuse access — must be actively empowered. This directly operationalizes Indigenous data sovereignty as a dimension of political sovereignty.

- **R — Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data (researchers, institutions, governments) bear ongoing responsibilities for how data are used, including expanding Indigenous communities' capacity to govern their own data and ensuring that Indigenous languages and worldviews are reflected in data systems.

- **E — Ethics:** Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing must be the primary ethical consideration at every stage of the data lifecycle, from collection through storage to analysis and publication. This principle resists the model in which ethics review is a bureaucratic hurdle rather than a genuine accountability mechanism.

The CARE Principles connect to the course's justice themes by framing data governance not as a technical matter but as a political and ethical one. They assert that without self-determination over data — especially genomic data, which carries deep implications for identity, ancestry, and health — Indigenous communities cannot achieve the substantive justice that formal political sovereignty alone does not guarantee.
