## Previous Lecture Summary

**Session:** Wed 2026-05-27 — *Data Ownership (Indigenous Nations examples)*, Week 9: Community-Led Justice  
**Guest Lecturer: Krystal Tsosie, PhD, MPH, MA (Diné/Navajo Nation)**

> *(No lecture deck was found in the repository for this session. The following summary draws from the session title, syllabus context, and reading materials.)*

Week 9 turns the course's justice lens toward data sovereignty and Indigenous communities. The session, led by guest lecturer Krystal Tsosie—a genomics researcher from the Diné/Navajo Nation and a leading voice in Indigenous data governance—examined what it means for tribes and Indigenous nations to claim ownership and stewardship over their own health and genomic data. The central argument is that mainstream data-sharing norms, designed around individual consent and open-science ideals like FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), routinely extract data from Indigenous communities without commensurate benefit or control returning to those communities. Historical harms—most famously illustrated by the Havasupai case—demonstrate that broad consent forms and institutional review boards have been insufficient safeguards. The lecture introduced community-centered alternatives: the CARE Principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics), Tribal data repositories governed by Native nations, and federated architectures that keep data within community jurisdictions. A key justice claim is that sovereignty over data is an extension of political and cultural sovereignty: you cannot have self-determination if outside actors control the stories your DNA can tell about your population. Students are invited to consider how participatory and Rawlsian frameworks covered earlier in the course apply—and fall short—when the rights-holders are entire nations rather than individuals.

### 1. Anderson MZ, Tsosie KS, et al. "A Tribal Data Repository to Advance Indigenous Health and Sovereignty." *Nature Genetics* 57(11): 2614–2619 (2025).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This paper introduces the Data for Indigenous Implementations, Interventions, and Innovations (D4I) Tribal Data Repository—described as the first federally funded data repository of its kind—built specifically to advance Indigenous health research under Native nation governance. The central claim is that existing genomic and health research infrastructures extract data from Indigenous communities while routing governance authority to universities and federal agencies; the D4I repository inverts this arrangement by placing Tribal Nations at the center of access decisions.

**Architecture and design:** The repository uses federated and decentralized data-access models rather than a single-institution custodianship model. Rather than depositing tribal data into NIH repositories with broad consent that permits indefinite reuse, the D4I model routes access requests through tribally controlled governance structures. Krystal Tsosie—a Diné (Navajo) researcher and the paper's driving intellectual force—has argued that broad consent as currently practiced enables data to migrate far from originating communities and be reused for studies those communities never anticipated or approved.

**Justice connection:** The paper operationalizes several principles discussed in the course: participatory justice (communities determine their own research priorities), data sovereignty (the CARE framework's "Authority to control"), and restorative justice (correcting the historical extraction documented in cases like Havasupai). The D4I repository was supported by approximately $9 million in NIH funding, pointing to a tension the paper must navigate—institutional funding within a system the paper critiques.

**Course connection:** Pairs tightly with the CARE Principles and Havasupai readings. Together, the three readings move from historical harm (Havasupai), to normative principles (CARE), to a concrete infrastructure response (D4I). Students should ask whether technical solutions like federated repositories are sufficient to achieve justice, or whether legal and political changes in sovereignty recognition are also required.

### 2. Mello, M.M. & Wolf, L.E. "The Havasupai Indian Tribe Case — Lessons for Research Involving Stored Biologic Samples." *New England Journal of Medicine* 363(3): 204–207 (2010).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** This NEJM Perspective, published just after Arizona State University's $700,000 settlement with 41 members of the Havasupai Indian Tribe, uses the case to argue that existing consent frameworks for biospecimen-based research are inadequate—particularly for populations whose cultural identity is tied to origin narratives that genetic research can threaten. The core claim is that informed consent as currently designed fails to capture the full scope of potential research uses when samples are stored for future, yet-unspecified studies.

**The case:** Starting in the late 1980s, Havasupai tribal members provided blood samples for diabetes research. The samples were subsequently used—without re-consent—for population migration studies, schizophrenia research, and other investigations. The tribe argued these uses violated tribal beliefs about ancestry and stigmatized the community. The settlement in 2010, which included the return of the blood samples, financial compensation, and commitments to provide health services, was the largest such settlement in US history at the time.

**Lessons identified:** Mello and Wolf highlight the clash of underlying values: researchers tend to view biospecimen donation as a gift that enables broad scientific benefit; participants (especially in Indigenous communities) often retain an ownership conception and expect direct benefit sharing. The authors argue that IRBs and consent processes need to better account for community-level harms, not only individual ones, and that study populations' perspectives must be solicited more systematically.

**Justice connection:** The case illustrates Rawlsian and participatory justice failures simultaneously. The least-advantaged party (the Havasupai) bore the reputational and cultural risks without being included in deliberation or sharing in benefits. The case is foundational for data sovereignty arguments: it is the primary US legal precedent that shows community consent matters alongside individual consent in research involving Indigenous peoples.

### 3. CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance. *Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)* (2019).

*(external link, paywalled — summarized from citation + secondary sources)*

**Key argument:** The CARE Principles (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics) are a normative framework developed by the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) in 2019 to complement—and, when Indigenous data is involved, take precedence over—the widely adopted FAIR data principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Where FAIR focuses on technical data-sharing standards, CARE centers the rights, interests, and governance capacity of Indigenous Peoples.

**The four principles:**
- **Collective benefit:** Data ecosystems must be designed and function in ways that enable Indigenous Peoples to derive benefit from the data—not only for research purposes but for governance, self-determination, and community wellbeing.
- **Authority to control:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and interests in their data need recognition at all levels. Data governance frameworks must strengthen Indigenous Peoples' capacity to control how data about their communities is generated, stored, accessed, and used.
- **Responsibility:** Those who work with Indigenous data have a responsibility to share how it is used, to support Indigenous data capacities, and to nurture respectful relationships with the communities involved.
- **Ethics:** Indigenous Peoples' rights and wellbeing should guide all stages of the data life cycle, and all research and innovation should be minimally harmful and maximally ethical from Indigenous community perspectives.

**Relationship to FAIR:** GIDA argues that FAIR principles, while technically useful, were designed without Indigenous community governance in mind. Applying FAIR standards to Indigenous data without first addressing CARE questions can perpetuate extraction: making data more findable and accessible may mean making it easier for outside researchers to use community data without community authority. GIDA's more recent communiqué (*CARE Directs Us Home*) goes further, arguing that locally developed Indigenous data standards should take priority over global ones like CARE itself.

**Course connection:** The CARE Principles function in this course as a bridge between the abstract justice frameworks of Week 1 (Rawls), the participatory justice of Week 2, and the practical infrastructure needed for community-led justice. Students should consider where CARE aligns with and diverges from the bioethics frameworks they have studied: unlike principlist frameworks focused on individual autonomy, CARE is explicitly collective and political, treating data governance as an expression of Indigenous sovereignty.
